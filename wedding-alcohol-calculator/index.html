How the Wedding Alcohol Calculator Works

Bar planning is three multiplications in a trench coat. This tool runs them in order and rounds the way a caterer would: every bottle and case count rounds up, never down.

The formulas

Drinks = guests × per-guest rule × daytime factor, where the rule is 1/hour (standard) or 2 + 1 × (hours − 1) (generous). Then each type's drinks convert to containers: beer ÷ 24 per case, wine ÷ 5 per bottle, liquor ÷ 17 per 750ml, champagne flutes ÷ 6 per bottle. Cost sums each count × your unit prices.

How to use it

Enter guest count and hours, pick a drinking rule and a mix that matches your crowd, and toggle the toast. Prices are optional — leave the defaults for a ballpark or type your venue's retail prices for a real budget. Daytime weddings scale the total to 85%.

A worked example

100 guests, 4-hour evening reception, standard rule: 100 × 4 = 400 drinks. With the standard 50/25/25 mix that's 200 beers = 8.33 cases → 9 cases; 100 wine glasses ÷ 5 = 20 bottles; 100 liquor drinks ÷ 17 = 5.9 → 6 bottles. A champagne toast adds 100 flutes ÷ 6 = 16.7 → 17 bottles. At default prices that's 9 × $25 + 20 × $12 + 6 × $20 + 17 × $15 = $840 of alcohol. Switch to the generous rule and everything scales: 500 drinks → 11 beer cases, 25 wine bottles, 8 liquor bottles.

Two adjustments worth knowing. If only 60 of the 100 guests drink, the honest total is closer to 240 drinks — but most hosts keep the full 400 as buffer because running out at hour three is worse than leftover cases. And if the venue charges corkage or requires their bartenders, that fee rides on top of the alcohol cost itself.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much alcohol do I need for 100 guests?

Using the standard rule of one drink per guest per hour, a 4-hour reception for 100 guests needs about 400 total drinks. Split 50% beer, 25% wine, 25% liquor, that's 200 beers (9 cases of 24), 20 bottles of wine, and 6 bottles of 750ml liquor. Add roughly 17 bottles of champagne if you're pouring a toast.

What is the one drink per guest per hour rule?

It's the industry planning rule bartenders and caterers use: budget one alcoholic drink per guest for every hour of the reception, assuming roughly 60% of guests drink. For a heavier crowd, use the generous variant — two drinks the first hour, then one per hour after — which yields 5 drinks per guest over 4 hours instead of 4.

How many glasses of wine are in a bottle?

A standard 750ml bottle holds 25.4 ounces, and a wine pour at a wedding is 5 ounces, so you get about 5 glasses per bottle. A 1.5L magnum holds about 10. Champagne toast pours are 4 ounces, so one bottle fills about 6 flutes.

How many shots are in a 750ml bottle?

About 16 to 17 standard 1.5-ounce shots — 25.4 ounces divided by 1.5. Real-world count runs lower once you account for cocktail recipes using 2 ounces of spirit plus mixers; planning at 14-15 mixed drinks per bottle is safer for a full bar. A 1.75L handle holds about 39 shots.

How much alcohol for a beer and wine only wedding?

Skip the liquor math and split the same total drinks between beer and wine — a common mix is 60% beer, 40% wine. For 100 guests over 4 hours that's 400 drinks: 240 beers (10 cases) and 160 glasses of wine (32 bottles). Beer-and-wine bars typically cut total consumption 10-15% since guests drink liquor faster.

Do daytime weddings need less alcohol?

Yes — a morning or early-afternoon reception usually runs 10-20% lighter than an evening one. Multiply the calculator total by 0.85 for a brunch or lunch wedding, and consider shifting the mix toward sparkling wine and light beer. Evening dances with an open bar run the full estimate or more.
