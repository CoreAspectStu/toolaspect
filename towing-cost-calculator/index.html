How the Towing Cost Calculator Works

Tow bills are refreshingly linear: a hook-up fee that usually includes the first handful of miles, a flat per-mile rate after that, and a short list of add-ons. This calculator makes each piece visible so a phone quote can't hide anything.

The formula

Total = hook-up fee + max(0, miles − included miles) × per-mile rate + after-hours surcharge + storage days × daily rate + winch hours × hourly rate. The effective per-mile figure (total ÷ miles) is what to compare against roadside plans, because short tows are dominated by the hook fee.

How to use it

Pick your tow type — the fields prefill with typical national midpoints, and you can overwrite any of them with a quoted rate. Mileage is billable miles, door to door, not map-straight-line. If the car's in a yard already, count the days it might sit; storage at $20-$80 a day is how a $150 tow becomes a $500 problem.

A worked example

Alternator dies 12 miles from home. Light-duty tow: $95 hook-up covering the first 5 miles, $4.50 a mile after. Bill = $95 + 7 × $4.50 = $126.50. Now stretch it: the same tow 40 miles out runs $95 + 35 × $4.50 = $252.50 at that rate (the $163-$300 table band reflects the market's $2.50-$5.00 rate spread). At 100 miles, most operators switch to long-haul pricing of roughly $1.50-$3.00 a mile plus the hook, landing at $250-$600 total.

The plan math writes itself. A mid-tier plan at $120 a year with 100-mile tows covers that 40-mile tow entirely; two tows in a household with drivers of differing luck, $470 worth of bills, and the plan still cost less than one of them.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a tow cost per mile?

Light-duty tows charge a hook-up fee of $75 to $125 (usually covering the first 5 to 7 miles) plus $2.50 to $5 per mile after that. Long-distance tows over about 100 miles often price at $1.50 to $3 per mile flat. Heavy-duty towing for semis runs $10 to $35 per mile on top of a $350 to $700 hook fee.

How much does a 100-mile tow cost?

Typically $250 to $600 for a passenger car. Most companies either drop to a flat per-mile rate for long hauls (roughly $1.50-$3.00/mile) or keep the hook fee plus a lower per-mile charge. A 100-mile flatbed move is a different market — closer to car-shipping pricing if you can wait a day.

Is it cheaper to tow or use a roadside assistance plan?

If you tow even once a year, the plan wins. A 40-mile tow bills around $235 out of pocket; a mid-tier plan with 100-mile coverage (AAA Plus class) runs roughly $100 to $140 a year. Insurance-add-on roadside coverage at $15 to $50 a year is the cheapest option, though claims handling varies by carrier.

What makes a tow bill jump higher than the quote?

The big add-ons: after-hours or weekend service ($25-$50), winching a car out of a ditch or off-road ($100-$300 per hour plus hookup), storage once the car sits in the yard ($20-$80 per day), and dollies or extra labor for a car that won't roll or steer. Ask for the out-the-door total before the truck rolls.

How much does heavy duty or semi towing cost?

Heavy-duty recovery is a different business: $350 to $700 for the hook-up and $10 to $35 per mile, so a 60-mile tow of a loaded tractor-trailer can run $1,700 or more. Recovery after a rollover adds winching and rigging time at $100-$300 per hour on top.

Does car insurance cover towing?

Only if you added roadside assistance or a towing rider, typically $15 to $50 a year — standard liability and comprehensive don't include it. Some carriers treat roadside claims gently, others log every call; if yours surcharges, a standalone plan like AAA keeps claims off your insurance record entirely.
