How the Calculator Works

Alignment pricing is mostly two dials: what the shop charges in your area, and how much vehicle you're putting on the rack. The calculator starts from national midpoints ($75 two-wheel, $125 four-wheel), scales for vehicle class (trucks and lifted rigs need more labor), and scales again for regional price level. On top of that sits the plan comparison: it divides the plan price by your per-visit cost to find the break-even, then projects both paths across the alignments you'll actually do.

Two-wheel vs four-wheel: which do you need?

A two-wheel (front-end) alignment adjusts camber, caster, and toe on the front axle only. That's adequate when the rear axle is a solid beam that can't go out of adjustment — older trucks, some econoboxes. A four-wheel alignment also squares the rear axle and checks thrust angle, the geometric line the rear wheels actually push along. If thrust is off, the car "crab-walks" down the road with a straight-appearing steering wheel. Most modern cars have independent rear suspension, so four-wheel is the default service for them.

A worked example

Take an SUV in an average-cost area doing four-wheel alignments. The calculator prices a visit at $125 × 1.1 = $137.50. A $200 lifetime plan against that price breaks even at 200 ÷ 137.5 = 1.45 visits, so two alignments. This driver potholes their way to two alignments a year and keeps the SUV four years: pay-per-visit totals 8 × $137.50 = $1,100, while the plan costs $200 once — a $900 difference, which buys a lot of tires.

Flip it: a commuter car in a low-cost town doing two-wheel alignments at $63.75 each, once a year for three years, spends $191.25 pay-per-visit against a $170 plan. The plan still wins on paper ($21 over three years), but barely — one skipped year flips the verdict. That's the honest shape of the decision: plans are cheap insurance for rough roads and long ownership, and marginal for gentle drivers who replace cars every few years.

What the plan fine print says

Lifetime plans (Firestone's is the best-known at roughly $200, with similar programs at other chains) cover unlimited alignments for as long as you own that vehicle, usually on a recommended 6-month/6,000-mile rhythm. They're not transferable to a new owner, they cover the alignment itself but not worn parts — bent tie rods, bad bushings, and camber kits are extra — and moving to a lifted suspension can void coverage. If you sell the car in two years, you've donated the tail of the plan to the shop.

Why Alignments Pay for Themselves in Tires

Misalignment doesn't just pull the wheel; it drags rubber off your tires every mile. Toe that's off by an eighth of an inch can shave thousands of miles off a tire's life, and one-sided wear patterns quietly eat the warranty value since wear from alignment isn't a tire defect. Against $125 for the service and $600–$1,000 for a replacement set of tires, the alignment is the cheap half of the deal. Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a wheel alignment cost?

Typical U.S. shop ranges run $50-$100 for a two-wheel (front-end) alignment and $100-$180 for a four-wheel alignment, with SUVs and trucks at the higher end. A lifetime alignment plan costs about $150-$250 up front and covers unlimited alignments for as long as you own the vehicle.

Is a lifetime alignment plan worth it?

If you align the car at least twice, usually yes. Divide the plan price by your per-visit price: a $200 plan against $137 alignments breaks even at 1.45 visits, so the second alignment is already free. Aligning twice a year for four years costs $1,100 pay-per-visit versus $200 on the plan.

What's the difference between 2-wheel and 4-wheel alignment?

A two-wheel (front-end) alignment adjusts only the front axle's camber, caster, and toe. A four-wheel alignment also squares the rear axle and checks the thrust angle so the rear wheels track straight behind the fronts. Independent-rear-suspension cars, SUVs, and trucks generally need the four-wheel service.

How often should I get an alignment?

Most shops suggest checking alignment every 6,000 miles or once a year, and always after hitting a serious pothole or curb, replacing tires or suspension parts, or noticing pulling, wandering, or uneven tire wear. Lifetime plans are typically structured around that 6-month/6,000-mile rhythm.

What are the signs my car needs an alignment?

The steering wheel sits off-center on a straight road, the car pulls to one side, the wheel doesn't return smoothly after a turn, or tires show feathered or one-sided wear. Any of these after a pothole strike means get it checked soon - misalignment chews through a set of tires far faster than the service costs.
