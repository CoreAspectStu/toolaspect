Why customs need a rate card

Custom requests are the highest-margin work most creators do — and the most commonly underpriced, because each one arrives as a friendly one-off conversation rather than a line on a menu. Without a published structure, every custom becomes a negotiation from scratch, which (a) takes time, (b) anchors to whatever the last person paid, and (c) makes it socially awkward to charge what the work is actually worth. A rate card fixes all three: it moves the conversation from "how much?" to "which option?", filters out low-serious buyers before they consume your inbox, and makes your pricing legible enough that returning fans know what to budget.

The exclusivity multiplier is the one sellers most often forget: if the buyer wants content that never appears on your page, they are buying not just the work but the future revenue it would have earned — that costs extra by definition. Conversely, offering a discount tier where you retain resale rights lets price-sensitive buyers in while keeping the content productive.

Setting your base rate

Base rate should be your honest floor: what an hour of your shooting-and-editing time is worth plus materials, rounded to a number that feels easy to say. A common sanity check is to divide your target monthly income by the number of custom hours you can actually sustain — if the result is higher than your current base, your base is too low. Raise it in steps rather than one jump; regulars tolerate a creeping menu far better than a doubling, and every raise is information about what the market accepts.

Terms that protect you

Three clauses belong in any custom workflow: payment before production (or a deposit for large projects), one revision round included with further changes priced, and an explicit statement of delivery format and window. Chargebacks and "I changed my mind" shrink dramatically when the terms were visible at purchase. Keep records of what was agreed — screenshots of the order conversation — because platforms side with whoever has the clearer paper trail.

Customs as part of the product line

Treat the rate card as one product in a line-up: free channels funnel to the subscription, the subscription feeds PPV, and customs sit at the top for the most invested fans. That layered structure is what turns an audience into an actual business — and running it (the funnel, the fan management, the storefront) is infrastructure work that Veyzi handles for creators so the creative hours stay creative.

Handling the awkward conversations

Custom work surfaces requests that fall outside any rate card, and the professional responses are fewer than they feel. Scope creep mid-project: "happy to add that — it's a $X addition to the order." Requests outside your boundaries: a flat, kind no, no justification owed — the rate card's existence makes boundaries look like structure rather than judgment. Haggling: the menu is the menu, said warmly. Non-payers: no work begins without payment, which is not distrust but the same terms every service business runs on. Each of these gets easier with practice and dramatically easier when published terms carry the weight instead of your in-the-moment politeness.

Scaling customs without burning out

Custom demand is the flattering trap: the highest income per hour and the fastest route to resentment, because bespoke work has no ceiling on requests and no reuse. Sustainable patterns: cap open custom slots (visible scarcity also lifts prices), batch similar requests into themed content that resells after the exclusivity window, and keep a waitlist rather than a queue you quietly neglect. As demand grows, the right endgame is a structure where premium requests flow through a managed storefront rather than a chaotic inbox — the kind of fan-facing infrastructure Veyzi builds for creators operating at scale.

A worked example

Concrete numbers make the structure real. A creator with a $40 base takes a standard-complexity request with priority turnaround and exclusivity: $40 × 1.5 × 1.5 × 1.75 ≈ $157. The same request without exclusivity (creator keeps resale rights) prices around $90 — visibly cheaper for the buyer while remaining productive for the seller, which is exactly the two-sided menu a good rate card publishes. A rush exclusive at top complexity lands near $260, and that number's job is less to be paid than to make the mid-tier options look reasonable. This anchoring is not manipulation; it is the same layout every service menu in every industry uses, and buyers read it fluently.
