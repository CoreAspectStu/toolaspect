The hidden line item in every hire

Recruiting budgets usually track job-board fees and agency retainers, but the biggest quiet cost is screener time: every applicant who gets a resume review or a phone screen consumes twenty to forty minutes of a salaried person's day. At a hundred applicants a month and twenty minutes each, that is over thirty hours — nearly a full working week — spent before a single interview is scheduled. Worse, most of that time goes to applicants who were never viable: unqualified share is routinely half or more of any pipeline.

What the calculator assumes

Simple multiplication, shown in full: applicants times minutes gives hours; hours times your screener's hourly cost gives the monthly spend; the unqualified share splits that spend between wasted and useful screening. The AI comparison band is a per-screening fee you can override with a real quote. The point is not precision — it is whether the order of magnitude justifies a conversation about automating the first-pass screen.

Where AI screening fits

The highest-value use is not replacing the final interview; it is automating the top-of-funnel grind. An AI voice agent can call every applicant, ask the same structured questions, and hand the recruiter a ranked shortlist with transcripts — so human time goes only to plausible candidates. Consistency is the underrated benefit: every applicant gets the same questions in the same tone, which is both fairer and more defensible than reviews done at 5pm on a Friday.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should dwelling coverage match my home's market value?

No. Coverage A should match rebuild cost, which excludes land and reflects construction expenses. In pricey metros, rebuild cost often runs well below market value — a $900,000 California bungalow might rebuild for $450,000. In cheap-housing, high-labor-cost areas the reverse can happen. Insurers set the limit from construction data, never from the sale price.

What is the 80% rule in homeowners insurance?

Most policies require you to insure the home to at least 80% of its replacement cost to receive full replacement-value payment on a partial loss. Fall below that threshold and the insurer prorates your payout — on a $100,000 claim with 60% coverage, you might collect only three-quarters of it. The practical lesson: don't shave Coverage A to save premium.

How much does extended replacement cost add?

The endorsement typically costs a few percent of your premium and adds 25% or 50% above the dwelling limit. On a $300,000 policy with 25% extended replacement, the insurer pays up to $375,000. It exists because post-disaster construction demand — a wildfire or hurricane hitting hundreds of homes at once — reliably inflates contractor and material prices.

Does dwelling coverage include my basement?

Finished basement space is covered, but most insurers value it differently from above-grade square footage, and estimating tools that use above-grade footage can overshoot or undershoot it. Walk-out basements, finished rec rooms, and specialized buildouts deserve a conversation with your agent rather than a square-foot rule of thumb.

Where screening time really goes

Break down a screener's week and three sinks dominate: reading resumes that never had a chance, repeating the same five qualification questions on every phone screen, and chasing scheduling for interviews that sometimes no-show. Each sink is automatable independently — keyword and structured filtering for the first, voice agents for the second, self-serve scheduling for the third — and each removes hours that the calculator above prices in dollars. Teams that automate all three routinely reclaim most of a recruiter's screening week.

A caution on the numbers: screening cost is real, but so is screening judgment. The goal of automation is to move human attention up the funnel, not to remove it — the interview where a candidate reveals they are perfect for a different role, or the read-between-the-lines sense that a resume is inflated, still pays for itself. Use the calculator to justify removing drudgery, then reinvest the recovered hours in the conversations only humans can have.

Consistency as a quality feature

Human screening varies by hour and by mood: the fourteenth resume of the day does not get the same read as the first. Structured AI screening applies identical questions, in identical order, with identical scoring — which improves not just throughput but fairness and defensibility. When hiring decisions are challenged, a consistent process with transcripts is far easier to stand behind than memories of phone calls. This is why consistency, not raw speed, is the benefit screening teams cite most after adopting structured automation.

Candidate experience is still on you

Automation can hurt candidates when it becomes a black hole — applications vanish into filters with no reply. Whatever screens the pipeline, the experience bar is the same: acknowledge every application quickly, tell candidates the process and timeline, and close out rejected applicants with a real message. Recruiters who pair automated first-pass screening with respectful communication convert better offer-acceptance rates, because the applicant's experience of the process is itself an advertisement for the role.

It also pays to revisit the inputs quarterly. Screening minutes creep upward as teams add "just one more question" to every screen, and unqualified share shifts with the job market and the wording of the posting. Re-running the calculator with fresh numbers each quarter keeps the automation case honest — and occasionally reveals that a rewritten job ad, which costs nothing, cuts the unqualified share more cheaply than any software could.

A workable rollout plan

Start with one high-volume role, define the screening questions and knockout criteria in writing, and let the AI run alongside the human process for a fortnight — same applicants, both tracks. Compare shortlists, read transcripts, and tune. Teams that do this quickly learn which questions predict success and which were noise.
