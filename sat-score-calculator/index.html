How the SAT Score Calculator Works

The SAT reports two section scores, Reading & Writing and Math, each from 200 to 800, summing to a 400–1600 total. Getting there takes three steps: count your correct answers per module (that's your raw score), convert raw to scaled through the form's equating table, then place the total on the national percentile distribution.

Raw score: no guessing penalty

Every correct answer adds 1 raw point. Wrong answers and blanks both add 0, and a wrong answer never subtracts — the guessing penalty died in 2016. Strategy follows the rule: never leave bubbles empty at the end of a section.

Scaled score: equated, not curved

Raw counts aren't comparable across forms — one month's math section might be a touch harder than another's. Equating fixes that: College Board pre-calibrates each form's raw-to-scaled table so a raw 38 earns essentially the same scaled score whenever you sit for the test. This calculator uses the official table published for the practice-test form; your real form's table will differ by a question here or there, which is part of why you get a range.

A worked example

Say you count 25 and 28 correct in the two Reading & Writing modules — raw 53 out of 66 — and 18 plus 20 in Math, raw 38 out of 54. The conversion gives RW 630–650 and Math 570–600, so the total lands between 1200 and 1250. On the user-group percentile table that's the 75th to 80th percentile: you scored even with or better than three-quarters of test takers.

Push to the 1400s: raw 61/66 in RW scales to 710–730, and 46/54 in Math to 690–720, totaling 1400–1450 — the 92nd–95th percentile. On this form that's about 5 questions wrong in RW and 8 in Math. The margin is thin up top: one raw point in Math around 50 is worth about 10 scaled points.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is the SAT scored?

Each correct answer is 1 raw point, with no penalty for wrong answers. Your raw score in each section (Reading & Writing and Math) converts to a scaled score of 200–800 through equating, which adjusts for small difficulty differences between test forms. The two section scores add up to the 400–1600 total.

How many questions can I miss and still get 1400?

On the College Board practice form this calculator uses, a raw 61/66 in Reading & Writing scales to 710–730 and a raw 46/54 in Math scales to 690–720, for a 1400–1450 total. That's roughly 5 questions wrong in RW and 8 in Math. Exact allowances shift a few questions by form, so treat it as a target band, not a guarantee.

Is there a penalty for wrong answers on the SAT?

No. Since 2016 the SAT has used rights-only scoring: wrong answers and blanks both score 0, and a wrong guess can never hurt you. If time is running out, fill in an answer for every remaining question — a coin-flip guess has positive expected value.

Is the SAT curved?

No, and this distinction matters. A curve would set your score relative to that day's test takers. Instead the SAT is equated: each form's raw-to-scaled conversion is calibrated in advance against a reference group, so a raw score earns the same scaled score no matter when you test or who else is in the room.

What is a good SAT score?

Context sets the bar. The national mean has run about 1020–1030 in recent graduating classes, so 1050 clears the average, 1200 (roughly the 75th percentile) is competitive for state flagships, 1400+ (93rd percentile and up) opens selective admissions, and 1500+ puts you in the top 2–3%. Match the target to your actual college list rather than chasing the ceiling.

Why does this calculator show a score range instead of one number?

Because the source is College Board's official scoring worksheet for the paper edition of its practice tests, which is deliberately simplified: it reports each section as a lower–upper range instead of a single equated score. Bluebook, the digital testing app, scores the real adaptive test precisely. Treat the range as the honest uncertainty band — your official score should land inside it.
