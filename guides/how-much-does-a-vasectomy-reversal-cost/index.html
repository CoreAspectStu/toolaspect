How Much Does a Vasectomy Reversal Cost?

⚕️ CPC: $12⏱️ 8 min read

A vasectomy reversal in the US commonly costs $5,000 to $15,000 all-in, with many totals near $10,000, and insurance usually pays nothing. Success odds track the years since the vasectomy: pregnancy rates ran 76% under 3 years and 30% at 15+ years in the landmark 1,469-patient study.

What are you actually paying for?

Every reversal quote is three lines stacked together: the surgeon's fee, anesthesia, and the facility fee. The surgery itself is microsurgery, often two to four hours under an operating microscope, reconnecting the vas deferens (vasovasostomy) or, when a secondary blockage has formed in the epididymis, routing around it (vasoepididymostomy, the harder repair).

The spread between those three lines is what makes prices swing so widely. The same operation can be done in a doctor's office procedure room with IV sedation, an ambulatory surgery center, or a hospital operating room under general anesthesia, and each step up the ladder adds thousands.

Dedicated reversal practices, some of which do high volumes under local or IV sedation, advertise flat all-inclusive prices that can undercut this table; hospital-based academic centers sit above it. The number that matters is an all-in written quote naming all three components, because "surgeon's fee only" quotes hide two-thirds of the bill.

Why doesn't insurance cover it?

Because the vasectomy itself is contraception, and the reversal of contraception is, in plan language, an elective sterilization reversal. Nearly every US health plan carries that exclusion. It's one of the odder asymmetries in benefits law: the ACA made vasectomies coverage-required as preventive contraception, and simultaneously no plan is required to undo one.

Occasional partial coverage happens, usually by accident of billing: an in-network hospital's facility and anesthesia fees get processed under major medical rules while the surgeon's reversal fee stays self-pay. If you're exploring this, call your insurer, ask about sterilization reversal exclusions specifically, and get anything they promise in writing. Price the procedure as fully self-pay in your budget and treat partial coverage as found money.

How much do the odds matter?

Cost is half the decision; probability is the other half. The reference data comes from the Vasovasostomy Study Group's 1991 multicenter study of 1,469 men, still the backbone of counseling today:

Years since vasectomyPatency (sperm returns)Pregnancy
Under 397%76%
3 – 888%53%
9 – 1480%44%
15+71%30%

Read the two columns differently. Patency, sperm returning to the semen, is what the surgeon controls, and even at 15+ years it's 71%. Pregnancy is a couple's outcome, and it's where the female partner's age enters: published series consistently report much lower pregnancy rates when the partner is 40 or older, sometimes halving the numbers above. Later microsurgical series report patency results comparable to or better than the 1991 figures, but the interval-and-age pattern has held up for three decades.

The interval also nudges the price. Longer obstructions raise the chance that at least one side needs a vasoepididymostomy, and since the surgeon can't know until they're operating, most couples budget for the possibility: another $1,000 to $3,000.

Reversal or IVF?

The alternative path to the same goal is in vitro fertilization with sperm extracted via a quick needle procedure (no second surgery for the vasectomy itself). Commonly published IVF-with-ICSI prices run $12,000 to $15,000+ per cycle, with medications often adding $3,000 to $5,000, so one cycle typically costs more than one reversal.

The economics hinge on how many children you want. A reversal is one payment, and if it works, subsequent pregnancies are free and natural. IVF prices every child separately. On the other side of the ledger, IVF wins when the female partner is over 40 (each cycle uses her current egg quality, unaffected by his vasectomy), when the obstruction interval is very long, or when female-factor infertility exists. Many couples get a fertility workup for both partners before choosing; a reproductive urologist and a fertility clinic will each happily run the comparison for your specific numbers.

What does recovery add to the real cost?

Budget a week off your feet and two to four weeks without heavy lifting, which for some workers means real lost income on top of the medical bill. Semen analyses start at six to eight weeks after surgery and repeat for months; follow-up visits and tests commonly add $300 to $800 over the first year. Some couples also freeze sperm found during the procedure as an IVF backup, another $300 to $1,000, worth considering when the odds row is less favorable.

Once pregnancy happens, the calculator you'll want next is a different one entirely: a due date calculator for the arrival, or an ovulation calculator while you're trying, since timing intercourse to the fertile window matters more after a reversal.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a vasectomy reversal cost out of pocket?

Commonly published US totals run $5,000 to $15,000, with many near $10,000. An ambulatory surgery center with IV sedation typically lands in the middle: roughly $3,750 surgeon, $1,000 anesthesia, $2,750 facility, about $7,500 all-in. Dedicated reversal practices sometimes advertise flat bundles below that; hospital ORs with general anesthesia run well above it.

Will insurance pay for a vasectomy reversal?

Almost never. Health plans classify reversal as an elective sterilization reversal and exclude it. The ACA's contraception coverage requirement applies to vasectomies, not reversals. Occasional partial coverage exists (anesthesia or facility through an in-network hospital), but you should price the procedure as self-pay and treat any coverage as a discount, not a plan.

How successful is a vasectomy reversal after 10 years?

In the Vasovasostomy Study Group report, the 9-to-14-year interval showed 80% patency and 44% pregnancy. At 15+ years it was 71% and 30%. Patency means sperm return; pregnancy depends equally on the female partner's age, with published rates dropping sharply once she's past 40.

Is a reversal cheaper than IVF?

For couples wanting more than one child, often yes. IVF with ICSI commonly runs $12,000 to $15,000+ per cycle plus $3,000 to $5,000 in medications, and each child generally needs another cycle. A reversal is one payment after which conception happens naturally. With a female partner over 40, a long obstruction interval, or female-factor infertility, IVF usually wins on odds per dollar.
