What Happens to SSDI at Full Retirement Age?

📄 7 min readUpdated October 16, 2026

SSDI doesn't end when you get older. At full retirement age, which is 67 for anyone born in 1960 or later, Social Security quietly converts your disability benefit into a retirement benefit. You don't reapply, and for most people the check is the same size the next month. What does change is the rulebook: the earnings limit disappears, medical reviews stop, and you get the option to delay to 70. Here's what to expect.

Does SSDI stop when you reach full retirement age?

The payments don't stop. The label changes. In the month you reach full retirement age (FRA), Social Security reclassifies your disability insurance benefit as a retirement benefit. You don't file a new application, and there's no gap in payments. Most people only notice because a letter arrives.

The amount usually stays exactly the same. That follows from how SSDI is figured. Your disability check is your primary insurance amount (PIA), the same number Social Security would pay you if you retired at FRA. SSDI never applies an early-claiming reduction, so there's nothing to "restore" at conversion. The 2.8% cost-of-living increase for 2026 applied to SSDI checks too, and future COLAs carry over as normal.

There's one more piece working in your favor: the disability freeze. Years when you were disabled and earning little are left out of your benefit calculation, so a long stretch on SSDI doesn't drag your retirement amount down.

When exactly does the switch happen?

It depends on your birth year. Full retirement age has been climbing since the 1983 amendments, and it tops out at 67:

So the people converting right now were born in 1959. Anyone born in 1960 or later converts at 67, starting in 2027. One quirk: Social Security treats you as reaching an age the day before your birthday, which matters if you were born on the 1st of a month: you're treated as reaching FRA a month earlier, and a January 1 birthday counts as the previous birth year.

A related rule catches people out. You can't apply for SSDI once you've reached FRA, because at that point you'd just take retirement benefits. If you're 64 or 65 and can no longer work, apply now rather than waiting.

What changes after SSDI becomes a retirement benefit?

The check doesn't change, but several rules around it do. Most of them loosen:

The biggest practical difference is work. On SSDI, earning more than the substantial gainful activity (SGA) level, $1,690 a month in 2026, can end your benefits after the trial work period. Once you've reached FRA there's no earnings test at all. You could take a full-time job and keep every dollar of your Social Security. If you've been holding back from work to protect your SSDI, that constraint disappears.

Can my check go up when SSDI converts?

Usually not on the conversion itself. It can rise for other reasons, though:

  • Earnings while on SSDI. If you worked during a trial work period or after leaving the rolls and those earnings beat one of the years in your record, an automatic recomputation can raise your PIA.
  • Delayed retirement credits. Once you're at FRA you can ask Social Security to suspend your benefit. Every month it's suspended earns two-thirds of 1%, or 8% a year, until 70.

Here's what suspending looks like on an $1,800 SSDI check that converts at 67, ignoring COLAs:

ChoiceMonthly benefitPaid from 67 to 70Break-even age
Keep collecting at 67$1,800$64,800n/a
Suspend 67 to 70$2,232 (124%)$0About 82 and a half

You give up 36 checks, $64,800, to get $432 more a month. That takes 150 months, or 12.5 years, to win back. It pays off if you expect to live well past your early 80s, or if you're the higher earner and want a bigger survivor benefit for your spouse. Anyone getting benefits on your record, other than a divorced spouse, is suspended along with you, so check that first.

What if I took early retirement before getting approved for SSDI?

SSDI decisions are slow, so someone often files for disability at 62, waits a year, and claims reduced retirement in the meantime to pay the bills. If SSDI is later approved with an onset date before the retirement claim, Social Security can replace the reduced retirement benefit with SSDI and pay back the difference.

If the approved onset date falls after you started retirement benefits, it's messier: the months you were paid reduced retirement before you became entitled to SSDI can leave a smaller, permanent reduction once you reach FRA. Get the onset date right in your application, and appeal if it's set too late. Our guide to SSDI denials and appeals covers the deadlines.

Does my family's benefit change at conversion?

A spouse or child already collecting on your record keeps collecting. Conversion doesn't end their benefits or change who qualifies. Children's benefits still end at 18, or 19 if they're in high school full time, the same as before.

If you get SSI rather than SSDI, none of this applies. SSI is a needs-based program, not an insured benefit, so there's nothing to convert. At 65 you can stay on SSI on the basis of age instead, as long as you still meet the income and asset limits.

And if you have a private long-term disability policy, read it now. Many of them pay only until 65 or Social Security's FRA, and most reduce their payment by your SSDI amount while it runs.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to apply for retirement when my SSDI ends?

No. Social Security converts your disability benefit to a retirement benefit automatically in the month you reach full retirement age. You'll get a notice, but there's no application and no break in payments.

Will my Social Security check go down when SSDI converts at 67?

No. Your SSDI check is already your full, unreduced benefit, so the converted retirement benefit is normally the same amount. It can be higher if your later earnings raised your record, but it won't be lower.

Can I work after my SSDI converts to retirement?

Yes, without limits. The SGA rule ($1,690 a month in 2026) and the retirement earnings test both stop applying at full retirement age, so you can earn any amount and keep your full benefit.

Can I delay my benefit to 70 after being on SSDI?

Yes. Once you reach full retirement age you can ask Social Security to suspend your benefit and earn delayed retirement credits of 8% a year until 70. Family members paid on your record, other than a divorced spouse, are suspended too.

Does Medicare change when SSDI turns into retirement?

No. If you already have Medicare through SSDI, your coverage just continues. You don't re-enroll, and you don't get a new initial enrollment period.
