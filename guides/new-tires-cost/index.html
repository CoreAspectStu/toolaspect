How Much Do New Tires Cost?

🛞 Auto maintenance⏱️ 6 min read

A set of four new tires costs $400 to $1,200 installed for most cars and crossovers. The rubber runs $80-$200 per tire in common 15-18 inch sizes, and the shop adds $100-$200 in labor for mounting, balancing, valves, and disposal. Big wheels, performance compounds, and all-terrain truck sizes change the math fast — those sets land between $1,000 and $2,500. Here's how the price is built and where the money goes.

What drives the per-tire price

Three things set the sticker: diameter, category, and tier. Diameter first — a 20-inch tire simply contains more material than a 16-inch one, and low-volume giant sizes carry low-volume pricing. Category second, because compounds differ: a soft performance tread grips harder and costs more than a hard-wearing touring tread from the same brand. Tier third: within one category, the budget-to-premium spread is often two-to-one.

The installation stack

The out-the-door price hides in the labor line. A typical invoice has four pieces:

  • Mount and balance: $15-$50 per tire. Warehouse clubs package it near $20, independents run $25-$35, dealers charge the most.
  • Valve stems or TPMS kits: $3-$15 per tire. Sensors themselves are reused unless their 7-10 year battery is dead — then add $60-$120 each.
  • Disposal: $1-$5 per tire. State-mandated in much of the country.
  • Alignment: $75-$150 two-wheel, $100-$200 four-wheel. Optional, but read the next section before skipping it.

Add it up and installation runs $100-$200 for a set of four at most shops before any alignment — roughly 15-20% of the total bill on a mid-priced set.

When the alignment is worth it

An alignment isn't a required part of a tire purchase, and shops that push it on every car are overselling. But do say yes when the old tires show uneven wear (one edge chewed down), the car pulls, or you've hit a pothole hard enough to remember it. Driving a new set on a misaligned chassis can shave 20-30% off its tread life — on a $900 set, that's throwing away $200-plus to save $125.

Thinking in cost per mile

The cheapest set and the longest-lasting set are rarely the same tires, and sticker price hides that. Divide installed cost by expected tread life:

  • $700 mid-tier set, 55,000 miles: 1.3 cents per mile, about $153 a year at 12,000 miles.
  • $1,216 premium performance set, 40,000 miles: 3.0 cents per mile, about $365 a year.
  • $1,900 all-terrain set, 50,000 miles: 3.8 cents per mile, about $456 a year.

Now the comparison is honest: the performance set costs about $212 more per year to own than the mid-tier set. Grip may well be worth it — just decide it as an annual cost, not a one-time number.

Five ways to pay less without cheaping out

  1. Buy in the rebate seasons. Spring and fall promotions from major brands routinely knock $60-$120 off a set of four.
  2. Compare the installed price, not the tire price. A shop that quotes $10 more per tire but includes rotation and road hazard is usually cheaper over the life of the set.
  3. Shop your size, not your model. Different vehicles share tire sizes; pricing the size across brands beats starting from your car's OEM tire.
  4. Ask about house brands. Many are built in the same factories as name brands and carry longer warranties at a lower price.
  5. Keep the alignment honest and the rotations current. Free rotation every 5,000-7,500 miles is the cheapest tread-life extension available.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much is a full set of new tires?

Most drivers pay $400 to $1,200 for four tires installed. Common 15-18 inch all-seasons run $80-$200 per tire, installation adds $100-$200 for the set, and tax applies in most states. Performance tires, 20-inch-plus wheels, and all-terrain truck sizes push a set to $1,400-$2,500.

Why are bigger tires so much more expensive?

Larger-diameter tires use more rubber and belting, come in lower-volume sizes, and skew toward performance and truck categories where buyers pay more. Moving from a 16-inch to a 20-inch size can triple the per-tire price even within the same brand and model line.

Can I replace just two tires instead of four?

Yes if the other two have plenty of tread, and it's the standard move after a single unfixable puncture. Put the new pair on the rear axle for wet-traction stability regardless of drive wheels. On some all-wheel-drive vehicles, a large tread-depth difference can strain the drivetrain, so check your owner's manual first.

How often do tires need replacing?

Most all-season and touring tires last 50,000-65,000 miles; performance tires 30,000-45,000; all-terrains 40,000-60,000. Replace at 4/32 inch of tread for wet safety (2/32 is the legal minimum), and regardless of tread after about six years of age — rubber hardens and grip drops.

Do I have to replace TPMS sensors with the tires?

No. Sensors last roughly 7-10 years, so most tire changes reuse them with a $3-$15 service kit per wheel. Replace a sensor only when its battery dies or it's damaged during the swap; a new one adds $60-$120 per wheel including programming.
