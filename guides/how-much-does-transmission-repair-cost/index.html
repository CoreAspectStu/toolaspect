How Much Does Transmission Repair Cost?

⚙️ 25K searches/mo⏱️ 7 min read

"The transmission's going" is the scariest sentence in car ownership, mostly because nobody tells you it has five different prices. The same car can leave a shop $400 lighter (a solenoid) or $4,000 lighter (a remanufactured unit). Here are the national ranges for every path, what moves them, and the one ratio that decides whether you fix the car or replace it.

What does each repair path cost?

National installed ranges for a typical car (sedan, automatic). SUVs and vans run about 10% more, pickups about 15%, and luxury or European vehicles roughly 50-60% more:

Labor is the quiet constant: removing and reinstalling a transmission runs roughly 6-12 flat-rate hours, which is $600-$2,000 at prevailing rates of $100-$180 an hour before any parts. That's why even a "free" salvage-yard unit still costs four figures installed, and why shops charge real money just to open the thing up.

Type-specific wrinkles: a CVT reman runs $3,500-$5,500 because CVTs are rarely rebuilt (worn pulleys and belts mean replacing the core). A manual rebuild runs $1,000-$2,500, and the far more common manual job, a clutch replacement, runs $800-$1,800 including the kit. A fluid and filter service runs $150-$400.

Is it worth fixing, or should I sell the car?

The ratio mechanics use: repair cost against the car's value after the repair.

  • Under 30%: fix it, this is a mathematically easy call
  • 30-50%: usually still fix it, but audit the rest of the car (tires, brakes, suspension, rust) before committing
  • 50-75%: the used-unit path or a different car starts winning
  • Over 75%: the repair bill is the down payment on its replacement

Worked example: a 2016 Ford Explorer with a slipping automatic. Rebuild on an SUV: $1,650-$3,850, call it $2,640 in the middle. The Explorer's worth about $9,000 fixed, so the repair is 29% of value. Fix it. The same repair on a 2012 Sentra CVT worth $4,500 fixed is $4,300 at midpoint, 96% of value, and that car's next stop is a salvage yard. What are the first signs of transmission trouble?

Slipping (engine revs, car doesn't accelerate), hard or delayed shifts, a burnt smell on the dipstick, red-brown puddles under the car, grinding or whining, and any transmission warning light. Timing matters enormously here: caught early, the fault is often a solenoid, a valve body, or low fluid, which is the $150-$1,200 row of the table. Driven until the clutches glaze over, it's the $2,500 row. If the fluid is burnt or there's metal in the pan, stop driving it; every mile is converting a repairable unit into scrap.

Rebuild vs replace vs used: how to choose

  1. Diagnose first. A $150-$200 diagnostic (scan, fluid check, pressure test, maybe a pan drop) tells you which row of the table you're actually in. Never buy a rebuild on symptoms alone.
  2. Rebuild keeps your case and hard parts that are still good, with fresh clutches, seals, and solenoids. Right call for a car you're keeping, from a shop that will show you the teardown.
  3. Used is fastest and cheapest, and it's a coin flip: you're installing someone else's 80k-120k miles. Sensible for cars worth under ~$6,000 where every other path doesn't pencil.
  4. Remanufactured means the unit was rebuilt to factory spec industrially, with updated parts, and typically carries the best warranty. Best value per year of confidence if the car justifies it.
  5. Get two quotes and make sure they name the same path, include the torque converter (add $300-$800 if it doesn't), fluid, and any reprogramming a modern unit needs after install.

One more angle before signing anything: if an extended warranty or manufacturer powertrain coverage is still in play, a covered replacement changes all of this math. And if the verdict lands on "replace the car," the junk car value calculator prices what the dead one is worth as scrap and parts, which is often $200-$600 more than people expect.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the first signs of transmission failure?

Slipping between gears, hard or delayed shifts, a burnt smell on the dipstick, grinding or whining, fluid leaks (red or brown puddles), and a transmission warning light. Catching a solenoid or valve-body problem early is the difference between a $150-$1,200 repair and a $1,500-$3,500 rebuild, so symptoms that appeared in the last few hundred miles deserve a diagnostic visit this week, not this quarter.

Why do transmission quotes vary so much between shops?

Because the labor is the same but the parts strategy isn't. Remove-and-replace runs 6-12 flat-rate hours ($600-$2,000 at $100-$180/hr), then the shop chooses: rebuild your unit in-house, install a salvage unit, or install a remanufactured unit. Each path prices differently and carries a different warranty. Always ask which path a quote assumes, and whether the price includes the torque converter, fluid, and any programming the new unit needs.

Is a transmission flush a repair?

No, and on a failing transmission it can make things worse. A fluid and filter service ($150-$400) is maintenance for a healthy unit and occasionally fixes sticking caused by old fluid. But if the clutches are worn or there's metal in the pan, fresh fluid won't rebuild hard parts, and shops that push a flush as a fix for real symptoms are guessing with your money. Diagnose first: a $150 scan and pressure test beats a $400 hopeful flush.
