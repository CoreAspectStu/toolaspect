Do free image tools upload your photos?

Plenty of them do. The usual pattern is simple: you drop a file, it goes to a server, the server does the work, and you download the result. The site promises to delete it after an hour or a day. That's often fine. It's less fine for a passport scan, a photo of your kids or a client's unreleased product shot.

Browser-based tools work differently. The page downloads the code, and sometimes a small AI model, then does the processing on your own machine. You can test any site in ten seconds: load the tool, turn on airplane mode, then run the job. If you still get a result, the photo never left your device. Everything from us in the table above passes that test, apart from the reverse image search, which has to talk to the search engines by design.

How much smaller can you make a photo?

More than most people expect, and the biggest win usually isn't the quality slider. It's the pixel count. A typical 12-megapixel phone photo is 4,032 by 3,024 pixels. If it's going on a web page that shows it 1,200 pixels wide, it's carrying about 11 times more pixels than anyone will see.

When should you use GIMP or Photopea instead?

Single-purpose tools are quick because they do one thing. Once you need layers, masks, healing, text on a curve or careful color work, you want a real editor. The two free ones worth knowing are GIMP, a desktop program for Windows, Mac and Linux, and Photopea, a browser editor that opens PSD files and feels a lot like Photoshop. Photopea is free with ads. Inkscape is the free pick if you're drawing vectors rather than editing photos.

A good rule: if you can describe the job in three words ("crop to square", "remove the background", "make it smaller"), use a single tool. If it takes a sentence, open an editor.

What about AI image tools?

Two of the tools above use small AI models: the background remover and the upscaler. They run in your browser, which keeps them private and free, but they're smaller than the server models paid services use. That shows up in the hard cases: flyaway hair, glass and very blurry source images. For a profile photo or a product shot on a plain wall, the free versions are usually enough. Frequently Asked Questions

What's the best free image editor in 2026?

For full layer-based editing, GIMP on the desktop and Photopea in the browser are the two free options most people end up with. For single jobs like compressing, cropping or removing a background, a dedicated tool is faster because there's nothing to learn.

Are free online image tools safe?

It depends on where the work happens. Many free sites upload your photo to their server, process it there and delete it later. Tools that run in your browser never send the file anywhere. You can check by loading the page, switching on airplane mode and running the job. If it still works, nothing was uploaded.

How do I make an image smaller without losing quality?

Convert it to WebP at a quality setting around 75 to 85 and, if it's bigger than it'll ever be displayed, cap the width too. A 4000-pixel photo shown at 1200 pixels wide is carrying more than ten times the pixels it needs. Resizing usually saves more than any quality slider.

Can I remove a background for free without a watermark?

Yes. In-browser background removers such as ours export a transparent PNG at the photo's own size with no watermark. iPhone and Mac users can also long-press the subject in Photos to lift it out. Paid services still do better on fine hair and glass.

Why do some free image tools add a watermark?

Usually because the free tier is a trial for a paid plan. The watermark, a size cap or a low-resolution download is what nudges you to pay. Tools that run entirely in your browser cost very little to operate, so they don't need that nudge.
