How Much Does a Car Battery Cost? (2026)

🔋 ~20K searches/mo💰 CPC: $7⏱️ 7 min read

A car battery costs $79-$160 flooded, $130-$230 EFB, and $180-$320 AGM, with installed totals of $100-$400 for most cars — and in a twist most people miss, often $0 extra for installation, because the big parts chains bolt it in free with purchase. Here's the whole bill: chemistry, labor routes, the refundable core charge, and what a battery actually costs per year of service.

The label on your old battery settles this: if it says EFB or AGM, that's the spec (AGM counts as an upgrade over EFB; a plain flooded battery does not). If your car restarts its engine at stoplights and you install a $99 flooded battery to save money, the deep cycling of the restarts will eat it in months — a false economy that costs more per month of service than the battery you were supposed to buy. AGM also charges at different voltages, so even the alternator cares.

The free install most people don't price

AutoZone, O'Reilly, Advance, and most Walmart locations install standard underhood batteries free with purchase, which collapses the "installed" line to zero. The catches are real but narrow: they won't work on cars where the battery lives under a seat or in the trunk (some BMW, Mercedes, and Dodge models), they don't do battery registration on European cars, and the vehicle has to be at the store since nobody tows a dead car to a free install.

When those catches bite, the ladder is: independent shop at $30-$80 labor, dealer at $60-$150 plus a $30-$100 registration fee on European start-stop vehicles. Dealer AGM replacements have been quoted past $600 on premium cars — which sounds outrageous until you decompose it into a $300 battery, $100+ labor, coding, and dealership overhead, and then ask an independent to do the same job for $250-$350.

What is a battery core charge?

A core charge is a $10-$25 deposit added when you buy a battery without trading in the old one. It's not a fee — it's collateral. Dead batteries are nearly 100% recyclable into new ones, so the deposit exists to pull old cores back through the system. Bring the old battery with you and the charge never appears; return it within the store's window (usually 30-90 days, longer in states with battery deposit laws) and the money comes back. Retailers are required to accept cores for return even months later in most states, so there's rarely a reason to leave it behind.

What a battery costs per year

The sticker price lies a little: what you care about is cost per year of service. A $79-$160 flooded battery lasting 3-5 years runs $16-$53 a year. An EFB at $130-$230 over 4-6 years runs $22-$58. An AGM at $180-$320 over 5-7 years runs $26-$64. Notice the bands overlap heavily: AGM's premium mostly buys start-stop capability and deep-cycle tolerance, not a dramatically cheaper year of service. Heat is the other variable — in Phoenix or San Antonio, trim a year or more off every one of those lifespans.

For context inside the maintenance budget, a battery is roughly the same order of magnitude as the year's oil changes: the oil change calculator prices those per mile, and the 30/60/90K maintenance calculator places both inside the $380-$800 a year a mainstream car typically consumes.

How to buy without overpaying

  • Match the group size, then compare price per battery, not per brand. The group number (35, 48, H6…) is on the old label; within a chemistry, retail brands are closer than marketing suggests.
  • Buy at a store that installs free if the battery is under the hood and your car doesn't need coding; the labor line disappears.
  • Trade the core at purchase. The $10-$25 never leaves your pocket.
  • Date-check the battery. A letter and digit stamp the month and year; anything older than six months on the shelf has already spent part of its life.
  • Test before you replace. Free load tests at parts stores confirm the battery is actually the problem, not the alternator or a parasitic drain.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much should I pay for a car battery installed?

A flooded battery runs $109-$240 installed at an independent shop ($79-$160 for the part), EFB $160-$310, and AGM $210-$400. Most parts stores install free with purchase, so the installed price and the battery price are often the same number. Dealer installs run $270-$570 on AGM cars because of $60-$150 labor plus battery registration on European vehicles.

Is it worth paying more for an AGM battery?

If the car came with AGM, it is not a choice: start-stop systems deep-cycle the battery on every restart and a flooded battery fails in months. If your car didn't come with AGM, the premium buys little. Per year of service, flooded at $79-$160 costs $16-$53 and AGM at $180-$320 costs $26-$64, so the extra money mostly buys capability, not longevity.

What happens if you don't return a battery core?

You simply lose the $10-$25 deposit. The core charge exists because dead batteries are recycled into new ones; stores must accept the old core for return even months later in most states, so there's rarely a reason to leave money on the table.
