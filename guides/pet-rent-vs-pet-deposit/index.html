Pet Rent vs Pet Deposit vs Pet Fee: What Renters Actually Pay

🐾 "pet rent" / "pet deposit" / "pet fees for apartments" 20-35K searches/mo⏱️ 8 min read

Three pet charges, three different meanings. Pet rent is monthly (averaging $35.65 in 2025) and never comes back. A pet fee is one-time (about $315 on average) and never comes back. A pet deposit (around $250) is one-time and refundable if your pet doesn't damage anything. Added up, the average dog costs about $743 in year one and $428 a year after that — before you buy a single bag of food. Here's how the three charges compare, which ones cost you most, and where the law draws lines.

Which one costs the most? It depends how long you stay

The time horizon flips the answer. On a 12-month lease, a $315 fee and $35 monthly rent are almost identical ($315 vs $420), and the refundable $250 deposit is the cheapest of all if your pet behaves — you get it back. Stretch to a three-year stay and the picture changes completely:

This is why the industry has shifted hard toward monthly pet rent: it's the charge that keeps growing. If you plan to stay put, negotiating a larger one-time deposit in place of monthly rent can save four figures over a long tenancy. If you move often, the reverse is true — one-time fees hurt most when you pay them every year at a new building.

Why landlords love monthly pet rent

Three reasons, all economic. First, recurring revenue beats one-time revenue, especially when management companies report monthly income. Second, deposits are regulated — many states cap total security deposits and require returning them with itemized deductions within a deadline, while pet rent has almost no restrictions anywhere. Third, legal shifts accelerated the move: California's AB 12, effective mid-2024, folds pet deposits into a security-deposit cap of one month's rent, so a landlord who used to collect two months of deposit plus a pet deposit now can't. Monthly pet rent stayed untouched, and much of that deposit money converted straight into pet rent.

Assistance animals: the line the law draws

Here's the rule that overrides all the others: under the federal Fair Housing Act, service animals and emotional support animals are not pets. A landlord cannot charge pet rent, a pet fee, or a pet deposit for them — not $10 a month, not a dollar. What a landlord can do is request reliable documentation of the disability-related need (for ESAs, typically a letter from a treating professional), charge for actual damage the animal causes just like any other damage, and hold the animal to legitimate conduct rules. A "pet screening fee" applied to an assistance animal is equally off-limits. If you run into a blanket policy, a note referencing fair-housing rules — or a call to a local tenant-rights group — usually resolves it.

How to pay less in pet charges

  1. Negotiate before applying. Pet charges are set by policy, not statute, in most markets. Offering a bigger refundable deposit or a longer lease in exchange for waived pet rent works more often than renters expect.
  2. Ask for a cap. Many buildings that quote pet rent per pet will cap total charges for a second animal, or waive the fee at renewal. Ask the question — silence costs you the discount.
  3. Check the deposit math. In states where pet deposits ride along with security-deposit rules, an oversized pet deposit may be legally limited. Know the cap before you hand over the check.
  4. Get pet rent terms in the lease. Verbal "we don't charge for cats" assurances evaporate at renewal. Whatever you agreed to belongs in writing, including whether the charge applies per pet or per apartment.
  5. Document condition at move-in. The refundable deposit only comes back if damage is provably pre-existing. Photos of baseboards, blinds, and flooring on day one protect your deposit on day last.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's cheaper: pet rent or a pet deposit?

It depends on how long you stay. A $250 refundable deposit costs you nothing if your pet does no damage, while $35 monthly pet rent costs $420 a year — so short stays favor deposits. Stay four years and the deposit is still $250 while pet rent has hit $1,680. The catch: landlords know this, which is why many have dropped deposits for rent and fees.

Can a landlord charge both pet rent and a pet deposit?

In most states, yes — pet rent, a pet fee, and a pet deposit can all be charged together, and they're typically quoted per pet. A few states fold pet deposits into the overall security-deposit cap (California's one-month cap, effective 2024, includes them), but that limits the deposit, not the monthly rent or non-refundable fee.

Do emotional support animals avoid pet rent?

Yes. Under the federal Fair Housing Act, assistance animals — service animals and emotional support animals with reliable documentation — are not pets, so pet rent, pet fees, and pet deposits can't be charged for them. The landlord can still charge for actual damage and can verify the disability-related need, but a blanket 'pet rent' charge on an ESA violates fair housing rules.

Is pet rent negotiable?

More than you'd think — especially at smaller landlords and in softer rental markets. Offering a larger refundable deposit in place of monthly rent, signing a longer lease, or showing vaccination and training records all work as bargaining chips. The numbers are small enough that many landlords would rather fill the unit than hold out for $35 a month.

How much do pets add to rent per year?

At the 2025 national averages — $35.65 a month pet rent plus a $315 one-time fee — a single pet costs about $743 in the first year and $428 every year after. Two pets at the same charges run roughly $1,485 in year one. Over a three-year lease, one pet at average charges costs about $1,598.

This guide is general information about pet charges and fair-housing rules, not legal advice. Tenant protections vary by state and city — confirm your situation with your lease, your state's landlord-tenant agency, or a local tenant-rights organization.
