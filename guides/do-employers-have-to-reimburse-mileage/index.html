Do Employers Have to Reimburse Mileage?

📄 7 min readUpdated October 13, 2026

Under federal law, usually not. The Fair Labor Standards Act has no rule that says employers must pay for miles you drive on the job. There are two big exceptions. If unpaid driving costs drag your pay below minimum wage, the employer owes the difference. And a handful of states, led by California and Illinois, require employers to cover necessary work expenses, mileage included. If you do get reimbursed, the IRS business rate for 2026 is 72.5 cents a mile through June 30 and 76 cents from July 1. Our Mileage Reimbursement Calculator handles both halves of the year.

What does federal law say about mileage?

Nothing that requires it directly. The FLSA sets the minimum wage and overtime, and that's it. An employer in most states can legally ask you to drive your own car for work and pay nothing extra, as long as one condition holds: after your vehicle costs are taken out, you still earned at least the minimum wage for every hour.

The federal regulation behind that, 29 CFR 531.35, says wages must be paid "free and clear." Costs an employer pushes onto you for its own benefit count against that. Lawyers call it the kickback rule.

Which states require mileage reimbursement?

When does unpaid mileage break minimum wage?

When you're paid close to the minimum and drive a lot. Take a worker earning $8 an hour for a 40-hour week, which is $320. The federal minimum for that week is $290, so there's only a $30 cushion. If they drive 100 business miles and the cost is valued at the IRS rate of 76 cents, that's $76 of car costs. Their pay drops to $244, or $6.10 an hour, which is under $7.25.

How the car cost gets valued is where the fights happen. The Labor Department's investigator handbook lets its staff use the IRS rate. A later Labor Department opinion letter said employers can use a reasonable estimate of actual costs instead, and in 2024 the Sixth Circuit rejected automatic use of the IRS rate in a delivery-driver case. Pizza and delivery drivers have brought a lot of these suits. The safest assumption for an employer is to track real costs or pay enough to stay clear.

Is mileage reimbursement taxable?

Not if it's paid under what the IRS calls an accountable plan. That means you log the business miles, the date and the purpose, submit them within a reasonable time, and any overpayment gets returned. Paid at or below the IRS rate this way, the money isn't wages and doesn't show up as income on your W-2.

A flat monthly car allowance with no mileage log is different. It's usually treated as taxable pay. So is the part of any reimbursement that runs above the IRS rate.

Can I deduct mileage if my employer doesn't pay?

On your federal return, almost certainly not. The 2017 tax law suspended the deduction for unreimbursed employee expenses through 2025, and the 2025 tax law (the One Big Beautiful Bill Act) made that permanent. A few groups can still claim them on Form 2106, including Armed Forces reservists, qualified performing artists, fee-basis state and local officials, and people with impairment-related work expenses.

Some states still allow the deduction on the state return, California among them. Self-employed people and independent contractors are a separate case: they deduct business miles on Schedule C, which lowers both income tax and self-employment tax. Gig drivers should log every business mile for that reason. The Self-Employment Tax Calculator shows what that deduction is worth.

What should I do if my employer won't pay?

  • Keep a mileage log: date, start and end point, miles, and the business reason. Commuting from home to your regular workplace doesn't count
  • Read the written expense policy, if there is one. Some employers pay but only if you file within a set window
  • Do the minimum-wage math for a heavy driving week, as in the example above
  • If you're in one of the states listed, put the request in writing and cite the law
  • File a wage claim with your state labor office if the employer still refuses. California claims can earn 10% annual interest from the date of each expense, plus attorney's fees

Frequently Asked Questions

Is my employer legally required to reimburse mileage?

Not under federal law, unless unpaid vehicle costs push your pay below minimum wage. Some states, including California, Illinois, Iowa, New Hampshire, Montana, North Dakota and South Dakota, require employers to cover necessary work expenses.

What is the IRS mileage rate for 2026?

For business driving it is 72.5 cents per mile from January 1 to June 30, 2026, and 76 cents per mile from July 1 to December 31, 2026. Medical and military moving miles went from 20.5 to 23.5 cents, and charity miles stay at 14 cents.

Do employers have to pay the IRS mileage rate?

No. The IRS rate is a tax benchmark, not a legal minimum. Employers can pay less, or use a flat allowance, as long as they meet minimum wage and any state law that requires full reimbursement.

Is a mileage reimbursement taxable income?

Not when it is paid under an accountable plan at or below the IRS rate, with a mileage log. A flat car allowance without records, or the part paid above the IRS rate, is usually taxable wages.

Can W-2 employees deduct unreimbursed mileage?

Not on the federal return for most people. The deduction was suspended in 2018 and the 2025 tax law made that permanent. Reservists, qualified performing artists, fee-basis officials and some others are exceptions, and a few states still allow it.
