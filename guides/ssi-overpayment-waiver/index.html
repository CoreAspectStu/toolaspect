SSI Overpayment Waiver: Who Qualifies and How to Ask

📄 7 min readUpdated October 28, 2026

An SSI overpayment notice can ask for thousands of dollars back. You may not have to pay it. A waiver on Form SSA-632 can wipe out all or part of the debt if it wasn't your fault and repaying would be unfair. Here's how SSA decides, what it takes from your check meanwhile, and the 30-day window that stops the deductions.

What is an SSI overpayment waiver?

It's a request asking Social Security to forgive some or all of an SSI overpayment so you don't have to pay it back. If SSA approves it, the waived amount is gone for good, for you and for an eligible spouse. It doesn't have to be all or nothing, either. The regulation's own example is a $390 overpayment where $260 was waived and only $130 was left to repay.

You ask for a waiver on Form SSA-632. There's no deadline. You can ask years into repayment, but timing still matters, which we'll get to below.

Who qualifies for an SSI overpayment waiver?

You need to clear two hurdles. First, you have to be without fault in causing the overpayment. Second, making you repay has to fail at least one of three fairness tests. Most denials happen at the first hurdle, not the second.

"Without fault" isn't the same as "SSA made the mistake." SSA's error doesn't clear you on its own. SSA looks at whether you understood what to report, what you did to report it, and anything that made it harder: age, memory, illness, education or limited English. If you called about a new job and the change never got entered, give the date. That detail is what wins these.

Then at least one of these has to apply:

You can't get SSI with unearned income above $1,014 anyway, so if your other income is a small Social Security check, a pension or nothing, you pass automatically. Only people with bigger paychecks need to show their budget. For most people on SSI, the case comes down to fault.

How much will SSA take from your SSI check?

By default, the lesser of your whole payment or 10% of your total monthly income. "Total income" means your countable income plus your SSI and any state supplement. In a state with no supplement, that sum works out to the federal rate for most people, so the default is about $99.40 a month in 2026.

That's a far gentler default than SSDI, where new overpayment notices default to 50%. Here's how long it lasts, and what it would take to clear the same debt in the 12 or 60 months SSA aims for when it negotiates a lower rate:

If the 10% leaves you short on rent, food or utilities, ask for a lower amount on Form SSA-634 and show your income, bills and bank balances. You can also ask for a higher rate to get it over with. If SSA finds fraud or deliberate concealment, the 10% cap doesn't apply.

Should you appeal or ask for a waiver?

They answer different questions, and you can file both. An appeal says "you're wrong." A waiver says "you're right about the amount, but I shouldn't have to repay it."

The timing that matters most is 30 days. SSA's manual has withholding start 60 days after the overpayment notice when you haven't responded within 30 days or asked for an appeal or waiver. File inside that window and nothing comes out of your check while SSA decides. File later and the deductions keep running until a waiver is approved, though they stop once it is.

What happens if SSA wants to deny the waiver?

It can't just mail a denial. SSA first has to offer you a personal conference, in person, by phone or by video, with a file review at least 5 days earlier so you can see the evidence. You can bring a representative, and the decision-maker must be someone who wasn't involved in the overpayment. Bring the phone logs, letters and receipts that show what you reported and when.

What if you've left SSI but get SSDI or retirement?

SSA can take the SSI debt from your Social Security check. This cross-program recovery defaults to the lesser of the balance or 10% of the monthly benefit, about $163 on the 2026 average SSDI check of $1,630. A pending waiver or appeal keeps the debt from being picked up, and you can ask for a lower rate.

What should go in your SSA-632?

Fill in the money questions completely, since they decide the "defeat the purpose" test if you don't pass it automatically. But most cases turn on the section about why you weren't at fault. Write it as a timeline: "March 3: started part-time job. March 10: called the 800 number and gave pay stub details. May: check unchanged." Attach anything that backs it up. If you were over the resource limit by $50 or less, say so up front. And don't offer money from a child's dedicated account for back pay: it can't be used to repay.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can SSA take my whole SSI check for an overpayment?

Not normally. The default is the lesser of your full payment or 10% of your total monthly income, about $99.40 in 2026 for most people. The cap doesn't apply to fraud or deliberate concealment.

Is there a deadline to request an SSI overpayment waiver?

No. You can file Form SSA-632 at any time. If you file within 30 days of the overpayment notice, SSA won't start withholding while it decides. File later and deductions continue until the waiver is approved.

Do I have to repay an SSI overpayment if it was SSA's mistake?

Not necessarily, but SSA's mistake alone doesn't win a waiver. You must also show you were without fault, for example that you reported the change on time. If you did, recovery is often waived.

What if I was only slightly over the $2,000 resource limit?

If your countable resources were over the limit by $50 or less, SSA treats you as without fault and waives the overpayment, unless you knowingly and willfully failed to report the money.

Can SSA take my SSDI to repay an SSI overpayment?

Yes, once you're off SSI. SSA withholds the lesser of the balance or 10% of your monthly benefit unless you negotiate a different rate.
