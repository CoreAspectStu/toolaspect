How Much Is Sales Tax on a Car? Rates by State (2026)

💡 22K searches/mo💰 CPC: $5⏱️ 8 min read ⚠️ Estimates, not tax advice

Sales tax on a vehicle is one multiplication — taxable price times your combined state and local rate — but the inputs vary more than almost any other purchase you'll make. Four states charge nothing. Georgia invented its own 7% title tax. South Carolina caps the bill at $500. And the trade-in sitting on the dealer's lot can be worth thousands in tax savings, unless you live in California. Here's the full map.

The formula, and what feeds it

Tax = (purchase price − trade-in credit) × (state rate + local rate). Three things move the result:

  1. Your state's base rate, from 0% (Oregon) to 7.25% (California's statewide base).
  2. Your county and city add-ons, which stack on top in most states — this is why the same state can produce very different bills a few miles apart.
  3. What counts as the taxable price, which is where trade-in credits, rebates, and special systems come in.

Registration, title, and documentation fees ride on top of the tax; they're separate line items on the dealer's worksheet.

The trade-in question that's worth thousands

Most states deduct your trade-in before applying tax. A $35,000 car with a $15,000 trade in Texas is taxed on $20,000, not $35,000 — a $1,230 difference. California is the major exception: the full purchase price is taxable no matter how large your trade. New Mexico credits trade-ins against its excise tax, and Illinois restored its full trade-in credit for standard passenger vehicles after a $10,000 cap era. If you're deciding between trading in and selling privately, the tax credit is often the deciding factor — private buyers pay you more on paper, but you lose the deduction on the new car.

The states that don't use ordinary sales tax at all

  • Georgia — 7% TAVT. A one-time Title Ad Valorem Tax on fair market value replaces annual vehicle property tax. Current rate set by the state revenue department: 7.0%.
  • North Carolina — 3% highway use tax. Paid when you title the vehicle, in place of sales tax. That's why NC often looks cheap on paper; the annual property tax on vehicles is separate.
  • New Mexico & South Dakota — 4% excise tax. A flat motor vehicle excise instead of the general sales tax. NM credits trade-ins; SD's 4% is the whole bill on the vehicle.
  • South Carolina — $500 cap. The Infrastructure Maintenance Fee is 5%, capped at $500, so a $60,000 truck pays the same $500 as a $12,000 hatchback.
  • Virginia — 4.15% with a $75 minimum. The motor vehicle sales and use tax replaces local sales taxes entirely.
  • Washington — sales tax plus extras. A 0.5% motor vehicle tax rides on top of sales tax (raised from 0.3% in January 2026), and an 8% excise applies to the amount of the price over $100,000 — a $150,000 EV pays $4,000 extra.

Tennessee deserves its own footnote: 7% state tax applies to the full price, but local option tax touches only the first $1,600, and an extra 2.75% state levy covers the $1,600-to-$3,200 band — which maxes at $44. In practice a $35,000 car in Nashville owes roughly 7.2% overall.

Buying out of state, or from a private party?

The tax follows registration. Buy in Oregon to dodge Washington's rates and Washington collects when you title the car at home; most states credit tax paid to another state so you're topping up, not paying twice. Private-party sales are usually taxed at the same rate when you title the car — but several states treat them differently (Illinois uses an age-and-value schedule), and a few exempt casual sales outright. One practical tip applies everywhere: keep the bill of sale. States that tax "fair market value" rather than your stated price will use their own book value if your paperwork looks thin.

Worked example: Texas vs California

Same car, same numbers: $35,000 purchase, $5,000 trade-in. In Texas, taxable base is $30,000, combined rate 8.20% (6.25 state + 1.95 average local): $2,460. In California, the trade-in doesn't count: $35,000 × 8.99% average combined = $3,146.50. Identical cars, $686.50 apart, before you've considered a single fee. Drop the trade-in and Texas rises to $2,870 — still $276.50 under California's bill. Now you know why Californians carefully weigh selling privately versus trading in, and why Texans celebrate their trade-in credit.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much is sales tax on a $35,000 car?

Anywhere from zero to about $3,540. The same $35,000 car owes nothing in Oregon, Montana, New Hampshire or Delaware; $1,050 in North Carolina (3% highway use tax); about $2,450 in Georgia (7% TAVT); $2,870 in Texas at its 8.20% average combined rate; $3,146.50 in California at its 8.99% average; and about $3,538 in Louisiana at the highest average combined rate, 10.11%.

Do you pay sales tax on a car before or after trade-in?

After, in most states — the trade-in value comes off the taxable price. That's why a $35,000 purchase with a $5,000 trade is taxed on $30,000 in Texas but on all $35,000 in California, which doesn't allow the credit. The difference on that example is $410 in Texas; in California the trade-in saves you nothing on tax.

Do I pay car sales tax where I buy or where I live?

Where you register the car. Buying in a no-sales-tax state doesn't dodge anything: register the car in your home state and that state's tax applies. Most states credit tax already paid elsewhere, so you top up the difference rather than double-paying, but you'll handle the paperwork.

Which states don't tax car purchases?

Oregon, Montana, New Hampshire, and Delaware have no general sales tax, and Alaska has no state-level tax (some boroughs add local tax). Fees still apply at registration — title, plates, and in Delaware's case a document fee — but there's no percentage-based sales tax on the purchase.

Are dealer fees and registration costs part of sales tax?

No. Title, registration, and documentation fees are separate line items, though several states include the doc fee in the taxable price. Expect a few hundred dollars in fees on top of tax, varying by state, and EVs often carry extra registration fees replacing fuel-tax revenue.
