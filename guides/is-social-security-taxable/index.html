Is Social Security Taxable?

📄 7 min readUpdated October 16, 2026

Yes, Social Security can be taxable, but often isn't. It comes down to your other income. Below about $25,000 of combined income for a single filer, or $32,000 for a couple, none of your benefits are taxed. Above that, up to 50% and then up to 85% of your benefits get added to taxable income. Nobody pays tax on all of it. Here's how the formula works, what it looks like at different income levels, and what the 2025 law actually changed.

How does the IRS decide if Social Security is taxable?

It starts with a number called provisional income, sometimes called combined income. You won't find it on your tax return. You work it out:

Provisional income = adjusted gross income (not counting Social Security) + tax-exempt interest + half of your Social Security benefits.

Notice the tax-exempt interest. Municipal bond income that's free of federal tax still counts here, which surprises a lot of retirees. Pension payments, traditional IRA and 401(k) withdrawals, wages, interest, dividends and capital gains all count. Roth IRA withdrawals don't.

Then you compare provisional income with two thresholds:

Those thresholds have never been adjusted for inflation. The first tier dates from the 1983 amendments and the second from 1993. When they were set, only higher-income retirees paid anything. Now a modest pension plus an average benefit is often enough to cross them, and more retirees get pulled in every year.

Watch the middle rows. Between about $20,000 and $40,000 of other income, each extra dollar makes up to 85 cents of benefits taxable on top of itself. That stretch is sometimes called the tax torpedo, and it's why a $5,000 IRA withdrawal can cost more tax than you'd expect.

A worked example

Take the single filer with $30,000 of pension and IRA income and $24,000 in benefits:

  1. Provisional income: $30,000 + half of $24,000 = $42,000.
  2. Amount over the first threshold: $42,000 − $25,000 = $17,000.
  3. Amount over the second threshold: $42,000 − $34,000 = $8,000.
  4. 50% tier: half of the $9,000 gap between thresholds = $4,500.
  5. 85% tier: 85% of $8,000 = $6,800.
  6. Total: $4,500 + $6,800 = $11,300. The cap is 85% of $24,000, which is $20,400, so $11,300 stands.

That $11,300 is added to taxable income. It isn't the tax. After the standard deduction, a retiree in the 10% or 12% bracket would owe a fraction of it.

Didn't the 2025 tax law end taxes on Social Security?

No, though you'll see headlines that suggest it. The 2025 law kept the taxation rules above exactly as they were. What it added is a temporary deduction of up to $6,000 for each person 65 or older, $12,000 for a married couple who both qualify, for tax years 2025 through 2028.

It phases out by 6% of income above $75,000 for single filers and $150,000 for joint filers, so it's gone completely at $175,000 and $250,000. You can claim it whether or not you itemize, on top of the extra standard deduction for being 65. For many middle-income retirees it wipes out the tax on their benefits, which is where the headlines came from. But you have to be 65, the benefit itself is still counted, and the deduction expires after 2028 unless Congress extends it.

Do states tax Social Security?

Most don't. For 2026, eight states still tax some benefits: Colorado, Connecticut, Minnesota, Montana, New Mexico, Rhode Island, Utah and Vermont. West Virginia finished phasing out its tax at the start of 2026. Almost all of the eight exempt lower and middle incomes or give older residents a credit, so the tax usually lands only on higher earners. Check your state revenue department's current rules before you plan around it.

How do I pay the tax on my benefits?

Nothing is withheld unless you ask. You've got two options:

  • Voluntary withholding: file Form W-4V with Social Security and choose 7%, 10%, 12% or 22% of each check. Those are the only rates on offer.
  • Quarterly estimated payments: better if your income is lumpy. Our guide to calculating quarterly taxes walks through the safe-harbor rules.

Every January you'll get Form SSA-1099 showing the benefits you received. SSDI follows the same rules. If you got a large lump of back pay in one year, the IRS lets you treat the parts that belong to earlier years as if they'd been paid then, which can lower the taxable amount. Publication 915 has the worksheet.

Can I reduce the tax on my Social Security?

You can't change the formula, but you can change what goes into it. Roth conversions in the years before you claim shift future withdrawals out of provisional income. Spending down a traditional IRA in your 60s, before benefits start, has the same effect. Delaying Social Security to 70 means a bigger check later and more room to draw on savings early. Frequently Asked Questions

At what income is Social Security not taxable?

None of your benefits are taxable if your provisional income, which is your other income plus tax-exempt interest plus half your benefits, is under $25,000 as a single filer or under $32,000 for a married couple filing jointly.

Is Social Security taxed after age 65?

Yes, the same rules apply at every age. From 2025 through 2028, people 65 and older can take an extra deduction of up to $6,000 each, which lowers taxable income but doesn't change how much of the benefit counts as taxable.

Does 85% taxable mean I pay 85% tax?

No. It means up to 85% of your benefit is added to your taxable income. You then pay your normal tax rate on that amount, so the actual tax is usually a small fraction of your benefit.

Is SSDI taxable?

It follows the same rules as retirement benefits. Many SSDI recipients owe nothing because their other income is low. If you received back pay covering earlier years, a lump-sum election can spread it back across those years.

How do I have taxes withheld from Social Security?

File Form W-4V with Social Security and pick 7%, 10%, 12% or 22% of each payment. You can change or stop withholding at any time with a new form.
