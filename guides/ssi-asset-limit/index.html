SSI Asset Limit: What Counts Toward the $2,000 Cap

📄 7 min readUpdated October 26, 2026

SSI lets you keep $2,000 in countable assets, or $3,000 as a couple, and the limit hasn't changed since 1989. Your home, one car and your belongings don't count. Bank balances, investments and your own retirement account do. Here's what counts, how the first-of-the-month test works, and the legal ways to stay under, including ABLE accounts, which far more people qualify for since January 2026.

What is the SSI asset limit in 2026?

$2,000 for one person and $3,000 for a married couple. SSA calls these "countable resources," and the test is simple: if what you own that counts is over the limit at the first moment of a month, you get no SSI for that month. There's no partial check for being $50 over.

The limit isn't adjusted for inflation. Congress set it in steps during the 1980s and it hasn't moved since January 1989:

What counts toward the $2,000?

Anything you own that could be turned into cash and used for food or shelter, unless a specific rule excludes it. SSA treats something as liquid if it can be converted within 20 days. Here's how the common items fall:

What doesn't count?

The big exclusions are your home, as long as you live there, one vehicle used for getting around, and your belongings, all at any value. A $40,000 car and a paid-off house don't affect SSI at all, while $2,100 in savings does. The smaller exclusions in the table, like a $1,500 burial fund in its own labeled account, are the legal ways to hold a little extra. Jewelry you wear is excluded, but coins or collectibles held as an investment count.

How does the first-of-the-month rule work?

SSA looks at what you have at 12:00 a.m. on the 1st. Money you receive during a month is income in that month, not a resource. Whatever is left at the start of the next month becomes a resource.

So if your checking account sits at $1,700 on the 1st, your SSDI of $600 arrives on the 3rd, and you spend $500 during the month, you start next month at $1,800. That's under the limit. A month where you don't spend the check pushes you to $2,300, and you lose that month's SSI even though nothing about your life changed. People on concurrent SSDI and SSI run into this more than anyone, because their income lands on different days.

The fix is timing. Pay rent or a big purchase before the 1st, and if you go over by accident, spending down below $2,000 before the next 1st restores eligibility for that month.

How do ABLE accounts help?

An ABLE account is a tax-advantaged savings account for people whose disability began before age 46. That age limit was 26 until January 1, 2026, which is why far more people qualify this year. SSI ignores the first $100,000 in it.

In 2026, total contributions from everyone (you, family, friends) are capped at $20,000 a year. If you work and nobody's putting money into a workplace retirement plan for you, you can add up to $15,960 more from your own earnings in the continental US (or your pay for the year, if that's less). Withdrawals for qualified disability expenses (housing, transportation, health care, education and basic living costs among them) don't count as income.

One catch: housing money you withdraw and still hold at the start of the next month counts as a resource, so take rent out in the month you pay it. Above $100,000, SSA suspends SSI rather than ending it, and Medicaid continues.

What happens if I give money away to get under the limit?

Don't. Giving away a resource, or selling it for less than it's worth, triggers a period of ineligibility of up to 36 months. SSA divides the uncompensated value by the monthly SSI rate (plus any state supplement it administers), rounds down, and that's how many months you lose, starting the month after the transfer. How do you spend down legally?

Spending on yourself at fair prices is always allowed: paying down debt, prepaying bills, household goods, a computer, repairs to your home or your one car, a burial fund or plot, or moving savings into an ABLE account.

If property you can't sell quickly puts you over, ask about conditional benefits: you sign an agreement to sell and get SSI while you try (9 months for real estate, 3 for personal property), then repay it from the proceeds.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the SSI resource limit for 2026?

$2,000 for an individual and $3,000 for a couple. It has been the same since 1989 and isn't adjusted for inflation. Only countable resources are included, so your home and one car don't count.

Does a 401(k) or IRA count as a resource for SSI?

Yes, if it's yours. SSA counts what you could withdraw after any early-withdrawal penalty. An ineligible spouse's IRA or work pension is excluded when SSA deems the spouse's resources to you.

Can I have $5,000 in an ABLE account and still get SSI?

Yes. SSI ignores the first $100,000 in an ABLE account. Your disability must have started before age 46, and in 2026 total contributions are limited to $20,000 a year, plus up to $15,960 of your own earnings if you work.

What happens if I go over $2,000 for one month?

You're ineligible for SSI for any month where countable resources are over the limit at the first moment of the month. SSA may treat the payment as an overpayment. Spend down to under $2,000 before the next 1st and you're eligible again that month.

Does SSDI back pay count against the SSI asset limit?

Not right away. Unspent retroactive SSDI or SSI payments are excluded for 9 months after the month you receive them, as long as you can identify them separately. After that, whatever is left counts.
