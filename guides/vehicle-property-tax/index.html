Which States Tax Your Car Every Year? (2026 Car Tax by State)

🏛️ ~12K searches/mo💰 CPC: $5⏱️ 8 min read

Buying the car is only the first tax. About two dozen states send a bill every single year you own it, as an excise, a personal property tax, or a license fee tied to value. On a $25,000 car, year one runs about $213 in North Carolina, $563 in Massachusetts, and $600 in Maine. Here's how each formula works, which ones fade with age, which never do, and how to check your real number.

What is a vehicle property tax?

It's a recurring tax on owning the car, billed by your town, county, or DMV, usually due alongside registration renewal. Unlike sales tax (paid once, at purchase) or the registration fee itself (a flat administrative charge), the property tax scales with what the car is worth — so it's largest on new cars and, depending on the state, either shrinks on a schedule or follows the car's market value down.

The names vary: Massachusetts and Maine call it excise, Arizona calls it a vehicle license tax (VLT), Virginia calls it personal property tax, North Carolina folds it into your registration. The mechanics are what matter, and they cluster into three families.

Which formulas fade with age, and which never stop?

Three of the nine taxes above run off the original price on a fixed schedule: Massachusetts drops to 10% of MSRP in year five and stays there ($70 a year on our $28,000 example), Maine steps from $24 to $3 per $1,000 by year seven, and Arizona cuts the taxable base 16.25% each year, compounding until a 20%-of-base floor. Buy a car in those states, hold it a decade, and the tax shrinks to a few hundred dollars a year or less.

The others tax current value. Virginia, Missouri, Kansas and North Carolina all track what the car is worth today, so the bill follows depreciation curves but never reaches zero. Same car, second decade of ownership: worth roughly $8,000, still billing $336 a year in a $4.20 Virginia locality before relief.

What about states with no annual car tax?

Texas, Florida, Georgia, Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Illinois, Ohio and most of the South and Mountain West outside the list above charge nothing recurring beyond standard registration fees, which are flat. They collect at purchase instead: sales tax on the transaction, or in Georgia's case a one-time title ad valorem tax of roughly 7% that replaced both sales tax and the old birthday tax. California's 0.65% vehicle license fee rides inside your registration bill — value-based, annual, but never itemized as a separate tax.

The takeaway for cross-state movers: the same car can cost $70 a year in year six (Massachusetts) or $500-plus (Virginia, Missouri). If you're registering in a new state, check both the annual tax and the one-time fees before you pick a registration date — for more on that side, see the vehicle registration cost calculator with its 50-state fee table, and the car sales tax calculator for the purchase-time side.

How do you lower a vehicle property tax bill?

  • Check the assessed value. NADA-based assessments lag the market and overstate clunkers in a falling market. Most states have a short appeal window each year; bring comparable listings.
  • Claim every exemption. Veterans, disabled, and in some states senior or low-income exemptions apply to vehicle taxes, not just real estate. Many Connecticut towns set their own exemption levels for veterans and seniors.
  • Watch the garaged-address rules. These taxes follow where the car is garaged, not where you work. Registering at a vacation home's address to catch a lower mill rate is fraud; knowing that your county's rate is 20% lower than the next one over is not.
  • Sell before the schedule flattens... or after it does. In schedule states the tax collapse at year four or five is automatic; in value states the tax argument for selling a beater is that the bill has stopped shrinking but the repairs haven't.
  • Deduct it if you itemize. Vehicle personal property tax counts toward the SALT deduction, capped at $40,000 across state and local taxes in 2025 (joint; phases down at high incomes). General information, not tax advice.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do all states have personal property tax on vehicles?

No. About two dozen states bill a recurring tax on cars, led by Massachusetts, Rhode Island, Connecticut, Maine, Virginia, Missouri, North Carolina, Kansas and Arizona. The rest collect their share once at purchase as sales tax or a title fee. California bills a 0.65% vehicle license fee inside registration, which is a value-based tax in practice.

How much is the annual car tax on a $25,000 vehicle?

In the major annual-tax states, roughly $525-$600 in year one for most formulas: about $563 in Massachusetts, $600 in Maine, $525 in Connecticut at 30 mills, $578 in Virginia after relief, $583 in Missouri, and $560 in Arizona. Rhode Island runs highest at about $1,380 because its $6 per $100 rate has only a modest exemption in many towns. Kansas ($275) and North Carolina ($213) run lightest.

Why does my car tax go down every year in some states but not others?

It depends on what's being taxed. Massachusetts, Maine and Arizona tax a depreciation schedule off the original price, so the bill falls predictably every year and eventually bottoms out. Virginia, Missouri, Kansas, Rhode Island and North Carolina tax current market value, so the bill tracks what the car is worth as it ages and never quite reaches zero.

Is vehicle property tax deductible on federal taxes?

Often, if you itemize: annual personal property taxes based on vehicle value count toward the state and local tax (SALT) deduction, capped at $40,000 for joint filers in 2025, phasing down at high incomes. One-time purchase taxes follow different rules. This is general information, not tax advice — confirm with a preparer.
