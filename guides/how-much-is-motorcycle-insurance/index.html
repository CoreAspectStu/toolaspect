How Much Is Motorcycle Insurance? 2025 Average Costs

💰 CPC: $16⏱️ 7 min read🏍️ 51 states & DC

Motorcycle insurance averages about $585 a year for full coverage, roughly $49 a month for a 45- to 54-year-old rider with a clean record on a mid-size cruiser (typical published 2025 averages). Liability-only runs about $222 a year. But the spread is enormous: North Dakota riders pay $330 while Louisianans pay $1,080, and a 22-year-old on a sportbike can top $3,000.

What does the average rider actually pay?

The $585 figure describes a benchmark policy: liability at state minimums plus comprehensive and collision on a mid-size cruiser. That's the "full coverage" most lenders require on financed bikes. Strip it to liability-only and the national average falls to about $222, which is why paid-off bikes are so cheap to keep on the road.

What makes motorcycle insurance cost more?

  1. Rider age. Under 25 pays about 1.9× the base rate. The penalty fades to 1.35× at 25–34, disappears around 45, and flips into a 10% discount at 55–64.
  2. Bike type. Sportbikes and supersports carry roughly a 65% surcharge for crash frequency and theft. Standards run 5% under a cruiser, and scooters pay about half.
  3. Engine size. Modest: under 500cc saves 15%, over 1,100cc adds 10%. Bike type matters far more than displacement.
  4. Record. One violation or claim in three years: 1.28×. Two or more: 1.60×. On a sportbike those stack into four-figure premiums fast.
  5. State and garaging zip. Litigation climates, no-fault laws, and theft rates explain the 3× spread between Louisiana and North Dakota.

A worked example: the sportbike penalty in real dollars

A 30-year-old Texas rider with one speeding ticket prices full coverage on a 600cc sportbike, MSF course completed. Start from Texas's $780 baseline. Age 25–34: ×1.35 = $1,053. Sportbike with a 500–749cc engine: ×1.65 × 0.95 = $1,650.63. The ticket: ×1.28 = $2,112.80. Course discount: ×0.93 lands at $1,965 a year, or $163.74 a month.

Same rider, same state, same record, but on a 650cc standard: $780 × 1.35 × 0.95 × 0.95 × 1.28 × 0.93 = $1,131. The bike choice alone is worth $834 a year. And a 52-year-old Ohioan with a clean record bundling a cruiser at liability-plus-comprehensive pays just $305, or $25.38 a month.

Five ways to pay less

  1. Take the MSF course. About 7% off for three years at most carriers, and it makes you measurably safer in the first six months, when new riders are most likely to crash.
  2. Bundle with your auto policy. Typically 10% off both. Know what your car coverage should cost first, the car insurance estimator gives you the baseline.
  3. Raise comprehensive and collision deductibles. Going from $250 to $1,000 on a bike you could self-insure trims the physical damage portion noticeably.
  4. Ask about lay-up coverage. If the bike sits from November to March, seasonal policies drop comprehensive-only for those months. Common in snow states.
  5. Quote before you buy the bike. Insurance is part of the purchase price. A $6,000 used standard can cost less to insure for five years than a $12,000 supersport costs for two.

One gap worth closing while you're at it: motorcycle liability limits are often state minimums, which can be as low as $15,000 or $25,000 for property damage. A single at-fault crash into a late-model truck can outrun that. An umbrella policy adds $1 million of liability for roughly $150 to $300 a year and typically covers you on two wheels too.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is motorcycle insurance cheaper than car insurance?

Liability-only usually is, because a bike does less damage to other people's property than a car. Full coverage can rival car prices because riders are injured far more often per mile and popular models get stolen at high rates. A 45-year-old with a clean record pays about $585 a year for full motorcycle coverage versus roughly $2,500 for a new car.

What state has the cheapest motorcycle insurance?

The Plains and upper Midwest. North Dakota ($330 a year), South Dakota ($340), Montana ($380), and Iowa ($400) sit at the bottom of the table. The most expensive states are Louisiana ($1,080), Florida ($950), California ($830), New York ($820), and Michigan ($870), driven by litigation climates, no-fault systems, and theft.

How much does an MSF course save on motorcycle insurance?

Most insurers knock about 7% off for a completed Motorcycle Safety Foundation basic rider course, and the discount usually sticks for three years. Stack it with a bundling discount (another 10%) and a $585 average policy drops to about $490.

Do I have to carry motorcycle insurance in Florida?

Florida is the notable exception to state insurance mandates: riders there aren't required to carry motorcycle liability insurance. But riders 21 and older who ride without a helmet must carry at least $10,000 of medical expense benefits, and an uninsured at-fault crash can still cost you everything. Skipping coverage to save $60 a month is a bad trade.
