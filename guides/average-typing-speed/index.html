What Is the Average Typing Speed?

⌨️ 7 min readUpdated Aug 2026

The average adult types about 40 words per minute, with big typing-test samples clustering between 38 and 42 WPM at 90 to 95 percent accuracy. For context, that's about six times slower than average reading speed (238 WPM) and roughly a third of conversational speaking pace (130 to 150 WPM). Office jobs typically ask for 50 to 60 WPM, professional typists run 65 to 80, and the standing record is 216 WPM on a typewriter from 1946.

How WPM is actually measured

Typing tests don't count dictionary words. The industry standard treats every five characters, spaces included, as one word, because average English words run four to five characters. Gross WPM is everything you typed divided by five divided by minutes. Net WPM counts only correct characters. Accuracy is correct characters over total characters.

Here's the arithmetic on a real run. You type for 60 seconds and log 348 characters, 340 of them correct. Gross WPM: 348 ÷ 5 ÷ 1 = 69.6. Accuracy: 340 ÷ 348 = 97.7%. Net WPM: 340 ÷ 5 ÷ 1 = 68. Those eight typos cost 1.6 WPM, which is why accuracy shows up on every serious test result.

Two records get confused. Stella Pajunas hit 216 WPM in a one-minute burst in 1946. Barbara Blackburn, the best-known sustained typist, averaged around 150 WPM with peaks near 212 on a Dvorak keyboard in the 1980s. Court reporters "type" over 225 WPM, but on stenotype machines that capture whole syllables per stroke, which is a different instrument, not a different skill ceiling.

Why typing is slower than you think

Typing is a bottleneck your brain works around. Reading averages 238 WPM for non-fiction, so your eyes consume text about six times faster than your fingers produce it. Speech runs 130 to 150 WPM, which is why dictation and voice memos beat keyboards for raw capture, and why lecture handwriting (13 to 20 WPM) forces students to summarize instead of transcribe.

The bottleneck is mechanical. Every keystroke is a finger round trip of roughly 100 milliseconds or more, and English packs about five characters per word. Faster typists don't move their fingers dramatically faster; they move them shorter distances, keep hands parked on the home row, and waste fewer strokes on errors and corrections.

What employers actually ask for

Most clerical, administrative, and customer-service postings ask for 40 to 60 WPM. Data entry roles often list 60 to 80 WPM with 95 percent or better accuracy, and transcription work usually means 70-plus with 98 percent accuracy. Above those numbers, extra speed stops mattering for hiring and starts mattering only for your own throughput.

If a job requires a certified score, the test is usually one to five minutes of standard prose, scored exactly like the formula above. Practicing the test format itself, forward-only typing with mistakes counted, is worth several WPM versus walking in cold.

How to get faster (without losing accuracy)

  • Slow down first. Practice at a pace where you almost never miss. Speed built on errors doesn't survive a real test, since net WPM subtracts them.
  • Keep eyes on the text. Looking down costs a quarter second per glance, and glances add up. Touch typing is mostly about never looking.
  • Short daily drills. Twenty minutes a day beats two hours on Saturday. Motor learning consolidates between sessions.
  • Type real material. Articles and emails, not random letter drills. Real prose has English's actual rhythm of common words and punctuation.
  • Fix your posture and keyboard. Wrists floating, elbows near 90 degrees. A shallow-profile keyboard reduces finger travel distance for free.

Expect two to three weeks to relearn key positions if you're converting from hunt-and-peck, a couple of months to a comfortable 50 WPM, and six months or more of deliberate practice to pass 70. The people who plateau are almost always the ones practicing at speeds where their accuracy collapses.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good typing speed?

For everyday computer work, 50 to 60 WPM is solid and clears the requirement on most clerical job postings. Above 65 WPM you out-type most office workers, and professional typists sit in the 65 to 80 WPM band. Speed only counts with accuracy attached: 60 WPM at 97 percent accuracy beats 80 WPM at 88 percent in any hiring test.

How many words per minute does the average person type?

About 40 WPM. Large typing-test samples consistently put the average adult between 38 and 42 WPM, at roughly 90 to 95 percent accuracy. Two-finger hunt-and-peck typists average around 27 WPM and plateau there, while trained touch typists reach 60 WPM or more.

Is typing faster than writing by hand?

Yes, roughly three times faster. Handwriting averages 13 to 20 words per minute in lecture-note studies, while keyboard typing averages about 40 WPM. Speech is quicker still at 130 to 150 WPM, which is why dictation wins for raw capture, though hands stay better at symbols, code, and structure.

How long does it take to learn touch typing?

Most people learn the key positions in two to three weeks of 20-minute daily practice, and reach a comfortable 40 to 50 WPM within a couple of months. Getting past 60 WPM usually takes several more months of deliberate practice at high accuracy. The biggest gains come from slowing down until errors nearly stop, then letting speed rebuild.
