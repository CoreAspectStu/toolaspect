Can You Work While Collecting Social Security?

📄 7 min readUpdated October 13, 2026

Yes. Anyone can work and collect Social Security retirement benefits at the same time. The catch only applies before your full retirement age (FRA). In 2026, if you're under FRA all year, Social Security withholds $1 of benefits for every $2 you earn above $24,480. In the year you reach FRA, it's $1 for every $3 above $65,160, and only earnings before your FRA month count. From your FRA month on, there's no limit at all. Withheld money isn't lost, either: your monthly check is raised at FRA to make up for it. To see what claiming at 62, 67 or 70 is worth in the first place, try our Social Security Calculator.

What are the 2026 earnings limits?

Social Security calls this the retirement earnings test. The limits rise each year with the national average wage, not with the cost-of-living adjustment.

For comparison, the 2025 limits were $23,400 and $62,160. Social Security usually announces the next year's figures in October alongside the COLA, so the 2027 numbers are due any day now.

Your FRA depends on your birth year. It's 67 if you were born in 1960 or later. If you were born in 1959, it's 66 and 10 months, which means most people born that year hit it in late 2025 or during 2026.

In the year you reach FRA, the hit is much smaller. Earn $75,000 in the months before your birthday month and you're $9,840 over the $65,160 limit, so $3,280 is withheld. At $85,000 it's $6,613. At $100,000, $11,613.

If the withholding is bigger than your total benefit for the year, you get nothing for that year. At that point it usually makes sense to pause or delay your claim. You can withdraw an application within 12 months of starting benefits, but you have to pay back everything you received.

How does the withholding actually work?

Social Security doesn't shave a little off each check. It holds back whole monthly payments, starting at the beginning of the year, until it has withheld enough. Then the checks start again.

Say you're 63, your benefit is $1,800 a month and you expect to earn $34,480 in 2026. That's $5,000 to withhold. Social Security keeps your January, February and March checks ($5,400), then pays you normally from April. The extra $400 comes back to you the following year, after your actual earnings are known.

That's why your earnings estimate matters. Tell Social Security when you start claiming, and update it if your pay changes. If you earn more than you said, you'll get an overpayment notice and have to repay. Earn less, and you'll be owed money.

What counts as earnings?

Only pay from work. That means gross wages from a job (before taxes and deductions, including your own 401(k) contributions) and net earnings if you're self-employed. These don't count:

  • Pensions and annuities
  • IRA, 401(k) and other retirement account withdrawals
  • Interest, dividends and capital gains
  • Rental income, unless real estate is your business
  • Other government benefits, including VA benefits

So someone living on a pension and investment income can claim at 62 with no withholding at all, no matter how large that income is.

What is the special rule for the first year?

Most people retire partway through a year, often after earning well over the annual limit. Counting all of that would wipe out the first year's benefits, so there's a monthly test for the first year you get benefits. You can get a full check for any month you're a "non-service month": you earn $2,040 or less in wages that month (2026) and don't do substantial work in your own business. The limit is $5,430 a month if you reach FRA that year.

Example: you retire on June 30, 2026 at 64 after earning $50,000, start benefits in July, and do no paid work after that. The annual math says $12,760 should be withheld, but the monthly rule lets you collect a full check for July through December.

Do I get the withheld benefits back?

Yes, just not as a lump sum. When you reach FRA, Social Security recalculates your benefit as if you'd claimed later, giving you credit for every month a check was withheld. If you claimed at 62 and had 12 months of checks withheld before FRA, your benefit is reset as though you'd claimed at 63. The higher check then lasts for life, so if you live into your 80s, you usually come out close to even.

One wrinkle: if your spouse or children get benefits on your record, your excess earnings can reduce their checks too. Their own earnings only affect their own benefits.

Will working make my benefits taxable?

It might, and that's a separate rule from the earnings test. Up to 85% of benefits become federally taxable once your "combined income" (adjusted gross income plus nontaxable interest plus half your benefits) passes $34,000 for a single filer or $44,000 for a married couple filing jointly. Up to 50% is taxable above $25,000 single or $32,000 joint. Those thresholds aren't indexed for inflation, so more retirees cross them every year. From 2025 through 2028, people 65 and older also get an extra $6,000 deduction, which phases out at higher incomes. Frequently Asked Questions

How much can I earn while collecting Social Security in 2026?

If you are under full retirement age all year, $24,480. Above that, $1 of benefits is withheld for every $2 you earn. In the year you reach full retirement age the limit is $65,160, with $1 withheld for every $3, and only earnings before your FRA month count.

Is there an earnings limit after full retirement age?

No. Starting with the month you reach full retirement age, you can earn any amount and your Social Security benefit isn't reduced.

Do pensions or 401(k) withdrawals count toward the earnings limit?

No. Only wages from a job and net self-employment earnings count. Pensions, annuities, retirement account withdrawals, interest, dividends, capital gains and other government benefits don't.

Do I lose the benefits Social Security withholds?

No. At full retirement age your benefit is recalculated to credit the months that were withheld, so your monthly check goes up permanently.

What is the monthly earnings limit for 2026?

In your first year of benefits, you can get a full check for any month you earn $2,040 or less and don't do substantial self-employment work. It's $5,430 a month if you reach full retirement age in 2026.
