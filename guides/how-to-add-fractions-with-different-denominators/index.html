How to Add Fractions With Different Denominators

⏱️ 5 min readUpdated September 24, 2026

To add fractions with different denominators, rewrite them so the bottoms match, then add the tops. For 1/6 + 1/4, the smallest shared denominator is 12, so you get 2/12 + 3/12 = 5/12. The same trick handles subtraction. Below are the steps, a quick way to find the least common denominator, mixed numbers, and the rules for multiplying and dividing, which are actually easier.

Why do fractions need a common denominator?

The denominator tells you what size the pieces are. One-sixth of a pizza and one-quarter of a pizza are different-sized slices, so "1 + 1" doesn't give you two of anything useful. Before you can add them, you have to cut both into the same size of piece. Twelfths work here: 1/6 is 2/12 and 1/4 is 3/12, so together you have 5/12.

That's the whole idea. Rewrite each fraction so the bottoms match, then add or subtract the tops.

How do you add fractions with different denominators?

  1. Find a common denominator. The least common multiple (LCM) of the two denominators is the smallest one that works. For 6 and 4, that's 12.
  2. Convert each fraction. Ask what you multiply the old denominator by to reach the new one, then multiply the numerator by the same number. 6 × 2 = 12, so 1/6 becomes 2/12. 4 × 3 = 12, so 1/4 becomes 3/12.
  3. Add the numerators. 2 + 3 = 5. The denominator stays 12.
  4. Simplify. Divide top and bottom by their greatest common factor. 5 and 12 share nothing but 1, so 5/12 is final.

Subtraction is identical except for step 3. For 5/8 − 1/6, the LCM of 8 and 6 is 24, so you get 15/24 − 4/24 = 11/24.

How do you find the least common denominator?

For small numbers, list the multiples of the larger denominator until you hit one the smaller denominator divides into. Multiples of 18 are 18, 36, 54... and 12 goes into 36, so 36 is the LCM of 12 and 18.

For bigger numbers, prime factors are quicker. Write each denominator as primes and take the highest power of every prime that appears. 12 = 2² × 3 and 18 = 2 × 3², so the LCM is 2² × 3² = 36. The factoring calculator gives you the prime breakdown if you'd rather not do it by hand, and our guide to finding prime factorization walks through the method.

You can always skip the LCM and just multiply the two denominators together. It works every time, but the numbers get bigger than they need to be and you'll have more simplifying to do at the end:

When the denominators share no factors, like 3 and 5, both approaches give the same number. When they do share factors, the LCM keeps things much smaller. 7/12 + 5/18 over 216 is 126/216 + 60/216 = 186/216, which you then have to reduce by 6. Over 36 it's just 21/36 + 10/36 = 31/36.

How do you add mixed numbers?

You've got two options. The first is to add the whole numbers and the fractions separately, then tidy up. Say you're adding two board lengths, 3 5/16 inches and 1 7/8 inches:

  • Whole numbers: 3 + 1 = 4
  • Fractions: 5/16 + 7/8 = 5/16 + 14/16 = 19/16
  • 19/16 is more than one, so carry it: 19/16 = 1 3/16
  • Total: 4 + 1 3/16 = 5 3/16 inches

The second option is to convert both to improper fractions first. 3 5/16 is (3 × 16 + 5)/16 = 53/16, and 1 7/8 is 15/8 = 30/16. Add them to get 83/16, which is 5 3/16. Same answer. The improper route is easier with subtraction, because you never have to borrow from the whole number.

How do you multiply and divide fractions?

Good news: neither needs a common denominator.

To multiply, multiply straight across. 3/4 × 2/9 = 6/36, which simplifies to 1/6. You can save a step by cancelling first: the 3 on top and the 9 on the bottom share a 3, and the 2 and the 4 share a 2, leaving 1/2 × 1/3 = 1/6.

To divide, flip the second fraction and multiply. 3/4 ÷ 1/8 becomes 3/4 × 8/1 = 24/4 = 6. That reads naturally, too: there are six eighths in three quarters.

What mistakes do people make with fractions?

  • Adding the denominators. 1/2 + 1/3 is not 2/5. Two-fifths is less than one-half, so that can't be right. The answer is 5/6.
  • Changing only the denominator. If you turn sixths into twelfths, the numerator has to double too. 1/6 is 2/12, not 1/12.
  • Forgetting to simplify. 6/36 is correct but unfinished. Most teachers and tests expect lowest terms.
  • Flipping the wrong fraction when dividing. Flip the one you're dividing by, the second one. Keep the first as it is.

A quick sanity check catches most of these. Estimate with decimals first: 1/6 is about 0.17 and 1/4 is 0.25, so the sum should be a bit over 0.4. 5/12 is 0.417, which fits.

The bottom line

To add or subtract fractions with different denominators, rewrite both over the least common denominator, combine the numerators, and simplify. Multiplication goes straight across, and division flips the second fraction first. Estimate with decimals before you commit to an answer.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you add fractions with different denominators?

Find the least common denominator, rewrite each fraction over it by multiplying top and bottom by the same number, add the numerators, and simplify. For 1/6 + 1/4, use 12: 2/12 + 3/12 = 5/12.

What is 1/2 + 1/3?

5/6. Over a common denominator of 6, one-half is 3/6 and one-third is 2/6, and 3 + 2 = 5.

Can you just multiply the denominators together?

Yes. Multiplying the denominators always gives a common denominator, but it's often bigger than it needs to be, so you'll have to simplify more at the end. The least common multiple keeps the numbers small.

Do you need a common denominator to multiply fractions?

No. Multiply the numerators together and the denominators together, then simplify. 3/4 × 2/9 = 6/36 = 1/6.

How do you subtract mixed numbers?

The simplest way is to convert both to improper fractions, subtract over a common denominator, and convert back. For 5 1/4 − 2 2/3, that's 21/4 − 8/3 = 63/12 − 32/12 = 31/12, or 2 7/12.
