Got an SSDI Overpayment Notice? What to Do Next

📄 7 min readUpdated October 21, 2026

An SSDI overpayment notice means SSA thinks it paid you money you weren't due, and since April 2025 its default is to take half of every check until it's repaid. You can challenge the amount, ask for the debt to be waived, or negotiate a smaller payment. Here's how each one works and how fast you need to move.

What is an SSDI overpayment?

It's money SSA says it paid you that you weren't due. The notice will give a total, the months it covers and a reason. You have real options before a cent comes out of your check. What you can't do is ignore it. Silence is what triggers the harshest collection rate.

Since notices dated April 25, 2025, SSA's default for Social Security and SSDI overpayments is to withhold 50% of your monthly benefit until the debt's repaid. It was 10% for about a year before that, and 100% before that. SSI overpayments still default to 10%. Withholding usually starts about three months after the notice, which leaves at least 60 days to respond.

Why did SSA say you were overpaid?

The reason on the notice decides which fix fits, so read it first. These are the common ones:

Should you appeal or ask for a waiver?

They're different requests, and you can make both.

  • Reconsideration (Form SSA-561) says "this is wrong." The months are wrong, the earnings figure is wrong, or you weren't overpaid at all. You have 60 days from receiving the notice, and SSA treats it as received five days after the date on it unless you show otherwise.
  • Waiver (Form SSA-632) says "the amount may be right, but I shouldn't have to repay it." There's no deadline for a waiver, though timing still matters for withholding.

Either way, if you ask within 30 days of getting the notice, SSA won't start withholding while it decides. Ask later and it still stops collecting once it has your request, but money already withheld isn't automatically handed back.

Who qualifies for an overpayment waiver?

You have to clear two tests. First, you weren't at fault: you didn't give SSA wrong information, you reported what you were supposed to, and you didn't have reason to know the payment was wrong. Second, repaying would either defeat the purpose of the program, meaning you need your income for ordinary living costs like rent, food and medicine, or be against equity and good conscience, meaning you relied on the money and gave something up because of it.

Fault is where most waivers are won or lost. Proof that you reported your work on time, such as an app confirmation, a fax receipt, a letter or the name of the person you spoke to, can be decisive. The SSA-632 also asks for a full budget, so add up income and expenses honestly. SSA can't deny a waiver without first offering you a personal conference to explain your side.

What if you can't afford the payments?

Ask for a lower rate. Form SSA-634 lets you propose a monthly amount based on your actual income and expenses, and SSA will often agree to a figure you can live with. It's the right move when the overpayment is valid and you were at fault, so a waiver isn't realistic. You can also repay in full or in a lump sum to end it.

If you're no longer on benefits, SSA can't withhold from a check, so it sends repayment requests instead. Unpaid debts can be sent to the Treasury Offset Program, which takes federal tax refunds and some other federal payments.

What should you do this week?

  • Write down the dates. The notice date, the day you got it, day 30 and day 60.
  • Get the overpayment calculation. Ask SSA for the month-by-month breakdown. Mistakes in which months were counted are common.
  • Collect your proof. Pay stubs, reporting receipts, award letters for workers' comp, school records for a child.
  • File the right form, in writing. Keep copies, and get a receipt if you file in person. Free help is available from legal aid and from protection and advocacy agencies.
  • Check any back pay. If you're also owed past-due benefits, SSA can net one against the other. The SSDI back pay calculator shows roughly what's owed, and the Social Security calculator covers retirement benefits if you're close to full retirement age.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does SSA take out for an SSDI overpayment?

For notices dated April 25, 2025 or later, the default is 50% of your monthly Social Security or SSDI benefit until the debt is repaid. SSI overpayments default to 10%. You can ask for a lower amount with Form SSA-634.

How long do I have to appeal an SSDI overpayment?

60 days from when you receive the notice to ask for reconsideration on Form SSA-561. SSA assumes you got it five days after the date on the notice. A waiver request on Form SSA-632 has no deadline, but filing within 30 days stops withholding from starting while SSA decides.

What is the difference between an appeal and a waiver?

An appeal says the overpayment is wrong, in amount or entirely. A waiver accepts it may be right but asks SSA not to collect because you weren't at fault and repaying would cause hardship or be unfair. You can file both.

Can SSA take my tax refund for an overpayment?

Yes, if you're not getting benefits it can withhold from and the debt isn't repaid or under an agreement. SSA can refer it to the Treasury Offset Program, which can take federal tax refunds and some other federal payments.

Do I still have to repay if the overpayment was SSA's mistake?

Not necessarily. An SSA error usually means you weren't at fault, which is the first test for a waiver. You still have to show that repaying would leave you unable to meet ordinary living costs or would be unfair, so file Form SSA-632 with a full budget.
