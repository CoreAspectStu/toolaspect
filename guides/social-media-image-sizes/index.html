How do you convert any photo to any of these sizes?

Crop first, then resize. Say you shot a 4000×3000 photo (a 12MP 4:3 frame) and need an Instagram square. The biggest square inside a 4000×3000 frame is 3000×3000 — cropping to 1:1 discards 1000 pixels of width. Scaling the remaining 3000×3000 down to 1080×1080 keeps 1080 ÷ 3000 = 36% of the linear resolution, which is 12.96% of the pixels, and looks perfect because Instagram only ever displays 1080 wide.

A LinkedIn banner from the same photo is more violent: the 4:1 frame means a 3000-pixel-wide crop stands just 750 pixels tall (3000 ÷ 4). Scaled to 1584×396 you keep 27.9% of the crop's pixels. Wide frames eat vertical context, so photos with one strong horizontal band survive banners; portraits don't.

Why does Instagram crop my photos?

The feed renders between 1.91:1 (wide) and 4:5 (tall). Outside that band, Instagram crops — and it crops centered, deciding for you which edge disappears. Panoramas and full-length portraits suffer most. Pre-cropping to 4:5 means you choose what's lost. Stories are the other trap: the frame is 1080×1920, but profile icons, the reply field, and the username bar overlay roughly the top 250 and bottom 310 pixels, so keep text and faces in the middle band.

JPG or PNG — and what quality?

  • Photos → JPG at quality 0.9. Smallest file that survives a platform's re-compression without visible loss. Every platform re-encodes your upload; giving it a 0.9 JPG means one extra generation of artifacts at most.
  • Text, logos, flat-color graphics → PNG. JPEG mushes sharp edges between flat colors; PNG keeps them crisp and costs the extra megabytes happily.
  • Screenshots of UI → PNG for the same reason. If the target is a thumbnail slot with a 2MB cap, JPG at 0.9 is the safe default.
  • Never upscale to hit a number. A 750px image stretched to 1280px is softer than the original at 750px. Start from the biggest source you have.

What about DPI?

Nothing. DPI is a print instruction — pixels per inch — and no phone or browser reads it. A 1280×720 image is 1280×720 whether its metadata says 72 or 300. The only time DPI matters is paper: at 300 DPI, that YouTube thumbnail prints 4.27 × 2.4 inches. For everything in this guide, count pixels and ignore the DPI field your export dialog keeps asking about.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size should I make a YouTube thumbnail?

1280 by 720 pixels, 16:9, at least 640 wide, under 2MB, as JPG, PNG, GIF, or WebP. That matches the player frame exactly, so nothing gets letterboxed or cropped.

What's the best Instagram post size in 2026?

1080 by 1350 pixels (4:5 portrait) takes the most vertical feed real estate of any allowed ratio, which is why most creators default to it. 1080 by 1080 square still works everywhere; the feed accepts anything between 1.91:1 landscape and 4:5 portrait.

Why does my LinkedIn banner look cropped on mobile?

LinkedIn banners are 1584 by 396 (4:1) and mobile apps show a narrower horizontal slice of the center. Keep logos and text inside the middle 1200 or so pixels and let the edges be background.

Should I export JPG or PNG for social media?

JPG at quality 0.9 for photos — smaller files upload faster and platforms re-compress everything anyway. PNG for graphics with sharp text, logos, or flat colors, where JPEG's blocky artifacts would show along edges.

Does resizing an image to a bigger size make it higher quality?

No. Upscaling stretches existing pixels across more space; it cannot invent detail. Always start from the largest original you have and scale down to the target — downscaling preserves sharpness, upscaling softens.
