How Much Alcohol for a Wedding? The Bar Math

🥂 ~15K searches/mo (est.)💰 CPC: $2–5⏱️ 7 min read

Bar planning has one load-bearing rule — one drink per guest per hour — and everything else is arithmetic: split the total across beer, wine, and liquor, then divide by servings per bottle. Here's the full calculation with real numbers, plus the adjustments that actually matter (daytime weddings, non-drinkers, the champagne toast).

The rule that runs every wedding bar

Bartenders and caterers plan on one alcoholic drink per guest for every hour the bar is open. It sounds low and isn't: averaged across a whole reception, it already includes the guests drinking three and the guests drinking zero. A 4-hour reception for 100 people is 400 drinks. For a crowd that leans boozy, use the generous variant — two drinks the first hour (arrival plus toasts), one per hour after — which gives 5 drinks per guest instead of 4.

Notice what the rule doesn't ask: what people drink, only how much. That's handled next.

Step 1: Total drinks

Guests × drinks-per-guest. Standard rule: guests × hours. Generous: guests × (2 + hours − 1).

Step 2: Split by type

The default evening mix is 50% beer, 25% wine, 25% liquor. Wine-forward crowds flip to 20/50/30; beer-and-wine-only bars commonly run 60/40 with no liquor. For 400 drinks at the standard split: 200 beers, 100 glasses of wine, 100 liquor drinks.

Step 3: Convert to bottles

A 750ml bottle holds 25.4 ounces. From there: wine pours at 5 oz give 5 glasses per bottle; liquor at 1.5 oz shots gives 16-17 per bottle; champagne toast pours at 4 oz give 6 flutes per bottle. Beer is one drink per bottle, 24 to the case.

  • Beer: 200 ÷ 24 = 8.3 → 9 cases (always round up)
  • Wine: 100 ÷ 5 = 20 bottles
  • Liquor: 100 ÷ 16.9 = 5.9 → 6 bottles of 750ml
  • Champagne toast: 100 flutes ÷ 6 = 16.7 → 17 bottles

Full-bar caveat: cocktails that pour 2 oz of spirit plus mixer drain a bottle in about 12-14 drinks, not 17. If your crowd orders old fashioneds, add a bottle per 150 cocktails.

What it costs

At typical retail — $25 a case of beer, $12 a wine bottle, $20 a liquor bottle, $15 a champagne bottle — the 100-guest example totals 9 × $25 + 20 × $12 + 6 × $20 + 17 × $15 = $840. Venue bar packages for the same headcount typically quote $1,500-3,000, which is why buying your own is the single biggest bar saving available. Check two things in your venue contract first: corkage fees (often $10-25 per bottle, which quietly re-adds hundreds) and whether sealed leftovers can be returned — most stores and many venues allow it, which changes the "buffer" decision below.

The three adjustments worth making

Non-drinkers

Typically 30-40% of guests don't drink alcohol, so the honest floor is about two-thirds of the rule's total. Most couples buy the full amount anyway as buffer: leftover sealed bottles return or keep, while a bar that runs dry during the toasts does not. If you buy light, at least protect the champagne — the toast is the one pour you can't improvise.

Daytime weddings

Brunch and early-afternoon receptions run 10-20% lighter. Multiply by 0.85 and shift the mix toward sparkling wine and light beer. Evening receptions with dancing run the full estimate; late-night snacks famously trigger a second drinking wave, so don't trim for a midnight pizza hour.

Signature cocktails

One or two signature cocktails simplify the liquor math: each 750ml bottle serves about 14-15 of a 2-oz-spirit recipe, and guests drink what's offered. A single signature cocktail plus beer and wine usually cuts total liquor needs by a bottle or two versus a full bar — and it speeds the bar line, which matters more than most couples expect during the cocktail hour crush.

Non-alcoholic math (don't skip it)

Plan soft drinks at one per guest per two hours — the same guests, plus the drinking ones between pours. Water is the sleeper: half-liter bottles or a water station at roughly one per guest for a summer reception. Coffee for 60-70% of guests if it's an evening wedding with a send-off. The bar gets the attention, but the non-alcoholic table is where weddings actually run out.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much alcohol do I need for a 100-person wedding?

One drink per guest per hour over a 4-hour reception is 400 drinks: about 200 beers (9 cases of 24), 100 glasses of wine (20 bottles), and 100 liquor drinks (6 bottles of 750ml) at the standard 50/25/25 split. Add 17 bottles of champagne for a toast. That's roughly $840 at retail prices.

How many bottles of wine do I need per guest?

If wine is a quarter of the mix, each drinking guest averages one glass of wine per reception — one bottle per five guests. A wine-forward crowd drinking half their drinks as wine needs closer to two glasses each, or two bottles per five guests.

Is it cheaper to buy your own wedding alcohol?

Usually yes — retail markups at venues run 2-4x store prices, so buying your own at $800-900 for 100 guests can replace a $2,000-3,000 venue package. The catch is venue corkage fees ($10-25 per bottle in many contracts), required licensed bartenders, and return policies. Run both totals before deciding.

Should I account for non-drinkers?

Roughly 60-70% of a typical guest list drinks alcohol, so the true minimum is about two-thirds of the rule's total. Most couples buy the full amount anyway as buffer, since leftover sealed bottles can often be returned — and running dry during the toasts is the worse failure mode.
