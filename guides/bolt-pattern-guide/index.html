Bolt Patterns Explained: Measuring 5x114.3, 5x4.5 and PCD

🔩 ~40-80K searches/mo cluster⏱️ 8 min read

A bolt pattern reads like "5x114.3": five studs on a 114.3mm circle. That circle — the pitch circle diameter, PCD — is the one number you must get right before buying wheels, and the one number people most often measure wrong on odd stud counts. The fix is a single trig fact: on a 5-lug hub, measure two adjacent studs center-to-center and multiply by 1.7013. Here's the geometry, the conversions, and the fitment factors that matter after the pattern matches.

What the numbers mean

The first number is the stud count: 4, 5, 6, or 8 on passenger vehicles, 10 on some heavy trucks. The second is the diameter of the circle passing through all stud centers — PCD in millimeters, or inches on older domestic listings. "5x114.3" and "5x4.5" are therefore the same pattern: 4.5 × 25.4 = 114.3, exactly. The wheel industry's inch-metric equivalents are worth taping to the toolbox:

How to measure even lug counts (4, 6, 8)

Easy mode: even counts have studs directly across from each other, so measure center-to-center across two opposite studs and you've measured the PCD itself. A 4x100 hub reads ~100mm across; a 6x139.7 truck hub reads ~139.7mm (5.5"). No math.

How to measure 5-lug (the 1.7013 trick)

Five studs sit 72° apart, so none oppose each other — a straight-across measurement is meaningless. Geometry fills the gap: on any circle, the distance between two adjacent stud centers equals PCD × sin(half the angle). For 5 lug, half-angle is 36° and sin 36° = 0.5878, so:

PCD = adjacent spacing ÷ 0.5878 = adjacent spacing × 1.7013

Worked example with clean numbers: measure 67.2mm between adjacent centers. 67.2 × 1.7013 = 114.3mm = 4.5" — the 5x114.3 (5x4.5) pattern. Measure 82.1mm on another hub: 82.1 × 1.7013 = 139.7mm = 5x5.5, the truck circle. Both check on a calculator, and both are what the bolt pattern calculator returns from those inputs. The factors for other counts: 3-lug ×1.1547, 4-lug ×1.4142, 6-lug ×2 exactly, 8-lug ×2.6131.

Practical tips: measure center-to-center (the edges of the studs mislead), use calipers if the number matters — the close pairs below differ by less than a tape measure's error — and if you get a number between two standards, re-measure before concluding anything.

The close pairs that cause bad purchases

  • 5x120 vs 5x120.65 (4.75"). 0.65mm apart — BMW-family circle vs long-running GM circle. Wedging one onto the other loads lug hardware at an angle; it fails in service, not in the driveway.
  • 5x114.3 vs 5x115. 0.7mm apart, and the temptation is real because both are common. Same answer: no.
  • 5x127 vs 5x130. 3mm apart — measurable, but sloppy database entries mix them up.

Legitimate dual-pattern wheels exist: manufacturers drill two circles (e.g., 5x114.3/5x115) into one casting, and both are then true fits. That's the safe way to cover close patterns — not tolerance-stacking on the lug nuts.

Pattern matched. Does the wheel fit? Not yet.

The bolt circle is one of four checks:

  • Center bore. The wheel's hub hole must be at least your hub diameter; hub-centric wheels need an exact bore or hub-centric rings to fill the gap.
  • Offset. How far the mounting face sits from the wheel's centerline, in millimeters. From backspace: offset = (backspace − width ÷ 2) × 25.4 — an 8" wheel with 5.5" backspace is +38.1mm. Wrong offset rubs fenders or suspension, or buries the wheel.
  • Width and tire size. The tire has to match the rim width range — the tire size calculator works out diameters, revs per mile, and speedometer error when you change sizes.
  • Lug seat type. Conical (acorn), ball (radius), or flat seats — the wheel and hardware must match; mixing them is another quiet failure mode.

Your authority for all four is the owner's manual and the door-jamb sticker, and any wheel retailer's "fits your car" claim should still be verified against them. While you're under the car, a wheel alignment after new wheels and tires protects the investment — new rubber on the old alignment wears out fast.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you measure a 5-lug bolt pattern without opposite studs?

Measure center-to-center between two adjacent studs and multiply by 1.7013 (that's 1 divided by sin 36°). A 67.2mm adjacent spacing equals 114.3mm — the 5x114.3 / 5x4.5 pattern. Alternatively, measure from the center of one stud to the outer edge of the stud two away, which approximates the circle diameter directly.

Is 5x4.5 the same bolt pattern as 5x114.3?

Yes — 4.5 inches × 25.4 = 114.3mm exactly. Metric and decimal-inch names for one circle. The same applies to 5x5 = 5x127, 5x5.5 = 5x139.7, 5x4.75 = 5x120.65, 6x5.5 = 6x139.7, and 8x6.5 = 8x165.1.

Can I put 5x115 wheels on a 5x114.3 car?

They are different circles 0.7mm apart, and the honest answer is no — forcing the combination wedges the lug hardware at an angle, and it's a failure mode tire shops see regularly. Some wheel makers drill dual patterns (5x114.3/5x115) into one wheel; that's the legitimate way to cover both.

Where do I find my car's bolt pattern without measuring?

The owner's manual lists it, the driver's door-jamb sticker usually references wheel/tire fitment details, and a dealer parts desk can confirm by VIN. Measuring takes two minutes with calipers, so most people verify rather than trust memory — pattern databases online are frequently wrong on close pairs like 120 vs 120.65.
