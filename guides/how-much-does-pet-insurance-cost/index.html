How Much Does Pet Insurance Cost?

🐶 60K searches/mo⏱️ 8 min read

Pet insurance costs about $56 a month for a dog and $32 for a cat — but that average hides a 4x spread between a young mixed-breed cat in Mississippi and a senior Bulldog in California. Here's what actually moves the number, what the policy won't pay for, and how to tell whether it's worth it for your pet.

The national averages

North American Pet Health Insurance Association members cover the large majority of U.S. policies. Their 2024 industry report puts the average accident-and-illness premium at $676 a year for dogs ($56 a month) and $383 a year for cats ($32 a month). Accident-only plans — which skip illness entirely — average closer to $16-$20 a month for dogs and about $11 for cats.

Those are averages across every age, breed, and state. Your quote is the average plus four multipliers, which is why the same animal can price anywhere in a ±20% band or more depending on the carrier.

How premiums climb as your pet ages

The number that matters more than today's quote is the trajectory. Premiums are roughly flat through age 5, then step up about 15-20% at renewal per age band: a dog that costs $56 a month at 4 might be $67 at 7, $78 at 9, and $90 past 11 — assuming no claims surcharge and average veterinary inflation, which has lately run hotter than that. Budget the lifetime cost, not the first year.

Enrolling early doesn't lock in the price, but it does lock in insurability. Pre-existing conditions are excluded permanently, and they're diagnosed, not chosen — one ear-infection visit before enrollment turns every future ear claim into a denial.

What the policy won't pay for

  • Pre-existing conditions — always excluded, including anything noted in records before enrollment.
  • Wellness and routine care — vaccines, checkups, dental cleanings; usually a separate add-on that rarely pays for itself.
  • Exam fees — many plans exclude the veterinary visit fee on top of the treatment.
  • Breeding, cosmetic, and elective procedures — declawing, tail docking, and related care.

Is it worth it? The honest math

Pet insurance is a hedge against the $5,000-$15,000 crisis, not a discount program. For context on what those crises cost: TPLO knee surgery runs $3,500-$6,000, an intestinal blockage $2,000-$5,000, and cancer treatment $5,000-$15,000. A dog policy at $56 a month costs $672 a year; over seven years that's about $4,700 — roughly one bad orthopedic event. Cats are cheaper to insure and cheaper to treat, which pushes the value closer to the line.

Three situations where coverage clearly earns its keep: you couldn't absorb a surprise four-figure bill without a decision you'd regret; your breed has known expensive conditions (Bulldogs and spines, Goldens and cancer, Persians and kidneys); or you want the "treat everything" conversation with your vet to be medical, not financial. If you have the discipline to fund a dedicated $10,000 pet account instead, self-insuring is a defensible alternative — most people don't, which is the actual argument for the policy.

How to buy it without overpaying

  1. Quote at least three carriers with identical deductible and reimbursement — headline prices compare different structures, so normalize before comparing.
  2. Raise the deductible before cutting the reimbursement rate. The reimbursement % is what protects you in a $8,000 claim; the deductible is a one-time number.
  3. Check the claim-payment method. Plans that pay the vet directly beat ones that reimburse you and hope you can float $6,000 for a month.
  4. Read the bilaterality clause if your breed has joint issues — some carriers treat both knees as one condition after the first claim.
  5. Skip the wellness add-on unless you've run the schedule of benefits against its price; the math usually favors paying routine care directly.

FAQs

What is the average cost of pet insurance per month?

About $56 a month for dogs and $32 for cats for accident-and-illness coverage, per NAPHIA's 2024 industry report ($676 and $383 a year respectively). Most real quotes fall in a ±20% band around those averages once breed, age, state, and plan structure are applied — roughly $35 to $100 a month for a dog, $20 to $45 for a cat.

At what age should I buy pet insurance?

As young as practical — usually right after the adoption checkup clears. Two reasons: premiums are at their lifetime low (roughly 5% under the young-adult price), and any condition diagnosed before enrollment is excluded forever. A limp at age 3 that becomes a documented knee issue means every future cruciate claim gets denied. Waiting costs you the rate and the coverage at the same time.

Why did my pet insurance premium go up so much at renewal?

Pet premiums are repriced annually on the pet's age and the carrier's recent claims experience, and veterinary inflation has been running hot — average claim costs rose roughly 10-20% in recent years. A typical dog policy climbs 15-20% at renewal around ages 6-8, and the increases compound. The price you were quoted at enrollment is not the price at age 10.
