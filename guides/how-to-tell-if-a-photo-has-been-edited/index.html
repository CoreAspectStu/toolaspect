How to Tell If a Photo Has Been Edited

📄 7 min readUpdated October 19, 2026

You can often tell a photo has been handled. Telling what was changed is harder. The metadata inside a JPEG records the last program that saved it, the dates, and sometimes an edit history, but all of it can be stripped or rewritten. Here's how to read those clues, what they prove and don't, and what to check in the picture itself when the metadata is gone.

Can you tell if a photo has been edited?

Sometimes. More often you can tell it's been handled without being able to say what changed. A JPEG carries a small record of where it's been: the camera, the dates, the last program that saved it, sometimes a list of edits. Those clues are easy to read and easy to fake. Here's what each clue proves, and what it doesn't:

ClueWhat it provesWhat it doesn't prove
Software tag names an editor (Photoshop, Lightroom, Snapseed)The file was saved by that programWhat was changed. A color tweak and a removed person look the same here
Modified date later than the date takenThe file was re-saved after captureManipulation. Every camera photo on our disk had been re-saved
XMP history entriesA sequence of saves, converts and derived copiesThe content of each edit, though Lightroom and Photoshop sometimes list the settings
EXIF pixel size differs from the actual image sizeThe image was cropped or resized after the EXIF was writtenWhere the crop was or what was removed
No EXIF at allIt passed through something that strips metadata, or it was a screenshotAnything about edits. Social platforms strip EXIF on upload

The honest summary: metadata can tell you a photo was opened in Photoshop. It can't tell you whether someone straightened the horizon or pasted in a person. For that you need the pixels, or a signed record.

What does the metadata actually show?

Drop the file into the EXIF viewer and look at three things: the Software tag (the last program to save the file), DateTimeOriginal against ModifyDate (shutter time against last save), and the XMP block, which may carry an edit history. To see what's normal, we read every JPEG on our own drives, 228 unique files:

FieldFiles that had it
Any EXIF metadata at all25 of 228
Camera make and model12 of 228
Date taken (DateTimeOriginal)12 of 228
Date taken differs from modified date12 of 12 that had both
XMP block present15 of 228
XMP edit history (xmpMM:History)6 of 228

Two things stand out. Most JPEGs have no EXIF at all, because stock libraries, web exports and messaging apps strip it, so an empty panel means almost nothing. And every camera photo we found had a modified date later than the capture date, by minutes to two months, with Lightroom Classic, Photoshop or Adobe Bridge in the Software tag. A date mismatch says a photo went through a workflow, not that the workflow lied.

The six files with an XMP history are the interesting ones. Adobe programs log each save and conversion with a timestamp and program name, the closest thing a plain JPEG has to a change log.

What happens to the metadata when a photo is cropped?

It depends on the tool. We cropped one of those portraits, a Canon EOS R6 frame at 4,717 by 3,145 pixels, to a quarter of its area with a Python image library, two ways. Saved the default way, the result had zero EXIF tags: no camera, no dates, nothing to examine. Saved with the metadata copied across, the crop still said Canon EOS R6, kept both original dates, and its EXIF pixel dimensions still read 4,717 by 3,145 while the actual image was 2,358 by 1,572.

That mismatch is the tell. Phone galleries and most desktop editors update the dimension tags when they crop; scripts, batch tools and some web services don't. If the EXIF says the image should be larger than it is, something cut it down after the camera wrote the record. The crop could be innocent. It's still a crop.

The flip side: copying old EXIF onto a new file leaves exactly this inconsistency. If you want a photo to carry nothing, strip it properly with the image metadata remover. Our guide on removing location from photos covers the privacy side.

How do you spot edits in the picture itself?

When the metadata is gone or clean, look at the image:

  • Light and shadow. Pasted elements rarely match the direction, softness and color of the scene's light. Check shadows under feet and chins, and reflections in glass and eyes.
  • Edges and repetition. Clone and heal tools copy texture from nearby. Look for the same patch of grass, brick or skin twice, and for edges too smooth or too sharp against their background.
  • Noise and compression. Sensor noise is roughly even across a frame. An inserted object brings its own noise and compression history. Error level analysis, offered by many free forensic sites, highlights regions compressed a different number of times than the rest. Read it with care; bright flat areas light up for innocent reasons too.
  • The source. Run it through reverse image search. An older, larger or different version is your answer and your original to compare against.

Fully generated images are a separate question. There's no camera, so metadata is useless. The AI image detector gives a probability, not a verdict, and should be read like any other clue here.

What are Content Credentials?

A newer approach that fixes the weakness of EXIF: instead of plain text anyone can rewrite, the camera or editing app cryptographically signs a record of what it did. The standard is C2PA; the user-facing name is Content Credentials. Adobe's apps can attach them, and a few camera and phone makers have started to.

The limits are those of any signature. A missing credential proves nothing, since most images don't have one. A valid one proves the signed steps happened, not that nothing else did, and it can be stripped by saving through an app that doesn't support it. When it's there, though, it beats every other clue on this page. And if a claim or dispute rides on the answer, only the original file from the camera or phone settles it. Frequently Asked Questions

How can I tell if a photo has been Photoshopped?

Check the EXIF Software tag; Photoshop and Lightroom write their names there when they save. Compare the capture date with the modified date, and the EXIF pixel size with the real size. Then look at the picture for mismatched light, repeated texture and odd edges. None of these alone proves manipulation.

Does editing a photo change its metadata?

Usually. Most editors update the Software tag and modified date and may add an XMP history entry. Some tools strip everything; some copy the old metadata onto the new file. In our test, a default script crop left zero EXIF tags, while a crop that copied metadata kept the camera's 4,717 by 3,145 pixel dimensions on a 2,358 by 1,572 image.

Does a missing EXIF mean the photo is fake?

No. Most JPEGs in the wild have no EXIF. In our sample of 228 files, only 25 had any, and social networks and messaging apps strip it on upload. A missing record only means you can't use metadata to help.

Can metadata be faked?

Yes, easily. EXIF and XMP are plain data that any number of free tools can rewrite, including camera model and dates. That's the problem Content Credentials, the C2PA standard, are designed to solve by signing the record so edits to it can be detected.

What is the best tool to check if an image has been edited?

An EXIF viewer for metadata, a reverse image search for earlier versions, and a forensic tool with error level analysis for the pixels. For a firm answer you need the original file or a valid Content Credential.
