SSDI vs SSI: What's the Difference, and Can You Get Both?

📄 7 min readUpdated October 25, 2026

SSDI and SSI are both Social Security disability programs, but they're paid for differently and follow different rules. SSDI is based on your work record and has no asset limit. SSI is needs-based, pays up to $994 a month in 2026, and caps savings at $2,000. Here's how they compare side by side, which pays more, and how they combine if you qualify for both.

What's the difference between SSDI and SSI?

Both use the same medical definition of disability and the same application, which is why they get mixed up. SSDI (Social Security Disability Insurance) works like insurance: you paid for it through payroll taxes, and the check is based on what you earned. SSI (Supplemental Security Income) is a needs-based program for people with very little income or savings, and work history doesn't matter.

Who qualifies for SSDI?

You need a medical condition that stops you doing substantial work and is expected to last at least 12 months or end in death, plus enough work credits. In 2026 you earn one credit for every $1,890 of wages or self-employment income, up to four a year. Most adults need 40 credits, 20 of them earned in the ten years before they became disabled. Younger workers need fewer. If you became disabled before 24, six credits in the three years before is enough, and between 24 and 31 it's credits for about half the time since you turned 21.

Income and savings don't matter for SSDI; a house, a 401(k) and a working spouse won't reduce it. For how the benefit is worked out, see how much does SSDI pay.

Who qualifies for SSI?

SSI is for people who are disabled, blind or at least 65, and who have limited income and resources. "Resources" means countable assets: no more than $2,000 for a single person or $3,000 for a couple. Your home and one car don't count; bank accounts, cash and investments do.

Income counts too, including help from other people. If you live in someone else's household and they cover your rent or mortgage share, SSI can be cut by up to a third. In 2026 that means a check of $662.67 instead of $994.

Which pays more, SSDI or SSI?

For most people who qualify for both, SSDI. The average SSDI check after the 2026 cost-of-living increase is about $1,630 a month, against an SSI maximum of $994. SSI is a ceiling and SSDI isn't, so a worker with good earnings can get several times what SSI pays.

SSI matters most for people with short or low-paid work histories, whose SSDI can come out below $994.

Can you get SSDI and SSI at the same time?

Yes, if your SSDI benefit is small. It's called getting concurrent benefits. SSI treats the SSDI check as unearned income: the first $20 is ignored and the rest comes off your SSI dollar for dollar. Here's how that works out at the 2026 rate, for someone with no other income:

How does working affect each benefit?

This is where the programs feel most different. SSDI has cliffs: after your nine-month trial work period, any month you earn over the substantial gainful activity limit ($1,690 in 2026) can cost you the whole check, though there's a grace period and a 36-month window to restart benefits. How do the waiting periods and back pay compare?

SSDI makes you wait five full months from the date your disability began, but it can pay up to a year before your application date once that wait is over. SSI has no waiting period, but it never pays for months before you applied: benefits start the month after you file. So apply as soon as you can; one application covers both.

Large SSI back payments, three times the monthly rate or more ($2,982 in 2026), usually come in up to three installments six months apart. SSDI back pay comes as a lump sum. Which should you apply for?

You don't have to choose. SSA checks every disability applicant for both, and SSI requires you to claim any other benefit you're due, SSDI included. Frequently Asked Questions

Is SSI or SSDI better?

If you qualify for SSDI it usually pays more: the 2026 average is about $1,630 a month against an SSI maximum of $994. SSDI also has no asset limit and leads to Medicare. SSI is better for people with little or no work history, who can't get SSDI at all.

Can I get SSI and SSDI at the same time?

Yes, if your SSDI benefit is low and your assets are under $2,000. SSI ignores the first $20 of your SSDI and reduces the rest dollar for dollar, so the two together come to $1,014 a month in 2026. Once SSDI reaches $1,014, SSI stops.

What is the maximum SSI payment in 2026?

$994 a month for an individual and $1,491 for an eligible couple. It drops if you have other income or someone else pays your shelter costs.

Does SSI have an asset limit but SSDI doesn't?

Yes. SSI limits countable resources to $2,000 for one person or $3,000 for a couple, not counting your home and one car. SSDI has no asset or income test at all; only work earnings over the substantial gainful activity limit affect it.

Do I get Medicare or Medicaid with SSI and SSDI?

SSDI leads to Medicare after you've been entitled to benefits for 24 months, with exceptions such as ALS. SSI usually comes with Medicaid straight away in most states. People who get both can have Medicaid during the Medicare waiting period.
