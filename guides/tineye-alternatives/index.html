What is TinEye good at?

TinEye builds a fingerprint of your image and matches it against its own index of tens of billions of crawled images. Crops, resizes, color changes and light edits still match, which makes it the go-to tool for tracking where one particular photo has spread.

Its sorting is the real standout. You can sort results by Best Match, Most Changed, Biggest Image, Newest or Oldest. Oldest is the one photographers love, because the earliest copy TinEye has seen is often the closest you'll get to the original source. You can also filter by domain, stock photo sites or collections like Flickr.

It's also unusually clear about privacy. TinEye says uploaded images aren't saved or added to its index, and they're deleted right after the search runs.

Why look for a TinEye alternative?

There are three common reasons.

  • It doesn't recognize faces. TinEye matches whole images, not people. A different photo of the same person won't come up.
  • It only finds copies. If you want "what is this object" or "where can I buy this lamp," TinEye can't help. It isn't built to understand what's in the picture.
  • There's a cap. Free, non-commercial use is limited to 100 searches a day and 300 a week. Commercial and API use is paid.

Its index is also its own. A copy that Google crawled last week might not be in TinEye yet, and the reverse happens too.

Is there a free face search like PimEyes?

Not a fully equivalent one. Google deliberately doesn't run facial recognition in Lens, though it may show results for well-known public figures. TinEye doesn't match faces at all. Yandex has been the strongest free option for faces, but users report its results have become patchier outside Russian and Eastern European sites.

Is reverse image search private?

It depends on the engine. TinEye says it deletes uploads straight after the search. Google, Bing and Yandex handle uploads under their own privacy policies, and those differ. For a photo of your face or your kids, read the policy before you upload, or search with a link to an image that's already public.

The photo you share can give things away too. Original phone photos often carry GPS coordinates and the time they were taken. What should you do when you find a stolen image?

Take a screenshot of the page with the URL and date visible, and save the image link. Then use the site's own reporting form. Most platforms have a copyright or impersonation option, and a DMCA notice to the host works for many websites. Keep your original files with their timestamps, since they're the easiest proof that the photo is yours.

Copies tend to come back, so repeat your searches every month or two for any image that matters to you.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is TinEye free?

Yes, for non-commercial use, with a limit of 100 searches per day and 300 per week. Commercial use and API access are paid. You don't need an account to run a search.

Does TinEye do facial recognition?

No. TinEye matches images, not faces. It finds copies of the exact image you search, including crops, resizes and edits, but it won't find a different photo of the same person.

Which is better, TinEye or Google Lens?

They do different jobs. TinEye is better for tracking copies of one specific image and finding the oldest version. Google Lens searches a much bigger index and also identifies objects, products and text. For a thorough search, use both.

Does TinEye save the images I upload?

TinEye says it doesn't. Uploaded images are fingerprinted for the search, aren't added to its index, and are deleted right after the results appear. Other engines handle uploads under their own privacy policies.

Can I reverse search an image that's only on my phone?

Yes. Google Lens, Bing, TinEye and Yandex all accept uploads from your device. Our multi-engine tool needs a public image link, so for a local photo, open each engine and use its upload button instead.
