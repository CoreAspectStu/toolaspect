Lap Time vs Split Time: How to Use a Stopwatch

⏱️ 6 min readUpdated September 27, 2026

A lap time is how long one segment took. A split time is the total time from the start up to that point. Run four laps of 1:32, 1:35, 1:37 and 1:30 and your splits are 1:32, 3:07, 4:44 and 6:14. Most stopwatches, including ours, show laps, so you get splits by adding the laps together as you go.

What's the difference between a lap and a split?

People mix these words up, but most running and swimming coaches use them like this.

  • Lap time: the length of one segment, measured from the last button press. It resets for every lap.
  • Split time: the cumulative time at a checkpoint, measured from the start. It only ever goes up.

Both come from the same button presses. The difference is just what you subtract. A lap is "this press minus the last press". A split is "this press minus the start".

Laps tell you about consistency. Splits tell you where you stand against a goal. If you're chasing a 6:00 mile, you want to see 1:30, 3:00 and 4:30 on your splits. To see if you faded, look at the laps.

How do you use a stopwatch?

Our online stopwatch has three buttons, and it works the same way as a sports watch.

  1. Start. The clock counts up in HH:MM:SS, with milliseconds shown as .mmm beside it.
  2. Lap. Press it at each checkpoint. The clock keeps running and a new row appears at the top of the list with that lap's time in MM:SS.mmm.
  3. Stop, then Resume. Stop pauses the clock and keeps the time. The same button then reads Resume and carries on from where you left off, which is handy for a rest break you don't want counted.
  4. Reset. Clears the display and the lap list back to zero.

Lap is disabled while paused, so you can't log one by accident during a break. Each row shows time since the previous lap, not the running total.

How do you turn lap times into pace?

Pace is time per distance, so you scale the lap time up. For a 400 m lap:

  • Mile pace = lap time × 1609.344 ÷ 400, which is lap time × 4.023.
  • Kilometer pace = lap time × 2.5.

A 1:32 lap is 92 seconds. 92 × 4.023 = 370 seconds, or 6:10 per mile. 92 × 2.5 = 230 seconds, or 3:50 per km. The table covers common lap times.

How accurate is hand timing?

The clock isn't the problem. Your thumb is. Simple reaction time to something you see is typically around 200 to 250 milliseconds, and it varies from press to press. You press late at the start and often early at the finish, and those errors don't cancel reliably.

Track and field takes this seriously. Under the rules, hand times are only counted to a tenth of a second, and any hundredths get rounded up to the next tenth, so 10.83 becomes 10.9. Statisticians who compare hand times against fully automatic timing commonly add 0.24 seconds to hand-timed 100 m and 200 m marks, because the timer at the finish is reacting to the starter's gun from a distance.

So treat a hand-timed reading as good to about a tenth or two, even though the display shows thousandths. Fine for a workout or a speech rehearsal, not for settling a close race.

One habit helps most: press when the same body part crosses the line every lap, such as the torso.

Does it keep time in a background tab?

Yes. Browsers slow down timers on hidden pages. Chrome checks timers in a background tab about once a second, and after five minutes hidden it can stretch some to once a minute. A stopwatch that counted its own ticks would drift badly.

Ours doesn't count ticks. It refreshes the display every 10 ms while you're watching, but each refresh works out the elapsed time from the system clock (now minus the start time). When the tab is in the background the numbers just update less often. Switch back and the reading is correct.

When should you use a timer instead?

A stopwatch answers "how long did that take?" A countdown timer answers "tell me when time's up." If you know the length in advance, like a 3-minute plank, a 25-minute bake or a 60-second rest, use the countdown timer.

For focused work sessions, the Pomodoro timer runs 25-minute work blocks with short breaks built in. Use the stopwatch when the finish is open-ended.

The bottom line

Laps are segments, splits are running totals, and you can turn one into the other with simple addition. Use laps to check pacing, splits to check against a target, and trust hand timing to about a tenth of a second.
