Is Identity Theft Protection Worth It? The Honest Math

💰 CPC: $20⏱️ 8 min read🔐 Security & insurance

Identity theft protection plans cost $8 to $35 a month, $96 to $420 a year per adult, and a couple on a typical $19.99 tier pays $479.76 a year. Meanwhile the single strongest protection available, a credit freeze at all three bureaus, is free under federal law. Here's the math that sorts who should pay from who shouldn't.

How common is identity theft, really?

Common enough to take seriously. The FTC logged more than 1 million identity theft reports in 2023, and a Bureau of Justice Statistics survey estimated roughly 24 million U.S. residents age 16 or older experienced identity theft in a 12-month period. Most of it is card fraud that banks absorb, but the scary subset, new accounts opened in your name, tax fraud, and synthetic identity abuse, is where victims spend months untangling records.

The honest framing matters here: the risk is real, but the protection product being sold is mostly a detection and cleanup service, not a shield. Whether it's worth $240 to $500 a year depends on which risks you actually face and which ones you've already closed for free.

What you can get for exactly $0

Before comparing subscriptions, know what's already free, because the free list covers most of the prevention column:

  • Credit freeze (all three bureaus). Free under federal law since 2018. Blocks new accounts in your name entirely, something no paid plan does.
  • IRS IP PIN. Free. A six-digit code that stops fraudulent tax returns filed under your SSN.
  • Basic credit monitoring. Free from most banks and card issuers, plus several apps. Alerts on new accounts and inquiries, usually one or two bureaus.
  • Free credit reports. Weekly from all three bureaus at the official site, annualcreditreport.com.
  • Recovery plan. The FTC's IdentityTheft.gov generates the official report and a step-by-step plan, free, and that report is what creditors actually ask for.

The paid column is real too, but narrower: near-real-time 3-bureau alerts, SSN and dark-web scanning, and above all a restoration caseworker who does the disputing for you.

The 3-year math nobody shows you

Monthly pricing hides the total. A couple on a $19.99-per-adult standard plan pays $39.98 a month, which sounds manageable, and $1,439.28 over three years, which sounds like a decision. A family doubling down on premium at $29.99 per adult pays $3,598.80 over five years, more than many of the incidents the plans cover would cost out of pocket after bank reimbursements.

That's not an argument that the plans are a scam. Restoration help is genuinely valuable when you need it: victims of new-account fraud can spend dozens of hours on calls and certified letters, and a caseworker absorbs most of that. The argument is narrower: know the total, know the free alternative, and buy based on exposure.

Who should actually pay for a plan?

  1. Business owners and public figures. Your name, EIN, and personal data circulate. High exposure makes monitoring and restoration worth real money.
  2. Past victims. Prior victims are disproportionately re-victimized, and cleanup help pays for itself the second time.
  3. People whose SSN has surfaced in serious breaches. SSN and dark-web monitoring is the tier feature aimed squarely at you.
  4. Anyone who won't do the free steps. An honest self-assessment: if you'll never get around to freezing your credit, a paid plan that watches your file is better than nothing. It's the expensive version of the cheap fix, but it's not nothing.

Everyone else, low exposure, willing to spend one evening on setup, is probably better served by the free stack plus the habits that stop account takeover: unique passwords from a password generator, 2FA on email and financial logins, and a carrier PIN on your mobile account to block SIM swaps.

What to do first if it happens anyway

Order matters. File at IdentityTheft.gov first, that report is the skeleton key creditors ask for. Then freeze all three bureaus, place a one-year fraud alert, change passwords starting with email, and notify banks and issuers. Log every call and keep every letter. If you later add a paid plan, that's the moment its restoration service earns a decade of premiums in one case.

One more angle: the money you'd spend on monitoring compounds elsewhere. $40 a month is $480 a year, money that could instead build the emergency fund that absorbs identity-theft friction costs, or close liability gaps with an umbrella policy. Risk budgeting is still budgeting. Price both sides before subscribing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can identity theft protection prevent identity theft?

No. Monitoring and alerts tell you after something has already happened. The only tool that prevents new-account fraud outright is a credit freeze, which is free at all three bureaus under federal law. Paid plans are faster detection plus professional cleanup, not prevention, no matter what the ads imply.

How much does LifeLock-style protection cost per year?

Typical mainstream plans run $8 to $15 a month for basic 3-bureau monitoring, $15 to $25 for standard tiers with SSN and dark-web alerts plus restoration, and $25 to $35 for premium with up to $1 million of expense reimbursement. For a single adult, that's roughly $100 to $420 a year, and couples usually pay per adult, $479.76 a year on a $19.99 tier.

Does identity theft insurance cover stolen money?

Usually not the money itself. Fraudulent credit card charges are reimbursed by the issuer (federal law caps your liability at $50), and fraudulent bank transfers fall under separate banking protections. What the policy covers is recovery costs: certified mail, notaries, legal fees, sometimes lost wages while you untangle things. Useful in a bad case, narrower than the '$1 million protection' headline reads.

How do I freeze my credit for free?

Visit each bureau directly, Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion, and request a security freeze online or by phone. It's free for everyone under federal law since 2018, takes about 15 minutes per bureau, and you get a PIN or login to lift the freeze when you actually apply for credit. Freezing does not affect your score, and thawing is instant at most bureaus when you need it.
