Flat Tow Setup Cost: What Dinghy Towing Really Runs

💡 Evergreen RV niche💰 CPC: $1-3⏱️ 7 min read

Flat towing a car behind your motorhome is the best way to travel: a five-minute hookup, no trailer to store, wheels-down freedom at every stop. The catch is the setup bill. Five component lines have to work together — base plate, tow bar, braking, lighting, extras — and the total lands between $1,970 for a DIY budget build and $4,800 for a premium dealer install, with the typical RVer's stack at about $3,500. Here's every line item, three full builds, and the two checks to run before spending anything.

Three full builds, line by line

The budget DIY build — $1,970

A $450 entry base plate, a $600 car-mounted tow bar, a $750 portable inertial brake unit, $80 magnetic tow lights, and $90 of cables and fittings, with your own Saturdays supplying the labor. That's the honest floor for a four-down setup that still brakes the toad. You can shave another $750 by dropping braking entirely — don't. Most states require brakes on towed vehicles above a weight threshold nearly every car exceeds, and the stopping-distance math gets ugly fast with an unbraked 3,500 pounds pushing you.

The typical installed build — $3,500

The one most RV shops quote: a $550 standard bolt-on plate, $1,000 self-aligning RV-mounted tow bar, $1,200 proportional portable braking (the Blue Ox Patriot class), $180 diode lighting kit, $120 in cables and charge-line parts — $3,050 of parts — plus $450 of shop labor. The bumper comes off for the plate; that's where the hours go. Against a typical $2,800 dolly purchase you're $700 over on day one, but every hookup after takes five minutes instead of fifteen, with nothing extra to park, store, or register — and the portable brake unit moves to your next car while a dolly's straps don't travel that way.

The premium installed build — $4,800

A $700 heavy-duty plate, $1,400 non-binding tow bar (disconnects at any angle, even jackknifed), $1,500 installed proportional braking that hides under the seat, $250 wireless LED lighting, $150 of extras, and an $800 dealer install. Full-timers and mountain travelers buy this tier; the permanent brake and the non-binding bar are the two features that earn their keep daily.

Flat tow, dolly, or trailer?

The dolly starts at $1,500-$3,500 to buy and $50-$75 a day to rent; an open car trailer runs $2,000-$5,000. So on purchase price alone, flat towing isn't automatically cheapest. It wins on operating friction: five-minute hookups, no second vehicle to park at the campground, no trailer registration or inspection, and no dolly tires and bearings to maintain. A dolly or trailer works with nearly any car, though, while four-down towing requires a drivetrain that tolerates it — which brings up check number one below. And if you tow fewer than about three weeks a year, renting anything beats buying everything: at $50-$75 a day, a two-to-three-week trip costs $1,000-$1,500 total, comparable to the budget build you'd own and store for 49 weeks.

The two checks before you buy anything

  1. Is your car flat-towable at all? The owner's manual's "recreational towing" section is the only authority. Many AWD vehicles and twin-clutch automatics can't be flat towed, period; some need a transmission lube pump or a fuse pulled. Discovering this after buying $400-$800 of base plate is the classic expensive mistake — verify before the hardware, ideally before the car.
  2. Does the weight math work? The toad's curb weight, plus everything loaded in the RV, has to sit under the motorhome's GCWR and hitch rating. Run your actual numbers through the towing capacity calculator before ordering parts.

After the build, two small recurring items: re-torque the base plate bolts after the first few hundred miles (free, 30 minutes), and expect the toad's tires to wear faster — they roll the whole trip, even if the odometer (electronic, key in accessory) mostly stands still.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to flat tow a car behind an RV?

Between $1,970 and $4,800 for most passenger cars. The budget DIY floor (entry base plate, basic car-mounted tow bar, portable inertial braking, magnetic lights, your own wrenches) runs about $1,970 in parts. The typical build RV owners land on — standard bolt-on plate, self-aligning tow bar, proportional portable braking, diode lighting, shop install — totals about $3,500. Premium hardware with a dealer install reaches $4,800.

What are the most expensive parts of a flat tow setup?

Two lines dominate: the supplemental braking system ($600 to $1,700 depending on inertial vs proportional vs installed) and the tow bar ($400 to $1,700). Together they're roughly two-thirds of a typical $3,500 build. The base plate ($400 to $800) looks cheap but drives the labor bill, because bumper disassembly and frame access take the hours.

Can I install a base plate and tow bar myself?

Usually yes — most kits are bolt-on with hand tools, and DIY zeroes out the $450 to $800 labor line, which is most of the spread between the $3,500 typical build and the $1,970 budget floor. The jobs that reward experience are wiring through the firewall and setting up the brake system; many DIYers do the mechanical work and pay a shop only for those two.

Is flat towing cheaper than a tow dolly?

Up front, not always: flat tow runs $1,970 to $4,800 while a dolly costs $1,500 to $3,500 to buy (or $50 to $75 a day to rent). Flat towing wins on per-trip effort — five-minute hookups, no dolly to park and register — and the portable brake unit moves to your next car. If you tow fewer than about three weeks total per year, renting beats buying anything.
