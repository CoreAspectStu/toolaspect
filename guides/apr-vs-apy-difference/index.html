APR vs APY: What's the Difference?

💡 12K searches/mo💰 CPC: $9⏱️ 7 min read

APR and APY look similar but they measure different things. Understanding the gap between them can save you hundreds on loans and earn you more on savings. Here's the plain-English explanation.

The Core Difference in One Sentence

APR (Annual Percentage Rate) is the simple annual interest rate without compounding. APY (Annual Percentage Yield) is the effective rate after factoring in compounding throughout the year.

That's it. The entire concept comes down to compounding — how often interest gets added to the balance, so that future interest is calculated on a larger amount.

How Compounding Creates the Gap

Imagine a loan with 12% APR, compounded monthly. Each month, you're charged 1% interest (12% ÷ 12 months). After one month on a $1,000 balance: $10 interest. Your new balance is $1,010.

Month two: 1% of $1,010 = $10.10. Your balance is $1,020.10. Month three: 1% of $1,020.10 = $10.20. And so on.

Over 12 months, the total interest isn't $120 (which is what 12% of $1,000 would suggest). It's $126.83. That means the effective annual rate — the APY — is 12.68%, not 12%.

APY = (1 + APR/n)ⁿ − 1

Where n = number of compounding periods per year.

Step-by-Step: Converting APR to APY

  1. Identify the APR and compounding frequency. Example: 18% APR on a credit card, compounded daily (n=365).
  2. Divide APR by n. 0.18 ÷ 365 = 0.000493 per day.
  3. Add 1 and raise to the power of n. (1 + 0.000493)^365 = 1.1972.
  4. Subtract 1 and convert to percentage. 1.1972 − 1 = 0.1972 = 19.72% APY.
  5. Interpret the result. An 18% APR credit card actually charges an effective 19.72% APY because of daily compounding.

Real-World Example: Why This Matters

You're comparing two savings accounts. Bank A offers 5.00% APR compounded annually. Bank B offers 4.90% APR compounded daily.

Bank A APY: 5.00%. Bank B APY: 5.02%. Despite the lower APR, Bank B actually pays more because of daily compounding.

On $50,000 over a year: Bank A pays $2,500. Bank B pays $2,510. Small difference — but on larger balances over many years, it adds up.

How Institutions Use APR vs APY to Their Advantage

The financial industry has a simple rule: quote whichever number looks more favorable to them.

For loans and credit cards, lenders advertise APR because it's lower. A "15.99% APR" credit card actually costs you 17.27% APY with daily compounding.

For savings accounts and CDs, banks advertise APY because it's higher. "4.5% APY" sounds better than the underlying 4.4% APR.

This isn't deception — it's just marketing. But it means you need to know which number you're looking at and convert when comparing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which is higher, APR or APY?

APY is always equal to or higher than APR for the same nominal rate, because APY includes compounding. The more frequently interest compounds, the bigger the gap.

Is APR or APY better for loans?

For loans, look at APR — it shows the true cost of borrowing including fees. APY on a loan would show an even higher effective rate, which lenders don't want to highlight.

How do I convert APR to APY?

Use the formula APY = (1 + APR/n)^n - 1, where n is the compounding frequency. Monthly compounding: n=12. Daily: n=365.

Why do credit cards show APR but charge APY?

Credit cards quote APR because it's the lower number. But since interest compounds daily, the effective rate you actually pay is the APY, which is higher.

What is a good APY for a savings account?

High-yield savings accounts currently offer 4-5% APY. Traditional bank accounts often pay less than 0.5%, so switching can earn 10x more interest.
