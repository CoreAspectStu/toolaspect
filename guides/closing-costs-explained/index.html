Closing Costs Explained: What You'll Actually Pay

💡 27K searches/mo💰 CPC: $12⏱️ 8 min read

You saved the down payment. Then, a few weeks before settlement, the Loan Estimate lands and there's another five-figure number on it. Closing costs typically run 2% to 5% of the purchase price, and where you're buying moves that number more than any other factor. Here's what's actually in the bill, who pays which parts, and where you have room to negotiate.

What are closing costs, exactly?

Closing costs are everything due when the sale legally completes, beyond the price of the house itself. Three buckets: fees for creating your loan (origination, appraisal, credit check), fees for transferring the property cleanly (title search, title insurance, recording, transfer taxes), and prepaids, the money your lender collects up front to seed your escrow account for property taxes and homeowners insurance.

Prepaids trip people up. They're not really a cost, since the money pays bills you'd owe anyway, but they're due in cash at the closing table, so your budget has to treat them as one. Expect $2,000 to $4,000 for a typical purchase.

How much should you budget?

The national average is about 1.8% of the purchase price including transfer taxes, roughly $7,200 on a $400,000 home. But the state-by-state spread is enormous. Delaware averages around 5.4%, Pennsylvania 4.3%, and Maryland 3.7%, while Missouri, Indiana, and North Dakota sit near 0.8%. Same house price, a five-figure difference at the table.

Why? Transfer taxes. Some states take a healthy slice of every sale to record the deed; others take almost nothing. Lender and title fees vary too, but taxes are the swing factor, and they're the one line you can't shop your way out of.

What's actually in the bill?

Line by line, here's a typical buyer's side on a $400,000 purchase with 20% down:

  • Loan origination fee: 0.5% to 1% of the loan, so $1,600 to $3,200 on a $320,000 mortgage. This is the lender's fee for making the loan.
  • Appraisal: $300 to $600. The lender's confirmation the house is worth what you're paying.
  • Title search and title insurance: roughly 0.5% of the price, around $2,000. Protects against ownership disputes and liens you didn't know existed.
  • Escrow or settlement fee: $500 to $1,000 to the company running the closing.
  • Recording fees: about $125 to make the transfer official with the county.
  • Transfer taxes: the state-dependent wildcard, from near zero to 2%+ of the price.
  • Prepaids: $2,000 to $4,000 into escrow for insurance and property taxes. Your ongoing tax bill is its own budgeting exercise; a property tax calculator will show what your county charges annually.

Who pays what: buyer vs. seller?

Buyers typically carry the lender fees, appraisal, title insurance, and prepaids. Sellers traditionally pay the agent commissions and, in many states, some or all of the transfer tax, though local custom varies a lot; in some markets buyers and sellers split it.

Nearly all of it is negotiable. In a slow market, seller concessions of 2% to 3% toward the buyer's closing costs are common and can nearly wipe out your bill. In a hot market, asking for concessions can sink your offer. Your agent will know which market you're in.

How can you pay less at closing?

  • Shop the shoppable services. Your Loan Estimate marks which services you can choose yourself, usually title and settlement. Quotes can differ by hundreds of dollars for identical coverage.
  • Compare lender fee sheets, not just rates. Two lenders at the same rate can be $1,500 apart on origination and junk fees. Get at least three Loan Estimates.
  • Consider a lender credit. Accept a slightly higher rate and the lender covers part of your costs. Worth it if cash is tight now, or if you'll refinance or move within a few years. Model the payment difference with a mortgage calculator before deciding.
  • Ask about first-time buyer programs. Many states offer grants or forgivable loans specifically for closing costs.
  • Close late in the month. Prepaid interest runs from your closing date to month end, so closing on the 28th instead of the 3rd trims that line meaningfully.

One last habit worth keeping: when the Closing Disclosure arrives three days before settlement, compare it line by line against your estimate and your original Loan Estimate. Fees that grew without explanation are exactly the ones worth a phone call.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are closing costs tax deductible?

Mostly no. The exceptions are discount points on a purchase (deductible if you itemize) and the prepaid property taxes and mortgage interest collected at closing, which count in the year paid. Fees like appraisal, title insurance, and origination aren't deductible, though they do add to your cost basis when you sell.

When do you actually pay closing costs?

At settlement, the day you sign and get the keys. You'll wire the money or bring a cashier's check covering closing costs plus your remaining down payment. Your lender must send a Closing Disclosure with the exact figure at least three business days before, so the final number shouldn't be a surprise.

What happens if you can't afford closing costs?

You have options short of walking away: ask the seller for concessions, take a lender credit in exchange for a slightly higher rate, or look into state and local assistance programs, many of which cover closing costs for first-time buyers. What you can't do on a purchase is simply add them to the loan.
