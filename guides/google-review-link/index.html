How to Get Your Google Review Link (and QR Code It)

⭐ 7 min read📍 For local businesses

Most happy customers don't leave reviews because the path has too many steps: find the business, find the reviews tab, find the button. A review link removes all three, and a QR code on the counter removes even the typing. Here's where the link lives, how to encode it so it never expires, and the arithmetic that tells you how many reviews actually move your stars.

Where is my Google review link?

Three routes, fastest first:

Ad
  1. Business Profile "Ask for reviews." Open your Business Profile Manager, click Ask for reviews, and Google shows a short share link like https://g.page/r/CODE/review. Copy it. This is the link Google built for exactly this purpose.
  2. Place ID deep link. Look up your business in Google's Place ID Finder and copy the identifier (usually 27 characters starting with ChIJ). Your review URL is https://search.google.com/local/writereview?placeid=YOUR_ID.
  3. From a Maps URL. Paste your full Google Maps listing URL into the review QR generator and it extracts the Place ID token for you, then builds the writereview link.

Both link types land on the same screen: your business name, the five-star picker, and an open textbox. The g.page version is shorter (about 41 characters versus about 79), which matters when you're printing the QR small.

Why a QR code beats a link on a sign

A link in a text message is easy for one customer and invisible to the next fifty. The counter reality is that people standing in your shop with a phone in hand will scan a code but won't type a URL, and half of them won't search your name correctly either. The QR bridges that gap in one camera raise.

Two design rules keep it working. First, encode the review link, not your Maps listing: a listing QR makes people find the review button themselves, which is where most of them quit. Second, keep the code static. The QR contains the URL itself, so there's nothing to renew and no scan cap. The 79-character writereview URL produces a 37×37-module code (QR version 5) that prints crisply at 2 cm; the shorter g.page link produces a 33×33 code (version 4) comfortable at 1.4 cm. The Google review QR generator shows the module count live as you paste.

Where should the QR code physically go?

  • Table tents and counters: reading distance under 50 cm, so a 2–3 cm code is plenty.
  • Receipts and invoices: print at least 1.5 cm on matte paper; glossy thermal receipts can glare under store lights.
  • Front door and window: people scan from farther away, so print 5 cm or larger.
  • Business cards: pair it with a vCard code on the back using the business card QR generator — one code saves the contact, the other collects the review.
  • Email signatures and checkouts: use the raw link where a tap works better than a scan.

The math of lifting a star rating

Your rating is just a sum divided by a count, which means you can plan it. Points banked = rating × review count. To hit a target T from count n and sum S, the five-star reviews you need are (T×n − S) ÷ (5 − T), rounded up.

Read the asymmetry twice. Early reviews swing the number hard, a single bad review on a thin rating wrecks it, and past a few hundred reviews the number barely moves. So the strategy is boring and effective: make the ask routine, at the moment of peak happiness, with the least possible friction. What about Google Forms for feedback?

A public review isn't always what you want first. A short Google Form catches the details (what went wrong, what to fix) privately, and you can act on them before they harden into a public one-star. The flow that works: QR to the form at the table or in the follow-up email, then a thank-you page on the form that links out to Google for the happy ones. The same generator encodes forms.gle links, and the form builder covers custom HTML forms when you'd rather own the page.

Is any of this against Google's rules?

Asking: allowed and encouraged. Paying, discounting, or gating: not. Review gating — filtering customers so only the satisfied ever see the Google prompt — violates Google's review policy, and so does a counter tablet that lands everyone on a 5-star prefilled screen. Ask everyone the same honest question, and put the same energy into responding: BrightLocal's latest survey edition puts 80% of consumers as more likely to use a business that responds to all its reviews, and 42% unlikely to use one that never replies.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where is my Google review link?

In your Google Business Profile Manager, click Ask for reviews. The box that opens contains a short link like https://g.page/r/CODE/review that opens your review form directly. Copy it and share it by text, email, or QR code.

What does a Google review QR code do when scanned?

It opens the five-star picker on your listing with one tap, skipping the search step where customers usually drift off. A QR encoding the writereview or g.page link shows your business name, the star selector, and the review textbox immediately.

How many reviews does it take to go from 4.2 to 4.5 stars?

At 4.2 over 35 reviews you hold 147 total points. Each five-star review adds 5 points and 1 count, so you need 21 of them: (147 + 105) / 56 = exactly 4.5. A single one-star review on a 4.8 rating over 50 reviews drops it to 4.73, and it takes 19 five-star reviews to recover.

Is asking for Google reviews against the rules?

No, asking is fine; incentivizing is not. Google's review policy prohibits paying for reviews, offering discounts in exchange, and review gating that filters out unhappy customers before they reach Google. Ask everyone, never reward, and don't run a tablet at the counter that pre-screens for 5 stars.

Why does my rating barely move after new reviews?

Because averages are weighted by count. At 200 reviews your rating has roughly 1,000 points banked, so ten new five-star reviews shift it by about 0.03 stars. Early reviews move the number dramatically; later ones defend it. That asymmetry is why consistent asking matters more than bursts.
