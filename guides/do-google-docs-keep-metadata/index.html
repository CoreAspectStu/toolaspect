Do Google Docs Keep Metadata When You Download Them?

📄 6 min readUpdated November 5, 2026

Mostly no, in the properties at least. The Word, PowerPoint and Excel files we downloaded from Google's public samples had no author field and no dates at all. What can still identify you is inside the document: comments, suggested edits and speaker notes. Here's what we measured, where our test falls short, and how to check your own file.

What metadata stays in a downloaded Google Doc?

Less than you'd get from Microsoft Office. A Word file made in Word normally carries a properties part with an author, a last-modified name and dates. The .docx, .pptx and .xlsx files we downloaded from Google had no properties part at all.

To test it, we took Google's public sample files from its API documentation and exported each one on November 5, 2026. Then we opened every package, searched the XML for author fields, date fields and email addresses, and read the PDFs with pdfinfo.

The limits matter here. We exported without signing in, and these were Google's own samples, so a file you download from your own account could behave differently. It was one file of each type, with no comments or suggestions in them. Treat the table as a strong hint, not a promise, and check your own file with the steps below.

So what can still give you away?

The content. Properties are the part people worry about, but the things most likely to embarrass you are the ones you typed. Our sample deck's speaker notes came through in the .pptx with their text intact, a list of image source links in this case.

Comments and suggestions are the bigger risk, and we couldn't test them on these samples. The best test we found is from the Digital Preservation Coalition, and it's from 2021, so things may have changed:

A name in a comment is the main way a Google download identifies a person. Someone who opens the .docx in Word will see every commenter who's still attached, and possibly a discussion you'd rather they didn't read.

Does opening it in Word add metadata back?

It can. Google's file may start with no author, but Office fills in those fields when someone saves the file. Open the .docx in Word on your own computer, make one small fix and save it, and your Office account name may end up in the properties. We didn't run this, since we don't have Word on our test machine, so check with File > Info after you save. Our guides on Word metadata and PowerPoint metadata cover what to do next.

Photos are a separate question. The images in our sample deck were PNG files with no EXIF data, but they weren't phone photos, so we can't tell you if location data survives for a photo you insert. If that matters, strip it before you add the picture. Our EXIF viewer shows what a photo holds.

How do you check a download yourself?

  1. Download the file and keep it apart from your working copy.
  2. Run it through our Document Metadata Remover, which reads it in your browser with no upload.
  3. For a PDF, open it in our PDF metadata editor, or use File > Properties in your PDF reader.
  4. Open the file like the recipient would, and look for comments, notes, extra tabs and leftover pages.

Here's what our remover did with the Google samples. It reported 0 identifying items for the .docx, .pptx and .xlsx. On the PDF it found 2, the title and the producer, and its cleaned copy no longer showed either in pdfinfo.

That zero needs a warning. It covers properties, not content. The cleaned .pptx still held all five notes parts, and the status line says "cleaned copy removes all of them", which refers only to listed items. Don't read it as a clean bill of health for your deck.

What should you do before you send one?

  • Clear comments. Resolving hides a comment in Google, and we can't promise how every export treats it. Work from a copy and delete what you don't want seen.
  • Accept or reject suggestions. Don't leave them pending in a file that's going outside your team.
  • Empty the speaker notes. In a copy of the deck, check every slide, skipped ones included.
  • Check spreadsheet tabs. A hidden tab is still data. Delete it if the recipient doesn't need it.
  • Read the title. Our PDFs carried the document's title, and "Draft v3 layoffs" isn't something you want to send. It usually names the download as well.
  • Prefer a PDF for reading copies. It's the simpler file, and it left out the notes in our test.

Sending a link is a different choice. Nothing leaves Google, but people with edit access can usually open version history and see who changed what. We haven't tested the permissions, so review them before you share.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a downloaded Google Doc show my name?

It didn't in the properties of the files we tested. Those .docx, .pptx and .xlsx exports had no author field. We tested Google's public samples without signing in, so check your own file. Comments can still carry names.

Do Google Docs comments stay in a Word download?

A 2021 Digital Preservation Coalition test found that .docx exports kept comments with names attached, and kept suggestions too. We couldn't test this on our samples, so try it on a copy first.

Does Google Slides keep speaker notes in a PowerPoint download?

Yes. Our sample deck's .pptx had five notes parts, with the notes text intact. Our metadata remover left them in place, so delete notes by hand in a copy of the deck.

Does a Google Docs PDF have metadata?

A little. Ours had the document title and a producer tag naming the Google Docs renderer, with no author or dates. The Slides and Sheets PDFs named Google as the creator.

Is version history included when you download a Google Doc?

We found no sign of it in our test files, though they didn't have much history to show. It stays in Google Drive. If you share a link instead, people with edit access can usually see it.
