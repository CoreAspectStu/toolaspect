Are QR Codes Safe to Scan?

📄 6 min readUpdated October 15, 2026

Scanning a QR code on its own is almost always safe. A QR code is just text printed as a pattern, and your camera reading it doesn't run anything. The danger is the next tap: the link it opens, the payment page you land on, the Wi-Fi network your phone joins. That's why scammers like QR codes. You can't read the destination with your eyes, so a sticker on a parking meter or a code in an email skips the "does this link look right?" check people have learned to do. Here's what can actually go wrong and how to check a code before you trust it.

Can scanning a QR code hack your phone?

Not by itself in any realistic case today. The camera app decodes the pattern into text and shows you a preview. Nothing happens until you tap it. The rare exceptions have been bugs in how phones displayed that preview: in 2018, a researcher showed the iOS 11 camera could show one domain in its notification and open a different one, which Apple fixed in iOS 11.3. Keeping your phone updated covers that kind of problem.

What a code can do is send you somewhere harmful or set something up on your phone with one tap. That's a social-engineering problem, not a technical one, and it's where all the real losses come from.

What do QR code scams look like?

Security people call it "quishing," QR phishing. The FBI warned about tampered QR codes in a January 2022 public service announcement, and the FTC issued a consumer alert in December 2023. The common setups:

  • Stickers over real codes. Parking meters and pay stations are the classic target. In early 2022 the City of Austin, Texas, found fraudulent QR stickers on its parking pay stations sending drivers to a fake payment site. Restaurant table codes and EV chargers get the same treatment.
  • Codes in email. "Scan to keep your account active" or "Scan to re-enable two-factor login." Corporate email filters check links, but a code is an image, so it can slip through. And because you scan it with your phone, you're off the protected work laptop.
  • Codes on letters and parcels. Fake missed-delivery cards, parking "tickets" and unexpected packages with a code to "track" or "pay."
  • Fake payment requests. A seller on a marketplace sends a code to "receive" payment that actually sends money out.

Almost every one of these adds urgency: a fine, a closing account, a delivery about to be returned. That's the tell. A real parking meter doesn't need you to hurry.

How do I check a QR code before opening it?

Look at the preview, and specifically at the domain, the part right before the first single slash. That's the site you'll actually land on. What's the 10-second check?

  1. Is it a sticker? Run a finger over public codes. A code stuck on top of another printed surface is a red flag.
  2. Read the domain in the preview. Does it match the business? Is it a shortener?
  3. Is there an official app or website? For parking, tolls and bills, type the address yourself or use the app you already have. It's slower, but you know where you are.
  4. Don't enter a password or card number on a page you reached from a code you didn't expect.
  5. Don't scan codes in email asking you to log in or "re-verify." Go to the service directly.

Made a code yourself and want people to trust it? Use a plain, readable destination on your own domain, avoid shorteners, and print the address under the code. I scanned a bad code. What now?

If you only opened the page, close it. That alone is rarely a problem. If you typed a password, change it right away on the real site, and anywhere else you've reused it, then turn on two-factor login. If you entered card details, call your bank and ask for the card to be cancelled and reissued. If you joined a Wi-Fi network, forget it in your settings. Then report it: to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov in the US, and to whoever owns the meter, shop or brand being impersonated, so they can take the sticker down.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you get hacked just by scanning a QR code?

Practically, no. Scanning only decodes text and shows a preview. The risk comes from opening the link, entering details or accepting what the code sets up, such as joining a Wi-Fi network. Keep your phone updated to cover rare preview bugs.

How can you tell if a QR code is fake?

Check whether it's a sticker over another code, then read the domain in the scan preview before opening it. Be suspicious of shortened links, brand names used as subdomains, and any page that rushes you to pay or log in.

Is it safe to scan QR codes at restaurants and parking meters?

Usually, but those are the places scammers put fake stickers. For payments, use the operator's official app or type its web address yourself instead of following the code.

What is quishing?

Quishing is phishing with a QR code. Instead of a clickable link, the scam email, letter or sticker shows a code that leads to a fake login or payment page. Because the link is inside an image, it can get past email link scanners.

Can I check where a QR code goes without opening it?

Yes. Your phone's camera shows the address in a preview before you tap. A QR scanner that decodes to text, like ToolAspect's QR code scanner, shows the full contents and won't open anything unless you choose to.
