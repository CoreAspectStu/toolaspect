How the SAT Is Scored: Raw, Scaled, and Percentiles Explained

💡 25–60K searches/mo, peaking with test dates💰 CPC: $4–12⏱️ 7 min read

You finish a practice test, count your correct answers, and want the number. Getting there takes three hops: raw score to scaled score to percentile. Each hop has a rule, two widespread myths are attached to the middle one (the "curve" and the guessing penalty — one never existed in the current format, the other died in 2016), and understanding the machinery makes practice-test scoring honestly simple.

Step 1: The raw score is just your correct count

The digital SAT splits into two sections, each with two modules. Reading & Writing carries 54 operational questions (27 per module); Math carries 44 (22 per module). Your raw score per section is simply how many you got right.

Wrong answers cost nothing. That's been true since April 2016, when the quarter-point deduction disappeared: a wrong answer and a blank both score zero. The strategy implication is worth internalizing — in the final minute of any module, bubble something on every unanswered question. A 25% chance at a point beats a guaranteed zero.

Step 2: Equating turns raw into scaled (200–800 per section)

Raw scores aren't comparable across test forms — one month's Math module is a touch harder than another's. So College Board pre-calibrates each form with a raw-to-scaled conversion table before the test is ever administered, mapping raw counts onto the familiar 200–800 section scale. Same raw score, same scaled score, regardless of when you tested or who sat beside you.

Two properties of the table surprise people. First, it's not linear: near the middle, one raw point is often worth 10 scaled points, while at the extremes the same raw point can be worth 20 or almost nothing. Second, each form has its own table, which is why "how many can I miss for a 700?" has no universal answer — only per-form answers, like the ones published in official practice-test scoring guides.

Step 3: The total, then the percentile

Add the two section scores and you have the 400–1600 total. The percentile translates it: a 75th-percentile score means you matched or beat about 75% of the comparison group.

Know which percentile you're reading. User group percentiles rank you against actual SAT takers from the past three graduating classes — 1200 is the 75th there. Nationally representative percentiles weight the numbers to reflect all U.S. juniors and seniors, including the many who never take the SAT, so the same score sits a few points higher on that scale. Colleges don't admit "against" either one directly; percentiles are for calibrating your own target.

Is the SAT curved?

No — and this myth refuses to die. A curve would mean your score depends on how everyone else did on your test date. Equating means it doesn't. The conversion table for your form was fixed before test day, calibrated against anchor items so raw 38 in Math buys roughly the same scaled score in any month.

The practical takeaway for practice: don't read anything into one test being "harder." If your practice form's table says raw 52 is 770–800 in Math, that is the equated value on that form. The variation between forms is a question or two of raw allowance, not a moving target you can game by picking test dates.

What changed with the digital SAT?

Since spring 2024 (and 2023 internationally), the SAT runs on Bluebook, shorter and section-adaptive: how you do on module 1 sets the difficulty of module 2. Scoring logic survived the format change intact — raw correct answers, equating, 200–800 per section, no penalty, 1600 max. Two differences matter for interpreting practice scores:

  • Adaptive scoring is computed, not looked up. Bluebook combines your module performance through its scoring model and reports one number, not a table. College Board's paper practice editions bridge the gap: their scoring guides publish the raw-to-scaled ranges this calculator uses, with honest lower–upper bounds instead of false precision.
  • Shorter test, thinner margins. With 44 Math questions instead of 58, each question carries more scaled weight. One careless error moves a section score more than it used to, which raises the value of pacing and error-checking over raw speed.

Setting a target score that makes sense

  1. Pull the middle-50% SAT range (25th to 75th percentile of admitted students) for each school on your list — every admissions page or Common Data Set lists it.
  2. Target the upper half of that range for the schools you care most about.
  3. Take a full official practice test cold, score it with the calculator, and measure the gap.
  4. Budget prep time to the section with the bigger gap; sectional scaled points are interchangeable in the total.

For calibration: recent graduating classes have averaged around 1020–1030, 1200 clears the 75th percentile of test takers, 1400 the 93rd, 1500 the 97th. A 1250 that's comfortably above every school's median on your list beats a 1500 that cost you your junior year. Frequently Asked Questions

What is a raw score on the SAT?

The number of questions you answered correctly. Reading & Writing has 54 operational questions across two modules (the paper practice form has 66), and Math has 44 (54 on the paper form). No points are subtracted for wrong answers, so your raw score can only help you. Raw scores convert to the 200–800 section scale through equating.

Is the SAT curved against other test takers?

No. The SAT is equated, not curved. A curve would score you relative to that day's test takers; equating calibrates each test form's raw-to-scaled table in advance so a raw 38 in Math earns essentially the same scaled score whether you test in August or March. How everyone else does on your test date doesn't move your score.

How many points is each question worth?

It varies by where you sit on the scale, because equating tables aren't linear. On the College Board practice form, going from raw 50 to 51 in Reading & Writing moves the scaled range from 610–630 to 620–640 — about 10 points — while at the bottom, raw 10 to 11 moves 230–250 to 240–260. Every question is worth 1 raw point, but its scaled value depends on the neighborhood.

What SAT score is the 75th percentile?

1200. A 1200 total means you scored equal to or better than about 75% of SAT takers in the user group. Other anchors from College Board's table: 1050 is the 55th percentile, 1400 is the 93rd, and 1500 is the 97th. Nationally representative percentiles (all juniors and seniors, not just test takers) run a few points higher at the same score.
