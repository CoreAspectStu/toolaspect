How to Read Roman Numerals (With Charts and Examples)

⏱️ 6 min readUpdated September 25, 2026

Roman numerals use seven letters: I = 1, V = 5, X = 10, L = 50, C = 100, D = 500 and M = 1,000. To read one, add the values from left to right, except when a smaller letter sits right before a bigger one. Then you subtract it. So VI is 6, but IV is 4. That single rule handles almost everything, and the charts below cover the numbers and years people look up most.

What do the seven Roman numeral letters stand for?

Everything in the system is built from these seven symbols. There's no zero and no place value, which is the big difference from the digits we use every day. In 2,026 the "2" at the front means two thousand because of where it sits. In MMXXVI, each M means a thousand no matter where it appears.

Notice the pattern: the values climb 1, 5, 10, 50, 100, 500, 1,000. Every "one" (I, X, C, M) has a "five" partner (V, L, D) sitting between it and the next one up. Once that clicks, you don't really need to memorize anything else.

How do you read Roman numerals step by step?

Work from left to right and look at each letter next to its neighbor:

  1. If a letter is the same size or bigger than the one after it, add it.
  2. If a letter is smaller than the one after it, subtract it instead.
  3. Keep a running total until you run out of letters.

Try MCMLXXXIV. Break it into chunks first: M, CM, L, XXX, IV. That's 1,000 + 900 + 50 + 30 + 4, which is 1984. Chunking is the trick. Most mistakes come from trying to read a long numeral one letter at a time, when it's really a handful of tidy groups for thousands, hundreds, tens and ones.

Another one: CDXLIV. The chunks are CD (400), XL (40) and IV (4), so it's 444. Three subtractions in a row looks intimidating, but each chunk is just one of six pairs you'll see over and over.

Why is 4 written as IV and not IIII?

Standard modern Roman numerals never repeat a letter more than three times. Instead of IIII, you write one before five: IV. Only six subtractive pairs are allowed, and they always use I, X or C as the small number in front:

PairValueWhy it works
IV45 − 1
IX910 − 1
XL4050 − 10
XC90100 − 10
CD400500 − 100
CM9001,000 − 100

That's the whole list. You can't write IC for 99 or IL for 49, even though the math would work. The small letter has to be no less than a tenth of the big one. So 99 is XCIX (90 + 9) and 49 is XLIX (40 + 9). V, L and D never go in front of anything and never repeat, since VV would just be X.

The Romans themselves weren't this strict. Inscriptions show IIII and even XIIX for 18, and the tidy rules we learn in school were standardized much later. That's also why so many clock faces still show IIII at the four o'clock position. Watchmakers kept the older form, partly for visual balance against VIII on the other side of the dial.

How do you write a year in Roman numerals?

Split the year into thousands, hundreds, tens and ones, convert each part, and join them. For 1994: 1,000 is M, 900 is CM, 90 is XC and 4 is IV, so you get MCMXCIV. For 2026 it's MM + XX + VI, which is MMXXVI. Years since 2000 are pleasantly short. Anything in the 1900s gets long fast because of that CM up front.

The Super Bowl is the best-known holdout. The NFL paused the tradition only once, using "50" instead of L in 2016 because a lone L didn't look right on a logo.

What's the biggest number you can write in Roman numerals?

With the standard rules, the largest is 3,999: MMMCMXCIX. Four thousand would need MMMM, which breaks the three-in-a-row rule, and there's no standard letter for 5,000. That's why most converters, including ours, stop at 3,999.

For bigger values, medieval writers used a bar over a numeral (called a vinculum) to multiply it by 1,000, so a barred V meant 5,000. It isn't used much today, and fonts handle it badly, so treat it as a curiosity rather than something you'll need for a tattoo or a date on an invitation.

There's also no zero. Roman accounting didn't need one, since the abacus handled the arithmetic. When medieval scholars needed to show "nothing" in tables, they sometimes wrote the word nulla or just an N.

How do you check a Roman numeral is written correctly?

This matters most for anything permanent, like engraving, tattoos or a wedding date on a sign. Run through three quick checks:

  • No letter more than three times in a row. XXXX is wrong; it should be XL.
  • Only the six allowed pairs. If you see IC, IL, VX or XM, it's non-standard.
  • Values mostly shrink left to right. Apart from those pairs, bigger letters come first.

Then convert it back to a regular number and make sure you get the date you meant. A converter is the fastest way to do that double check. The bottom line

Learn the seven letters and the six subtraction pairs, then read in chunks: thousands, hundreds, tens, ones. That gets you through any date, clock face or movie credit. For anything you're going to engrave, convert it twice.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you read Roman numerals?

Add the letter values from left to right (I = 1, V = 5, X = 10, L = 50, C = 100, D = 500, M = 1,000). When a smaller letter comes directly before a larger one, subtract it instead. MCMLXXXIV breaks into M + CM + L + XXX + IV = 1,000 + 900 + 50 + 30 + 4 = 1984.

What is 2026 in Roman numerals?

MMXXVI. That's MM for 2,000, XX for 20 and VI for 6.

Why is 4 written as IV instead of IIII?

Modern rules don't allow the same letter more than three times in a row, so 4 is written as one less than five, IV. You'll still see IIII on many clock faces, which is an older form watchmakers kept.

What is the largest number in Roman numerals?

Under standard rules it's 3,999, written MMMCMXCIX. Larger numbers used a bar over the letter to multiply it by 1,000, but that notation isn't standard today.

Is there a zero in Roman numerals?

No. The Roman system has no symbol for zero. Medieval scholars occasionally wrote the word nulla, or just N, when a table needed a value of nothing.
