TinyPNG Alternatives, Compared (Free, Batch and No Upload)

📄 7 min readUpdated October 8, 2026

TinyPNG is the default answer to "how do I make this image smaller", and for a handful of files it's great. People go looking for alternatives when they hit the free caps, when they've got hundreds of images, or when they'd rather not upload client photos to someone else's server. Why do people look for a TinyPNG alternative?

The 5 MB cap. A straight-off-the-camera JPEG from a modern phone or mirrorless body can easily pass 5 MB, and a large PNG screenshot often does too. On the free plan those files just get refused.

Batches bigger than 20. You can keep dropping groups of 20, but for a 400-image product catalog that's 20 rounds of dragging and downloading.

Privacy. Unreleased product shots, medical images, ID scans and client work are often covered by agreements that don't allow third-party uploads, however short the retention.

Control. TinyPNG picks the compression level for you. That's its charm, but sometimes you want to set quality yourself or resize at the same time.

How does TinyPNG make PNGs so small?

A normal PNG can hold millions of distinct colors. TinyPNG reduces that to a much smaller palette, picked so the difference is hard to see, then saves the result as an 8-bit PNG. Fewer colors means far less data, which is why a PNG can drop by half or more. The trade-off is that it's lossy. Smooth gradients and photos can show banding.

That's also why our compressor won't match TinyPNG on PNG-to-PNG. It re-saves PNG losslessly, which only trims a little. You get the big savings by switching the output to WebP, which keeps transparency and is supported by every current browser. If the file has to stay a PNG, use Squoosh with palette reduction turned on, or run pngquant.

Should you just stick with TinyPNG?

If you compress a few images a week, they're under 5 MB and they aren't confidential, TinyPNG is hard to beat for speed. Drop, wait, download. The alternatives earn their place when you need batches, bigger files, specific settings or no upload. Frequently Asked Questions

Is TinyPNG free?

The web version is free for up to 20 images at a time, 5 MB each, with up to 3 format conversions per session. Bigger files and unlimited conversions need a paid Web Pro or Web Ultra plan. The developer API gives 500 free compressions a month.

Does TinyPNG keep my images?

TinyPNG uploads your images to its servers to compress them, and says it keeps them for a maximum of 48 hours before deleting them permanently. If you'd rather a file never left your computer, use a tool that compresses in the browser or a desktop app.

What's the best free TinyPNG alternative for lots of images?

For big batches, a desktop app is the easiest route: ImageOptim on a Mac, or Caesium or XnConvert on Windows and Linux. None of them has a per-session cap. Developers can script pngquant for lossy PNG compression across whole folders.

Why didn't my PNG get much smaller?

PNG is lossless, so re-saving it as PNG only trims a little. TinyPNG gets its big PNG savings by cutting the number of colors, which is a lossy step. To match that, either convert the image to WebP, or use a tool with palette reduction such as pngquant or Squoosh.

Is Squoosh still available?

Yes, Squoosh.app from Google's Chrome team still works in the browser and compresses on your device. It handles one image at a time but gives very fine control over codecs, quality and palette reduction, with a side-by-side preview.
