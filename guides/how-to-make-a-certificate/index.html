How to Make a Certificate

🏅 Document design⏱️ 8 min readFree tool included

Every certificate, from a swimming-level award to a professional designation, is the same seven elements on one landscape page. Get those right and the design almost doesn't matter; get them wrong and no border fixes it. Here's what goes where, the sizes that print cleanly, and three free ways to produce the file.

The seven elements every certificate needs

1. Organization. Top of the page, modest size, often with the logo. It answers "who says so?" 2. The title. CERTIFICATE OF COMPLETION, ACHIEVEMENT, APPRECIATION — the largest text on the page after the name, traditionally letter-spaced. 3. The recipient. The star: largest or second-largest text, always centered, always with clear space around it. 4. The achievement. A short line naming the course, project or award. 5. The date. Written out ("August 31, 2026") reads more formal than 08/31/26 and avoids ambiguity across date conventions. 6. Signature blocks. One or two, each a rule with the signer's printed name and title beneath. Two signatures (program + academic, or director + witness) is the classic institutional layout. 7. A certificate ID. Small footer text with a reference number — it's what makes the document verifiable later.

Paper, points, and margins

Certificates are landscape almost universally. A4 landscape (297 × 210 mm) is the standard outside North America; US Letter landscape (279.4 × 215.9 mm) inside it. In PDF coordinates — which is what any tool is laying out underneath — those are 841.89 × 595.28 and 792 × 612 points respectively, since one point is exactly 1/72 inch and one inch is exactly 25.4 mm. The margin that matters: home and office printers can't usually ink the outer 3-5 mm of the sheet, and certificate frames overlap several more. A 12 mm safety margin clears both comfortably, which is why serious templates put a decorative border 12 mm in and nothing important outside it.

Typography that carries authority

Certificates survive on hierarchy, not decoration. A workable ladder for A4 landscape: organization at ~15 pt with wide letter-spacing, the title at 40-48 pt, the recipient's name at 28-32 pt, the course at 20-24 pt in the accent color, connective lines ("This certificate is proudly presented to") at 10-12 pt, and signature titles at 8 pt. Two typefaces maximum — one for the name and title, one for everything else. Character spacing on the capitalized headings (4-6 points of tracking) is the single cheapest upgrade to how official a certificate looks. And keep the accent palette to one color plus near-black text; the double border (a heavier outer rule, a hairline inner one) does more for the frame than any stock seal.

Three free ways to produce the file

Word templates. File > New, search "certificate", edit the placeholder text, print or export to PDF. Fast, familiar, and fine for a handful. The downsides: moving elements in Word is fiddly, and the exported PDF inherits the template's fonts unless you embed them. Canva. Strong design floor, drag-and-drop, decent free elements — but the free path pushes you toward PNG exports (raster, blurry at print scale) and upsells the background-removal and premium seals. A browser PDF maker. Our PDF Certificate Maker generates a true vector PDF from a form — no watermark, no account — and its optional editor lets you drag elements, add a logo, a drawn signature, or a QR code. Verification: QR codes and certificate IDs

A certificate is a claim; verification is what makes the claim checkable. The lightweight pattern: print a unique ID on the certificate and link a QR code to a page that confirms it — a course-platform profile, a register on your site, even a public spreadsheet for small programs. In the maker's editor, add the barcode element, choose QR, and point it at the verification URL; keep it 20-25 mm square in a corner, outside the signature zone. For high-stakes credentials, course platforms handle signed certificates and revocation lists; for everything else, an ID plus a link is honest and sufficient.

Making many certificates at once

Twenty students? Don't retype twenty forms. Design once, then vary the data: a tag-based approach (a document template with {{name}} placeholders merged against a CSV) turns one layout into a folder of personalized documents.
