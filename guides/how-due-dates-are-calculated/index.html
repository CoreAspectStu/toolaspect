How Your Due Date Is Calculated (and How Accurate It Is)

💡 90K searches/mo💰 CPC: $1⏱️ 7 min read

A due date is simple arithmetic: the first day of your last period plus 280 days. What surprises most people is how loose that number really is. Only about 1 in 20 babies arrive on the day itself. The due date isn't a deadline, it's the midpoint of a four-week window, and understanding how it's built tells you how much to trust it.

Where does the 40-week number come from?

Pregnancy is counted from the first day of your last menstrual period (LMP), not from conception. That feels odd, since you weren't pregnant yet on day one, but the LMP is the one date most people can actually name. Ovulation, and therefore conception, typically happens about two weeks later.

The 280-day figure is Naegele's rule, named for the German obstetrician who popularized it in the early 1800s. It assumes a 28-day cycle with ovulation on day 14: 280 days from the LMP works out to 266 days from conception, or 40 weeks on the calendar. The shortcut version, if you'd rather not count days: take the first day of your last period, subtract three months, add seven days, and add a year.

How do you calculate a due date from your last period?

Say your last period started on March 10. Add 280 days and you land on December 15 of the same year. That's the whole calculation for a textbook cycle. Along the way, weeks are counted from the same starting point, which is why you're called "8 weeks pregnant" when the embryo has existed for about six.

What if your cycle isn't 28 days?

Naegele's rule quietly assumes everyone ovulates on day 14, and plenty of people don't. If your cycles run 33 days, you likely ovulate around day 19, so a due date built on the 28-day assumption lands about five days too early. The fix is simple: adjust by the difference. A 33-day cycle adds 5 days to the standard date; a 25-day cycle subtracts 3.

If your cycles are irregular enough that no single length describes them, the LMP method loses its footing, and an early ultrasound becomes the better dating tool. Tracking ovulation directly helps too; an ovulation calculator can estimate your fertile window from your average cycle length.

How accurate are due dates?

The most accurate dating comes from a first-trimester ultrasound, which measures the embryo from crown to rump. Early embryos grow at a remarkably uniform rate, so between about 8 and 13 weeks that measurement pins gestational age down to within 5 to 7 days. The later the scan, the more individual growth variation creeps in and the wider the error bars get.

That's why providers follow a specific rule for when an ultrasound overrides your period-based date. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) re-dates the pregnancy when the scan and the LMP disagree by more than these margins:

Ultrasound timingDate changes if discrepancy exceeds
Up to 8 weeks 6 daysMore than 5 days
9 weeks to 15 weeks 6 daysMore than 7 days
16 weeks to 21 weeks 6 daysMore than 10 days
22 weeks to 27 weeks 6 daysMore than 14 days
28 weeks and laterMore than 21 days

Once a due date is set by an early scan, it stays set. A baby measuring two weeks ahead at 34 weeks is a big baby, not an older one.

When will labor actually start?

Here's the honest answer the single printed date hides: about 4 to 5 percent of babies arrive on their due date, and roughly 80 percent arrive within the two weeks either side of it. First-time parents tend to deliver a bit later than the average. Doctors describe the finish line in bands rather than a single day:

The bottom line

Your due date is the LMP plus 280 days, adjusted for your cycle length, and possibly corrected once by an early ultrasound. After that it's fixed, and it's best read as the center of a window: eight in ten babies arrive within two weeks of it, and only one in twenty on the day. Use it to pace your preparation, not to schedule your life to a Tuesday.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can my due date change?

Yes, and it usually happens early. If a first-trimester ultrasound disagrees with your period-based date by more than about 5 to 7 days, your provider will move the date to match the scan. After that, the due date is normally locked in; a baby measuring big or small at 32 weeks is a growth finding, not a reason to re-date the pregnancy.

What's the most accurate way to date a pregnancy?

A first-trimester ultrasound that measures the embryo from head to bottom, called crown-rump length. Between roughly 8 and 13 weeks it predicts gestational age within about 5 to 7 days, because embryos grow at a very uniform rate early on. Accuracy drops as pregnancy progresses, which is why later scans rarely change the date.

How is the due date calculated for IVF?

From the transfer date, and it's the most precise dating there is because the embryo's exact age is known. For a day-5 embryo, add 261 days to the transfer date; for a day-3 embryo, add 263 days. No last-period guesswork and no cycle-length adjustment needed.

What percentage of babies are born on their due date?

Only about 4 to 5 percent, roughly 1 in 20. The due date marks the middle of a window, not a deadline. Around 8 in 10 babies arrive in the two weeks either side of it, and first babies tend to run a little later than average.
