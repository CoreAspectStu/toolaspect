How Much House Can I Afford?

💡 165K searches/mo💰 CPC: $11⏱️ 8 min read

Buying a home is the largest purchase most people will ever make. The question isn't what the bank will lend you — it's what you can actually afford without becoming house-poor. This guide walks through the real math behind home affordability.

The 28/36 Rule: The Gold Standard

Mortgage lenders use the 28/36 rule as a baseline for affordability. It works like this:

28% rule: Your total monthly housing payment — principal, interest, property taxes, and homeowners insurance (PITI) — should not exceed 28% of your gross monthly income.

36% rule: All of your monthly debt payments combined — housing, credit cards, student loans, car payments — should stay under 36% of gross monthly income.

These aren't arbitrary. They come from decades of default data. Borrowers who exceed these ratios are statistically far more likely to struggle with payments.

Step-by-Step: Calculate What You Can Afford

  1. Calculate gross monthly income. If you make $75,000/year, that's $6,250/month. Include bonuses and side income if they're consistent.
  2. Apply the 28% cap. $6,250 × 0.28 = $1,750/month for housing (PITI).
  3. Subtract estimated taxes and insurance. Property taxes average 1.1% of home value annually. Insurance runs $100-200/month. For a $300,000 home: ~$275/month taxes + ~$150/month insurance = $425. That leaves $1,325 for principal and interest.
  4. Back into the loan amount. At 7% interest on a 30-year fixed, $1,325/month covers about $200,000 in loan principal. Add your down payment — if you're putting down $60,000, your max home price is roughly $260,000.
  5. Check the 36% debt ratio. If you have a $400 car payment and $300 in student loans, total debt is $1,750 + $700 = $2,450. That's 39% of $6,250 — over the limit. You'd need a cheaper house or lower debts.
  6. Factor in PMI if under 20% down. Private mortgage insurance adds 0.5-1% of the loan amount annually. On a $200,000 loan, that's $1,000-2,000/year ($83-167/month).

Real Example: $80,000 Income, 10% Down

Gross monthly income: $6,667. Housing budget (28%): $1,867/month. After taxes/insurance (~$450), you have ~$1,417 for P&I. At 7%, that supports a ~$213,000 loan. With 10% down ($23,700), max home price: ~$237,000. But PMI adds ~$140/month, reducing your P&I capacity to $1,277, which brings the loan down to ~$192,000. Final max home price: ~$213,000.

Costs Beyond the Mortgage Payment

Many first-time buyers focus only on the mortgage and forget these costs:

HOA fees: $200-500/month in many communities. Some luxury condos charge $800+.

Maintenance: Budget 1-2% of home value annually. On a $300,000 home, that's $250-500/month set aside for repairs.

Utilities: Expect $200-500/month for electric, gas, water, internet — often higher than apartment living.

Closing costs: 2-5% of the purchase price, due at closing. On a $300,000 home: $6,000-15,000.

What Lenders Look At Beyond the Ratio

Banks don't just look at the 28/36 rule. They also consider:

Credit score: Below 620 limits your options. Above 740 gets the best rates. A 100-point difference can change your rate by 0.5-1%, which dramatically affects affordability.

Employment history: Most lenders want 2+ years of stable income in the same field.

Cash reserves: Many lenders want to see 2-6 months of payments in savings after closing.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much house can I afford with a $60,000 salary?

With a $60,000 salary ($5,000/month gross), the 28% rule caps your housing payment at $1,400/month. Depending on rates and down payment, that translates to a home price of roughly $180,000-$220,000.

What is the 28/36 rule for mortgage affordability?

The 28/36 rule means no more than 28% of your gross monthly income goes toward housing costs (PITI), and no more than 36% goes toward all debt combined (housing plus credit cards, auto loans, student loans).

Does the 28/36 rule include property taxes and insurance?

Yes. The 28% covers your full PITI payment: Principal, Interest, property Taxes, and Insurance. HOA fees are also included if applicable.

How much do I need to make to afford a $400,000 house?

To afford a $400,000 home with 20% down at 7% interest, you need roughly $90,000-$105,000 in annual income, assuming minimal other debt.

Is 20% down always required?

No. Conventional loans require as little as 3% down. FHA loans require 3.5%. VA and USDA loans offer 0% down. However, less than 20% means you pay private mortgage insurance (PMI).
