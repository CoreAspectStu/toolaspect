What is engagement rate?

Engagement rate is the share of your audience that interacts with a post — likes, comments, shares, and saves combined, divided by the size of the audience exposed to it. It's the number brands glance at before the follower count, because it tells them whether an audience is alive or just parked there.

The metric earned that status by being hard to fake cheaply. Anyone can buy followers; bought followers don't comment. An account with 80,000 followers and a 0.4% rate is shouting into a void, while 8,000 followers at 6% is a small room paying attention.

How do you calculate engagement rate?

The standard formula divides interactions by followers:

Engagement rate = (likes + comments + shares + saves) ÷ followers × 100

Say an Instagram account with 5,000 followers posts something that earns 200 likes, 40 comments, 30 shares, and 20 saves. That's 290 interactions, so the rate is 290 ÷ 5,000 × 100 = 5.8%. The engagement rate calculator runs this for any platform and rates the result against that platform's benchmark.

One refinement: average before you calculate. A viral post can run five to ten times your baseline and a buried one can halve it, so sample your last 8–10 posts and use the average — the 30-day number analysts quote.

Two patterns explain the spread. Platforms with algorithmic, interest-based feeds — TikTok chief among them — show higher by-follower rates because content travels past the follower graph to strangers. Older graph-based feeds concentrate impressions among existing followers and decay fast, which is why a 2% on X can beat a 6% on TikTok at driving actual outcomes.

So the rule is simple: never compare across platforms. A rate means something only against its own platform's benchmark and your trailing average.

Why does your engagement rate fall as you grow?

Because the denominator races ahead of the numerator. Followers accumulate quickly; per-post interactions don't scale the same way, since newer followers tend to be less invested and no feed shows your posts to everyone. The arithmetic is unforgiving:

Ten times the audience means ten times the interactions needed to hold the same rate. That's why nano and micro accounts routinely post 5–10% by-follower rates while accounts past a million consider 1% healthy. If your rate dips as you grow, that's the math, not necessarily a content failure.

The flip side is that a shrinking denominator flatters you. Purging inactive followers mechanically raises your by-follower rate without a single extra like. Anyone gaming the metric that way hasn't changed their reach or their earnings, which is why serious buyers of sponsorships look at both rate and raw interactions.

Should you use followers or reach as the denominator?

Both exist, and they answer different questions. The by-followers version above is public and comparable — anyone can compute it on anyone. The by-reach version divides interactions by the number of people who actually saw the post, which strips out follower count entirely and measures content quality directly.

If that 290-interaction post reached 1,200 accounts, its by-reach rate is 290 ÷ 1,200 × 100 = 24.2% — a quarter of everyone who saw it did something. Reach-based numbers run high like that, so don't compare them to the follower-based benchmarks in the table above; they're different currencies. Reach lives in your native analytics, which is why public reporting sticks to followers. Track both if you can: when by-reach stays flat while by-follower sinks, your content is fine and your audience composition changed.

Does engagement rate affect what you earn?

Directly. Sponsorship pricing models start from expected interactions, and a higher rate means each post delivers more of them. A 10,000-follower account at 5% (500 interactions a post) can out-earn a 100,000-follower account at 0.5% (also 500) while charging a fraction of the rate — that's the pitch micro-influencers actually win with. How do you raise a low engagement rate?

No tricks, just levers with evidence behind them:

  • Front-load the hook. The first one to three seconds decide whether a video gets watched, and watch time feeds the distribution that feeds engagement.
  • Invite the comment. Ask a specific question, take a mild stance, or leave one thing unfinished — comments are the heaviest-weighted interaction on most platforms.
  • Post when your audience is around. Your analytics shows the hours; alignment buys you the early interactions that decide distribution.
  • Chase the format, not the trend. Platforms over-supply whatever they're pushing — short video, carousels, text posts — and under-supply reach to the rest.

Give any change four to six weeks across 15–20 posts before judging it — one good week proves nothing.

The bottom line

A good engagement rate is ~3% on Instagram, ~5% on YouTube, ~8% on TikTok, ~4% on LinkedIn, ~2% on Facebook, and ~1% on X — with roughly double the average marking excellent on every platform. Compare within a platform, average your last several posts before calculating, and expect the number to sag as you grow. Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good engagement rate on Instagram?

Roughly 3% by followers is an average baseline for Instagram feed posts, so around 4.5% is above average and 6% or more is excellent. Reels often run higher because they reach beyond your follower graph. Compare yourself to your own trailing average first and the platform benchmark second.

How is engagement rate calculated?

Divide total interactions by followers and multiply by 100. Interactions are likes plus comments plus shares plus saves. A post with 290 interactions in front of 5,000 followers has a 5.8% engagement rate. The alternative formula divides by post reach instead of followers.

Why does engagement rate go down as you get more followers?

Because the denominator grows faster than the interactions. Doubling followers rarely doubles per-post engagement: newer followers are typically less invested, and algorithmic feeds show your posts to only a share of them. At 3%, 1,000 followers need just 30 interactions per post while 100,000 followers need 3,000.

Is a 5% engagement rate good?

It depends on the platform. At 5% you are well above average on Instagram, above average on YouTube, and below average on TikTok, where algorithmic distribution pushes by-follower rates toward 8%. Never compare rates across platforms, only against the benchmark for the one you post on.

Do brands care more about followers or engagement rate?

Most sponsorship screening weighs engagement rate heavily, because it signals an audience that actually watches and responds. A 10,000-follower account at 5% and a 100,000-follower account at 0.5% both average 500 interactions per post, and the smaller account usually charges far less to deliver them.
