How Is Property Tax Calculated? Rates, Mills, and Assessments

💡 12K searches/mo💰 CPC: $2⏱️ 7 min read

Every property tax bill in America is the same equation: assessed value times the local tax rate. The complexity, and most of the confusion, hides inside those two terms. Your "assessed" value often isn't what your home is worth, and the rate might be quoted in mills, per $100 of value, or as a percentage, depending on which county you happen to live in.

What's the basic formula?

Three steps, done by your county assessor, not you:

  1. Market value: what the assessor thinks your home would sell for, based on comparable sales, square footage, and condition.
  2. Assessed value: market value times your area's assessment ratio. Some states assess at 100% of market value; others use a fraction. At a 40% ratio, a $350,000 home has an assessed value of $140,000.
  3. Tax: assessed value times the combined rate of every district with a claim on your parcel: county, city, school district, sometimes fire, library, and water districts on the same bill.

Put together: a $350,000 home, a 40% assessment ratio, and a 25-mill combined rate works out to $350,000 × 0.40 × 0.025 = $3,500 a year. Same house, but change either the ratio or the rate and the bill moves.

What's a mill rate?

One mill is one dollar of tax per $1,000 of assessed value, or 0.1%. It's a holdover from Latin millesimum, "thousandth," and local governments love it because small numbers sound painless. If your district's combined levy is 20 mills and your assessed value is $250,000, the bill is $250,000 × 20 ÷ 1,000 = $5,000. To convert any mill rate to a percentage, divide by ten: 20 mills is 2.0%.

Why did my bill go up when the rate didn't change?

Reassessment. Most counties periodically re-estimate market values, and after a hot housing market, assessments jump. A flat 1.1% rate on a home reassessed from $350,000 to $420,000 takes the bill from $3,850 to $4,620 with no vote and no headline. Some states blunt this: California's Prop 13 caps assessed-value growth at 2% a year until the home sells (with a roughly 1% base rate plus local add-ons), and Florida's Save Our Homes cap is 3% for homesteaded owners. In states without caps, the reassessment letter is the tax increase.

How can you lower your property tax bill?

  • Claim your homestead exemption. Most states knock a chunk off the assessed value of a primary residence, but many require a one-time application. Buyers regularly miss it in their first year.
  • Check for senior, veteran, and disability exemptions. These stack on top of homestead in many states, and some freeze assessments entirely past a certain age or income.
  • Appeal the assessment. If the assessor's market value is higher than what your home would actually fetch, file an appeal with recent comparable sales. Deadlines are short, often 30 to 90 days after the assessment notice, but the process is usually free and success lowers every future bill, not just this year's.
  • Fix errors on the property card. Assessors work from records. A card showing four bedrooms when you have three, or finished square footage that doesn't exist, inflates the value automatically.

The bottom line

Property tax is assessed value times the local rate; everything else is vocabulary. What deserves your attention is the effective rate on your actual bill, whether the assessed value is defensible, and whether you're claiming every exemption you qualify for. When you're shopping for a home, run the tax alongside the loan: our mortgage calculator shows how taxes change the full monthly payment, and the closing cost calculator covers the prepaid taxes due at the table. A low sticker price in a 2.4% county can cost more per month than a pricier home across the state line.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is property tax included in my mortgage payment?

Usually, through an escrow account. Your lender collects one-twelfth of the estimated annual tax with each monthly payment and pays the county on your behalf. That's why a mortgage payment can jump even with a fixed-rate loan: the principal and interest never change, but the escrow portion rises when the tax bill or insurance premium does.

What happens if I don't pay property taxes?

The unpaid amount becomes a lien on the home, and it outranks your mortgage. Interest and penalties stack up, and eventually the taxing authority can sell the lien or foreclose, even on a home that's fully paid off. If you're behind, most counties offer payment plans, and applying before the lien sale matters.

Are property taxes deductible?

If you itemize, yes, within the SALT (state and local tax) cap. That cap was $10,000 from 2018 through 2024 and rose to $40,000 starting with the 2025 tax year, phasing back down for incomes above $500,000. Since the standard deduction is large, many homeowners still come out ahead without itemizing at all.

Do renters pay property tax?

Indirectly, yes. The landlord gets the bill and it's priced into rent like any other operating cost. That's one reason rents in high-tax states run higher than home-price differences alone would predict. A few states offer renter credits on state income taxes to acknowledge exactly this.
