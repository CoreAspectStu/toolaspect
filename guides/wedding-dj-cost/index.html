How Much Does a Wedding DJ Cost? 2026 Pricing, Add-Ons, and the Band Question

💡 18K searches/mo💰 CPC: $4.10⏱️ 7 min read

Music is 6% of the average wedding budget, and the DJ is the person holding your entire evening together — the schedule, the toasts, the floor that stays full. Couples pay $1,800 on average for a wedding DJ, per The Knot's 2026 Real Weddings Study, with the 4-hour base running $1,000 to $2,000 for a standard professional. Here's how that number builds, and how to keep it honest.

The base: what 4 hours actually costs

Almost every DJ prices a 4-hour reception package first, and the tier you're buying matters more than the hours:

Tier4-hr priceTypical quoteWhat separates it
Emerging / part-time$500 – $1,200$800Building a portfolio; fine for casual receptions, lighter MC experience
Standard professional$1,000 – $2,000$1,40050+ weddings, own sound and basic lighting, planning meetings
Premium full-time$1,500 – $3,500$2,200Company with backup staff and gear, production-grade lighting

The $1,800 average sits above the standard base for a reason: almost nobody's final bill equals the base. Hours stretch, ceremonies need sound, and walls need uplighting. Budget from the base up, not the headline down, and the number stops surprising you.

The add-ons: where the quote grows

DJ contracts are à la carte after the base. The common lines, at typical pricing:

  • Extra hours: $100–$250 each ($150 typical). A 6 PM-to-midnight reception is six hours, not four — budget $300 more.
  • Ceremony sound + MC: about $300, covering a second system, processional cues, and microphone work.
  • Cocktail-hour coverage: $200 for a separate setup or a second zone.
  • Uplighting: $350 for 10–16 fixtures. The single most transformative non-music dollar in the room.
  • Dance-floor lighting package: $250; monogram gobo $150; karaoke $200; photo booth $500.
  • Travel: usually free within 30 miles, then $100 and up.

Run the standard math: a $1,400 base, six-hour night, ceremony sound, and uplighting lands at $2,350 — which is why "DJ prices" threads are full of confusion. Two couples describing the same DJ can be describing $1,400 or $2,400 bills.

DJ vs live band: the $2,700 question

Bands average $4,500 nationally; DJs $1,800. By size, a 4-hour band quotes roughly $2,200 for a trio, $3,000 for a 4-piece, $4,000 for a 5-piece, $5,500 for a 6-piece, and $7,000 for an 8–10 piece show band. The gap buys real things — live energy, custom arrangements, a performance your guests photograph. What it costs you besides money: set breaks (about 15 minutes an hour), a narrower catalog, and less schedule flexibility when the night runs long.

The honest framing: a band is an upgrade to your entertainment line, and like every upgrade it should come out of another category's budget on purpose. If the band matters more than the plated dinner, make that trade explicitly in your budget instead of discovering it in the final invoice. Contract lines that protect the price

  1. Hours in writing: the contract should name start and end times, what counts against them (setup shouldn't), and the overtime rate for night-of extensions.
  2. The named human: if you're hiring a company, the contract names your DJ or guarantees a qualified substitute. "An available DJ" is how strangers meet at receptions.
  3. Equipment and backup: professional rigs fail rarely, but the contract should promise backup amps, mics, and laptops on site.
  4. The music plan: your must-plays and do-not-plays attached to the contract, not left in a text thread.
  5. The payment schedule: typical is a deposit (often $300–$500) hold your date, with the balance due a week or two before — not in cash at the end of the night.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a wedding DJ cost for 4 hours?

A standard professional charges $1,000 to $2,000 for a 4-hour reception, with $1,400 the typical quote. Part-time and emerging DJs start around $500 to $800, and premium full-time companies run $1,500 to $3,500. The national all-in average, including hours and add-ons, is $1,800.

What makes the final DJ bill higher than the quote?

Overtime hours ($100 to $250 each), ceremony sound with a second system (about $300), cocktail-hour coverage ($200), uplighting ($350), travel beyond 30 miles, and same-night overtime requests. A $1,400 base routinely finishes near $2,350 once a six-hour night and lighting are added.

How much more does a live band cost than a DJ?

Bands average $4,500 against the DJ's $1,800, a $2,700 gap. By size: a 3-piece runs about $2,200, a 5-piece $4,000, and an 8 to 10 piece $7,000 for four hours. Even the smallest band costs about as much as a premium DJ with a full lighting package.

Should I tip my wedding DJ?

Tipping isn't required when you're working with a company owner, but 10 to 15 percent ($180 to $270 on the average bill) is a common thank-you for an employed DJ who nailed the night, and $50 to $150 is fine for solid service you want to acknowledge. Plan it in cash and hand it at the end of the night.

What questions should I ask a wedding DJ before booking?

Five: How many weddings have you done at my venue? Who exactly shows up on the day, and what happens if you're sick? How do you handle requests and a do-not-play list? What's your overtime rate? Is backup equipment on site? The answers separate the $1,400 you're happy to pay from the $800 you regret.
