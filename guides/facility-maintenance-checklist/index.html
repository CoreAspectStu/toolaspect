What Goes in a Facility Maintenance Checklist? Frequencies, Codes, and What Inspectors Ask For

🏢 Facilities⏱️ 7 min readFree tool included

Most facility checklists fail the same way: they list everything anyone might ever do, so nothing gets done consistently. The fix is to organize by frequency and let the code-driven dates form the spine — those are the ones an inspector checks first, and everything else hangs off them. Here's what belongs in each bucket and where the intervals come from.

Why does frequency drive the whole checklist?

Because the people doing the work change, and the schedule is what survives. A checklist grouped by system (all HVAC together, all fire together) forces three trips past the same corridor. Grouped by frequency, the weekly round is one ten-minute walk, the monthly round is one longer walk, and the quarterly-plus items become scheduled contractor visits. For a typical office building with all major systems, that works out to roughly 3 weekly tasks, 11 monthly, 8 quarterly, 5 semiannual, and 17 annual — about 44 recurring items, most of the annual ones owned by outside contractors.

What has to happen weekly?

Two things are genuinely weekly in most buildings: activate plumbed eyewash and shower units to flush stagnant water and prove flow (ANSI Z358.1), and verify that unsupervised sprinkler control valves are open, sealed, and accessible (NFPA 25). Buildings with diesel fire pumps add a weekly churn test. That's the whole list — weekly items need to be few enough that they actually happen every week.

What belongs on the monthly round?

The monthly walk is the workhorse, and it's mostly life-safety:

  • Fire extinguishers: visual check of access, pressure gauge, tamper seal, and condition — NFPA 10 requires it at least monthly, and anyone on staff can do it with a tag or log.
  • Emergency lighting: a 30-second functional test per NFPA 101 §7.9. Thirty seconds catches dead batteries and lamps without draining them.
  • Exit routes: signs illuminated, doors unlocked, aisles clear (OSHA 1910.37).
  • Sprinkler gauges: read and log wet-pipe gauges monthly; dry and preaction systems are weekly.
  • HVAC filters: check monthly, replace every 1-3 months by depth and MERV.
  • Generator: a 30-minute run under load (NFPA 110) where emergency power exists.
  • Housekeeping items: first-aid restock, roof drains and gutters, exterior lamps, and a leak walk through mechanical spaces.

What happens quarterly and semiannually?

Quarterly is when a licensed contractor typically re-enters the picture: waterflow alarm tests through the inspector's test connection, supervised valve and tamper switch checks, and dry-system low-air tests under NFPA 25. In-house quarterly work leans mechanical — AHU belts and bearings, boiler water chemistry, transfer switch exercising — plus envelope and pavement walks. Semiannual is dominated by kitchen systems: hood suppression service and hood/duct cleaning run every six months under NFPA 96 for most cooking operations, and seasonal HVAC changeover with coil cleaning lands here too.

What's in the annual bucket?

This is the budget line. A mid-size building's annual stack usually includes the full sprinkler ITM visit with a main drain test (NFPA 25), the fire alarm test (NFPA 72), extinguisher maintenance by a certified technician (NFPA 10), the 90-minute emergency lighting test (NFPA 101), fire door inspections (NFPA 80), backflow preventer tests, the generator load-bank test (NFPA 110), an infrared scan of switchgear (NFPA 70B), jurisdictional boiler inspection, professional roof inspection, and the full ANSI Z358.1 eyewash evaluation. Together these typically run a few thousand dollars a year — and they're the first thing an insurance carrier or fire marshal asks to see.

What records does an inspector actually ask for?

Dates and initials, kept somewhere findable. The NFPA standards are consistent about this: the monthly checks and periodic inspections must be documented, records retained (NFPA 25 asks for a minimum of one year of routine ITM records), and annual certifications kept with the device or in the compliance binder. Electronic logs are fine; the failure mode is always the same — tests done but not recorded, which reads the same as tests never done. The bottom line

Anchor the checklist to the code-driven dates — extinguishers monthly, emergency lighting monthly plus the annual 90-minute test, sprinkler alarms quarterly, eyewash weekly — and let the mechanical plant slot in around them. Sign everything, keep the log where the next person can find it, and the checklist becomes the cheapest insurance policy the building owns.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the legal minimums in a building maintenance checklist?

The code-driven spine: monthly extinguisher visual inspections (NFPA 10), monthly 30-second plus annual 90-minute emergency lighting tests (NFPA 101 §7.9), sprinkler gauge readings and quarterly waterflow alarms (NFPA 25), annual fire alarm testing (NFPA 72), annual fire door inspections (NFPA 80), weekly eyewash activation (ANSI Z358.1), and monthly generator run tests (NFPA 110) where emergency power exists. Which edition applies locally depends on the codes your state and fire marshal have adopted.

How long does a monthly facility walkthrough take?

For a mid-size office building, the monthly round is usually 30 to 60 minutes if nothing turns up: extinguisher gauges, exit signs, a 30-second emergency light test, filter checks, a leak walk, and exterior lighting. Weekly rounds run about ten minutes. The annual bucket is different — most of those tasks belong to licensed contractors and are scheduled visits, not walkthroughs.

Who can perform fire equipment inspections?

The monthly extinguisher visual check and the monthly/annual emergency lighting tests can be done by trained in-house staff — the standard requires the checks and the records, not a credential. The annual extinguisher maintenance, sprinkler ITM, and fire alarm inspection require qualified, and in many jurisdictions licensed, fire protection contractors. Eyewash weekly activation is in-house; the annual Z358.1 inspection can be in-house if someone knows the criteria.
