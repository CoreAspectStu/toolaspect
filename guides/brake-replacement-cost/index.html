Brakes price by axle and by depth of the job. At the ~$130/hr national median shop rate: front pads alone run $200-$388, pads plus rotors run $445-$825 on the front axle and $501-$901 on the rear, and doing pads and rotors on all four corners lands at $946-$1,726. A seized caliper adds $90-$280 plus an hour. Here's the full per-axle map — parts, flat-rate hours, and the shop-rate math — so you can tell a fair quote from a padded one.

Pads, rotors, or both — how do you know?

Every rotor has a minimum thickness cast into it, and a shop measures it with calipers the moment the wheels come off. Three honest outcomes:

  • Above spec, smooth, no pulsation: pads alone, or resurface the rotors at $10-$40 each plus about 0.3 hour to clean up the surface. Cheapest legitimate job.
  • Near the discard spec: pads plus rotors. By the second pad set, most rotors are close — and new pads on worn rotors wear unevenly, pedal soft, and can overheat.
  • Scored, heat-cracked, or pulsing: rotors, no discussion. Pulsation means thickness variation that machining may or may not fix.

Be suspicious of a pads-only quote at a shop that never measured the rotors, and equally of a pads-plus-rotors quote on a car with 20,000-mile-old rotors above spec. Ask for the measured thickness against the stamped minimum — honest shops write it on the estimate.

Why do rear brakes cost more than fronts?

Rear pads actually work less — fronts do 60-70% of the braking — so the parts premium isn't wear, it's architecture. Rear rotors frequently carry the parking brake: either an electronic caliper that needs a scan-tool retraction (and sometimes a $50-$150 recalibration), or a drum-in-hat shoe system inside the rotor that has to be adjusted and can be rusted solid. That adds $30-$50 of parts and 0.2 hours of labor, which is exactly the gap between the front and rear columns in the table.

When does a caliper need replacing?

Calipers fail two ways: a piston seizes (car pulls, one pad wears to metal, wheel gets hot) or the bore leaks. Neither is mileage maintenance — they're event-driven, usually after 100,000 miles or in salt-belt states. Pricing a single caliper honestly: $90-$280 in parts plus 0.8-1.2 hours, which on top of a front pads-and-rotors job means $639-$1,261 for that axle. Replacing calipers in pairs is the usual recommendation for even braking, though a failed caliper at 60,000 with a healthy opposite side is defensibly a one-sided fix — ask which situation you're in.

Dealer, chain, or independent — what's the difference?

Brakes are commodity work with flat-rate book hours every competent shop knows. The rate does all the separating: independents at $90-$150/hr, chains at $90-$140, dealers at $120-$190. On a front pads-and-rotors job (1.5-2.5 hours), $115/hr bills $423-$788 against $483-$888 at a dealer's $155 — about 12%. A good independent with ceramic-pads-upgrade options is the value play; the dealer earns its premium on warranty work and diagnostics, not wear items.

How do you keep a brake bill down?

  • Ask for the measurements. Rotor thickness vs stamped minimum, pad thickness in millimeters — on paper. Estimates get honest when they're specific.
  • Match parts to the car. A commuter doesn't need premium ceramic; a performance car shouldn't run economy semi-metallic. The middle tier is right for most.
  • Do axles only when axles are due. Fronts commonly wear twice as fast as rears; "all four corners every time" is a pattern, not a rule.
  • Watch the resurface upsell. Machining is legitimate above spec but thin rotors machined near the minimum warp early — replacement parts are cheaper than they used to be.
  • Don't ignore the squeal. The noise indicator exists so you fix pads before the backing plate eats the rotor — the difference between a $388 job and an $825 one.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much should brakes cost per axle?

Front pads alone run $200-$388 installed at a $130/hr shop rate; pads plus rotors run $445-$825 front and $501-$901 rear. Published averages agree: RepairPal puts pad replacement at $331-$390 and rotor replacement at $572-$695, and AAA pegs basic brake work at $150-$350 per axle. A single seized caliper adds $90-$280 in parts and 0.8-1.2 hours on top.

How much does a full brake job cost — both axles?

Pads and rotors on all four corners runs $946-$1,726 at $130/hr — $530-$1,050 in parts across 3.2-5.2 flat-rate hours. Add a caliper per axle and you're near $1,300-$2,500. Luxury vehicles and electronic parking brakes push each band up 10-30% plus a $50-$150 recalibration step.

Why did my brake quote include rotors when I only asked for pads?

Rotors carry a minimum thickness stamped on them, and pads sitting on worn or scored rotors wear out fast and pedal poorly. If the rotors are above spec and smooth, the shop can resurface them for $10-$40 each instead of replacing — but by the second or third pad set, most rotors are at or near discard and replacement is the honest quote.

How often do brakes need replacing?

Pads last roughly 30,000-70,000 miles depending on driving style and compound; front pads wear two to three times faster than rears because fronts do most of the braking. Rotors typically survive two pad sets. Grinding, squealing that persists after a few stops, a pulsing pedal, or the brake warning light means don't wait for a mileage number.
