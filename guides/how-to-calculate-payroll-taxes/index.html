How to Calculate Payroll Taxes: FICA, FUTA, and Withholding

💡 9K searches/mo💰 CPC: $8⏱️ 8 min read

Every paycheck carries two sets of taxes: the ones printed on the stub, and a nearly identical set the employer pays that the employee never sees. Get them straight and payroll stops being mysterious. The employee side is FICA plus income tax withholding; the employer side is a matching FICA payment plus unemployment taxes. Here's the whole system with a worked example.

What comes out of an employee's paycheck?

Three deductions, in order of predictability:

  • FICA: 7.65% of gross wages. That's 6.2% for Social Security and 1.45% for Medicare. The Social Security piece stops at the annual wage base, $168,600 in 2024 (rising to $176,100 in 2025), which caps the employee's 2024 Social Security tax at $10,453.20. Medicare never caps, and wages above $200,000 pay an extra 0.9% surtax.
  • Federal income tax withholding. Estimated from the W-4, annualized income, and the bracket tables. Unlike FICA's flat rate, this one is progressive, so the first dollars are taxed at 10% and later dollars at higher rates.
  • State income tax, anywhere from zero (Texas, Florida, and seven others) to double digits for high earners in California.

Here's the full calculation for a single filer paid biweekly, using the 2024 standard deduction of $14,600, a 5% state tax, and no 401(k), the same math our payroll calculator runs. Taxable income is $60,000 − $14,600 = $45,400, which spans the 10% and 12% federal brackets:

What does the employer pay on top of salary?

This is the half employees rarely see. For every worker, the employer separately owes:

  • Matching FICA: another 7.65% of gross wages, same caps as the employee side.
  • FUTA (federal unemployment): 6.0% on the first $7,000 of each employee's annual wages. Pay your state unemployment tax on time and a 5.4% credit drops the effective rate to 0.6%, a maximum of $42 per employee per year. Nearly every employer qualifies for the credit.
  • SUTA (state unemployment): varies by state and by the employer's layoff history, typically on the first $7,000 to $50,000+ of wages depending on the state.

How does a 401(k) change the math?

Pre-tax 401(k) contributions reduce income tax but not FICA. Defer 5% of a $60,000 salary ($3,000) and federal taxable income drops to $42,400, saving $360 a year at the 12% marginal rate, plus $150 of the 5% state tax where 401(k) money is state-deductible. But you'll still pay the full $4,590 of FICA, because Social Security and Medicare are levied on gross wages before the deferral. Plenty of people expect a 5% contribution to shrink their check by 5%; it actually shrinks it by less, about 4.1% in this example, which makes contributing cheaper than it looks.

What are the common payroll mistakes?

  • Misclassifying employees as contractors. A 1099 contractor pays both halves of FICA themselves, 15.3% self-employment tax. The IRS looks at control and independence, not what the contract says, and reclassification comes with back taxes and penalties.
  • Forgetting the additional Medicare tax. Employers must withhold the extra 0.9% once an employee's wages pass $200,000, regardless of the employee's filing status.
  • Missing deposit deadlines. Withheld taxes are trust funds, not working capital. The penalty for late federal deposits scales to 15%, and responsible individuals can be personally liable.
  • Assuming bonus withholding is the final tax. The flat 22% supplemental rate is only a withholding method; the real tax settles at filing.

The bottom line

Employee-side payroll tax is simple arithmetic: 7.65% FICA plus bracket-based withholding on what's left after pre-tax deductions. The employer mirrors the FICA and adds unemployment taxes that never appear on a pay stub. If you're an employee, the interesting number is your net percentage; on $60,000 here, take-home is about 78.7% of gross. If you're hiring, budget salary times 1.08 as the tax-only floor and more like 1.3 all-in. Frequently Asked Questions

What percentage is FICA?

7.65% of gross wages for the employee: 6.2% for Social Security plus 1.45% for Medicare. The employer pays a matching 7.65%, so the government actually collects 15.3% on most wages. Social Security's 6.2% stops at the annual wage base ($168,600 in 2024, $176,100 in 2025); Medicare's 1.45% has no cap, and an extra 0.9% employee-side surtax kicks in on wages above $200,000.

Do employers match all payroll taxes?

No, only FICA. The employer matches your 6.2% Social Security and 1.45% Medicare dollar for dollar. Federal and state income tax withholding comes entirely out of your pay; the employer just forwards it. FUTA and SUTA unemployment taxes run the other way: employers pay those alone in almost every state.

Why is my bonus taxed at 22%?

It isn't, exactly. The IRS lets employers withhold a flat 22% on supplemental wages like bonuses (for amounts up to $1 million) instead of running them through the regular withholding tables. Your actual tax on the bonus is whatever your real bracket says when you file, so the flat withholding trues up as a larger refund or balance due in April.

What is SUTA and why does my rate change?

State unemployment tax, paid by employers on a state-set wage base. New employers get a standard rate, often 2-4%, and after a few years the state assigns an experience rating: lay off a lot of people whose claims hit your account and your rate climbs; keep a stable workforce and it falls. Paying SUTA on time also earns the credit that cuts federal FUTA from 6.0% to 0.6%.
