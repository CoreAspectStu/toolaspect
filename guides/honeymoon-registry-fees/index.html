Honeymoon Registry Fees: Who Takes What, and How to Keep It

💳 Wedding fintech⏱️ 7 min read📊 $5,000 fund worked end to end

A honeymoon registry turns gift money into snorkeling and sunset dinners, which guests genuinely prefer to a fourth set of towels. The catch is the fine print: platform fees run from effectively 0% to about 9.65%, and the difference on a typical $5,000 fund is $482.50. Here are the published fee schedules as of mid-2026, who actually pays each fee, and the three moves that keep the spread in your trip instead of a processor's pocket.

The per-gift trap nobody prices

Percentage comparisons hide the fixed fee. Honeyfund's card path at "3.5% + $0.59" sounds close to Zola's 2.5% until you multiply the $0.59 by your gift count: 50 gifts add $29.50, pushing the effective rate from 3.5% to 4.09%. The math tilts further with many small gifts. A fund of 100 gifts averaging $50 pays $59 in fixed fees alone on top of the percentage; the same dollars in 25 gifts pay just $14.75. If your crowd gives many small amounts, the fixed fee matters as much as the percentage.

Who pays is a real choice, not fine print

  • Guest-pays (Honeyfund default): the card fee rides on the gift, so a $100 gift costs the giver about $104.09. Guests can always decline and hand you a check, which is the free path.
  • Couple-absorbs (Zola, Traveler's Joy option): the fee comes off the top of your payout. Smoother etiquette, and it makes the cost visible to you, which is honest.
  • Resort-bundled: the service fee is baked into the package price. You never see a line item, which is exactly why it can reach 9.65% without anyone objecting.

Three moves that keep the spread

  1. Put the free rail first. Order your registry options so direct transfer or offline appears above card payments. Most guests click the first button; make it the free one.
  2. Name the fund in experiences, not dollars. "$100 Sunset Sail" reads as a gift; "help fund our honeymoon" reads as an invoice. Experiences also make the card fee feel like a service guests opt into.
  3. Match the platform to your guests. A crowd of Venmo-native friends costs you nearly nothing on peer-payment rails. A crowd of check-writing aunties is Honeyfund's classic free model. Resort-linked registries only earn their 9.65% if you genuinely want the concierge hand-holding.

What the fees buy, honestly

Card processing alone costs 2.2% to 2.9% plus roughly $0.30 in the open market, so a registry at 2.5% to 2.95% is passing through processing at close to cost. That's fair. What isn't cost is the service markup layered on top by older and resort-linked platforms, and that's where 7% to 9.65% comes from. If a platform's fee is more than a point above card-processing cost, you're paying for setup help and pretty pages, so decide whether you need them.

Frequently Asked Questions

What percentage do honeymoon registries take?

Published fee schedules as of mid-2026 run from 0% to about 9.65%. The free paths are Honeyfund offline gifts and platforms that pay out on peer-payment rails like Venmo. Mainstream card-processing registries sit between 2.5% (Zola) and 2.95% (Traveler's Joy), while resort-linked services near 9.65% top the market. On a $5,000 fund that's a $482.50 spread.

Is a honeymoon registry worth the fees?

Usually yes, and often at near-zero cost. Guests already give cash at weddings; a registry just routes it with a story attached. If you pick a platform with a free payout option and treat card fees as a convenience some guests will still choose, your effective cost can land within a few dollars of zero.

Do guests pay more when a registry charges a fee?

Sometimes. On Honeyfund, the card fee sits on the guest side by default, so a $100 gift costs the guest about $104.09. On Zola and Traveler's Joy, the couple chooses to absorb the fee or pass it along. Etiquette-wise, absorbed fees are smoother; transparently optional fees are acceptable when guests can decline and give another way.

How do I avoid honeymoon registry fees entirely?

Route guests to free rails: registry platforms with direct bank transfer or offline gifts (Honeyfund's classic model), or peer payments like Venmo, CashApp, and PayPal that some platforms process at no fee. The trade-off is friction, since guests must leave the one-click card flow. Put the free option first and the card option second, and most guests follow the cheapest path.
