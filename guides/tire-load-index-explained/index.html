What the load index is

A coded maximum, set by the ETRTO and Tire & Rim Association, for how much weight one tire can carry at its maximum inflation pressure. It's a lookup, not a formula — each step doesn't add a fixed number of pounds, which is why you need the chart. The everyday range for passenger vehicles runs from about 75 (853 lb, subcompacts) through 104 (1,984 lb, half-ton pickups), while hard-working LT and trailer tires climb to 121 (3,197 lb) and beyond.

Two rules govern everything else. Multiply a single tire's rating by four to get your tires' total capacity, and that total must beat your vehicle's fully loaded weight. And never fit a lower index than the one printed on your door-jamb placard — the manufacturer already did the math for the worst case.

How to compute the index you actually need

One division: (curb weight + people + cargo) ÷ 4, then pick the index that clears it. Example: a 4,400 lb SUV carrying 1,200 lb of family and luggage sits at 5,600 lb gross, or 1,400 lb per tire. Index 92 (1,389 lb) fails; index 93 (1,433 lb) passes. The factory 102s carry 34% headroom over that requirement — exactly the margin a loaded vacation trip or a roof box full of gear eats into.

For trucks that tow, run the same math on the truck's own loaded weight (not the trailer's — trailer tires carry the trailer's weight). And when a placard specifies LT tires, replace with LT: the door placard assumed the higher pressures and reinforced construction.

Speed ratings: the letter's real job

The letter certifies sustained-speed durability: S = 112 mph, T = 118, H = 130, V = 149, W = 168, Y = 186. Since US speed limits sit far below even the lowest ratings, the practical meaning isn't legality — it's that higher-rated tires use stiffer construction that changes steering response, and that running the rated speed on an underinflated or overloaded tire is how heat failures happen. Match the letter across all four corners; mixing front-to-rear handling characteristics is how a budget upgrade becomes a handling downgrade.

The LT vs P-metric trap

Same size, same printed load index, different reality. A P235/75R15 carries 1,984 lb — but only at 35 psi, and only in passenger-car duty. Fit it to a pickup, a hauling SUV, or a trailer, and the standards require dividing its capacity by 1.10: 1,984 → 1,804 lb. Meanwhile the LT235/75R15 in load range C carries 1,985 lb at 50 psi, no derating, with sidewalls built for the abuse. On paper the tires look identical; in truck service the LT simply carries more, longer, hotter.

The load-range letters on LT tires map to the old ply ratings: B = 4-ply at 35 psi, C = 6-ply at 50, D = 8-ply at 65, E = 10-ply at 80, F = 12-ply at 95. If your truck placard says LT with a load range, that letter is part of the spec — an "equivalent" P-metric isn't.

Buying rules that keep you safe

  • Match or exceed the placard's size, load index, and (for LT) load range. Higher index is fine; lower never is.
  • Keep all four tires at the same index, size, and speed rating.
  • P-metric on a truck or trailer? Derate 10% in your head before trusting the sidewall number.
  • Run the pressure on the door placard, not the sidewall max — the index applies at max pressure, but the placard pressure is where load, wear, and handling were engineered.
  • Replacing one tire? Match the other three exactly, or replace in pairs with the new pair on the rear.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does 102H mean on a tire?

It's the service description: load index 102 and speed rating H. The tire carries a maximum of 1,874 lb (850 kg) at its maximum inflation pressure, and is certified for sustained speeds up to 130 mph. Four such tires support up to 7,496 lb.

How do I calculate the load index I need?

Take your vehicle's curb weight, add passengers and cargo, and divide by four — each tire needs a rating above that per-tire number. A 5,600 lb loaded SUV needs 1,400 lb per tire, so load index 93 (1,433 lb) is the floor. In practice, just match or beat the index on the door-jamb placard.

Why do P-metric tires lose capacity on trucks?

Tire and Rim Association standards require P-metric max load to be derated by dividing by 1.10 (about 9%) when the tire is fitted to a light truck, SUV used for hauling, or trailer, because those duty cycles are harsher than the passenger-car standard the rating assumes. A P235/75R15 rated 1,984 lb becomes about 1,804 lb in truck service — often less than the same-size LT tire it replaced.

What load index do trailer tires need?

Divide the trailer's fully loaded gross weight by the number of tires. A 7,000 lb tandem-axle trailer needs 1,750 lb per tire — load index 100 minimum, though ST tires are usually chosen by load range instead. Never fit passenger tires to a trailer; special trailer (ST) tires have stiffer sidewalls built for that duty.
