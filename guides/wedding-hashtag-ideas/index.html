How to Make a Wedding Hashtag: 6 Formulas That Work

💍 ~10K searches/mo (est.)🎉 Evergreen⏱️ 6 min read

Every good wedding hashtag is built, not brainstormed. There are only six formulas that consistently work — the mashup, the classic join, the family plural, the pun, the first-name lock, and wedding words — and your two surnames decide which ones fit. Here's each one, what it looks like with real names, and how to test a winner before it goes on the napkins.

Formula 1: The surname mashup

Take one full surname and graft on the other's last syllable. Smith + Johnson becomes #Smithson. Starling + Moss cross-pollinates into #Mossling. Newman + Young gives #Youngman. This is the formula behind the celebrity portmanteaus everyone remembers, and it works for a boring reason: the output reads like a plausible surname, so it feels like it was always destined.

The mechanics: find where each name's final syllable starts (usually the consonant before the last vowel), then splice. Not every pair blends cleanly — if the result trips your tongue or looks like a typo, drop to Formula 2. The generator below tries both splice directions so you can compare.

Formula 2: The classic join

#SmithJohnson. Both names, in full, in one tag. It never wins originality contests, but it's the easiest for guests to type, the easiest to remember, and the one your great-aunt will actually use correctly. If both orders sound fine, mint both and let the feed sort it out.

Formula 3: The family plural

#TheSmiths, #MeetTheJohnsons, #TheBerries. This formula announces the merger, not just the wedding, which is why it shows up on so many welcome signs. Watch the spelling rules: names ending in -y usually flip to -ies (Berry → Berries), and names ending in s, x, z, ch, or sh take -es (Moss → Mosses).

Formula 4: The pun

The highest-risk, highest-reward formula, and the only one that depends on what your names secretly sound like. The patterns that pay off most often:

  • -son names: #JohnAndOnly (one and only), #TheJacksonContinues
  • -bell: #ForWhomTheCampbellTolls, #SavedByTheBell
  • -wood: #WoodYouBeMine, #OutOfTheWoods
  • -berry: #HappilyEverBerry
  • -love: #AllYouNeedIsLove (lucky you)
  • -day: #SaveTheDay, #ADayToRemember
  • -moon, -star: #OverTheMoon, #StarStruck

Generic puns work on any surname too: #SmithAndOrder, #EatDrinkAndBeSmith, #SmithHitched, #ToHaveAndToSmith. The bar is simple — a pun earns its place when guests smile, not groan. Run yours past one bridesmaid before printing five hundred napkins.

Formula 5: First names

#AlexAndJordanForever, #EmmaAndLiamSayIDo. The move when surnames are long, hyphenated, unspellable, or identical (two Smiths don't need a mashup). It's warmer than the join and it sidesteps the taken-tag problem almost entirely.

Formula 6: Wedding words

Bolt a wedding phrase onto either name: #SmithJohnsonHitched, #TheSmithsSayIDo, #FromThisDaySmith. This is the fix when the clean versions of your tag are already taken — the added word makes yours unique without inventing a new pun.

How to test a hashtag before you commit

  1. Search it on Instagram, TikTok, and Facebook. Read the recent posts, not the total count. A dormant tag is fine; an active couple from last spring is not.
  2. Type it on someone else's phone. If autocorrect fights it or it takes three tries, shorten it or change formulas.
  3. Say it out loud as a sentence. "Did you see the photos under #Smithson?" should sound natural.
  4. Check the typo tolerance. Guests will misspell long tags (#MichaelsonFoster attracts #MichaelsonFoster). Shorter tags collect more photos.
  5. If it's contested, add the year or city. #Smithson2026 is instantly unique and self-dating in a good way.

Where to publish it

Digital first: wedding website, Instagram bio, the invite's details card. Physical at the venue: a welcome-table sign, table numbers, napkin or stirrer cards, the photo-booth strip, the guest book page. The pattern that works is repetition — one big banner at the entrance gets photographed once and forgotten by the first toast, while the hashtag printed on the cocktail napkin in your hand gets typed into a phone at exactly the moment photos get taken.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good wedding hashtag formula?

The most reliable formula is one surname plus the other surname's last syllable: Smith + Johnson → #Smithson, Starling + Moss → #Mossling. It works because the result reads like a plausible surname. Keep the classic join (#SmithJohnson) as a backup if the blend comes out clumsy.

How do I know if my wedding hashtag is taken?

Search the exact tag on Instagram, TikTok, and Facebook and read the recent posts, not just the post count. A few scattered posts from years ago won't hurt you; an active couple using it now will. If it's contested, append your year (#Smithson2026) or city.

Should both last names go in the hashtag?

Not always. Long or hard-to-spell surnames push couples toward first names (#AlexAndJordanForever) or a single-name pun. The test is whether a guest can type it from a bar napkin after two drinks without autocorrect mangling it.

Where do we actually tell guests the hashtag?

On the wedding website and invitations first, then physical reminders at the venue: a sign at the welcome table, napkin or stirrer cards, the guest book page, and the photo-booth footer. Guests forget hashtags within an hour unless something in front of them repeats it.
