Heart Rate Zones Explained

❤️ 7 min readUpdated Aug 2026

The Karvonen formula sets your training zones from heart-rate reserve: (max HR − resting HR) × intensity% + resting HR. For a 35-year-old resting at 60 bpm, that makes Zone 2 exactly 135–148 bpm. Two inputs, five zones, and a training system that adapts as your resting rate drops. Here's how the numbers work and what each zone is for.

Why zones exist at all

Your body has roughly three engines: phosphocreatine for seconds-long bursts, glycolysis for hard efforts lasting a few minutes, and the aerobic system for everything longer. Heart-rate zones are a proxy for which engine you're loading. Train too hard on an easy day and you blunt recovery without adding fitness; drift too easy on a hard day and you never stress the top end. Zones keep each session honest.

The Karvonen formula, worked

Three steps. Estimate max HR: 220 − age, or Tanaka's 208 − 0.7 × age for a better fit past 40. Subtract your resting HR to get heart-rate reserve, the headroom your heart can actually expand into. Then each zone boundary is reserve × intensity + resting HR.

For the 35-year-old at 60 bpm resting: max 185, reserve 125.

Zone% reserveCalculationbpm
Z1 Recovery50–60%125 × 0.50 + 60 → 125 × 0.60 + 60123–135
Z2 Aerobic base60–70%125 × 0.60 + 60 → 125 × 0.70 + 60135–148
Z3 Tempo70–80%125 × 0.70 + 60 → 125 × 0.80 + 60148–160
Z4 Threshold80–90%125 × 0.80 + 60 → 125 × 0.90 + 60160–173
Z5 VO2 max90–100%125 × 0.90 + 60 → max173–185

The percentages-of-reserve detail is what makes Karvonen worth the extra input. Consider two 40-year-olds, both with a 180 max. One is new to exercise and rests at 72; the other has trained for years and rests at 48. A %-of-max chart hands them both a Zone 2 of 108–126 bpm. For the veteran that band is 45–59% of reserve, close to right. For the beginner it's just 33–50% of their smaller reserve, barely above a brisk walk. Karvonen splits them properly: Zone 2 lands at 137–148 for the beginner and 127–140 for the veteran, each at the same honest relative effort.

Getting your resting heart rate right

Measure before standing up in the morning, count a full 60 seconds, and average three to five mornings. Adults typically rest between 60 and 100 bpm; regular exercisers in the 50s; endurance athletes in the 40s. Skip mornings after alcohol, illness, or four hours of sleep. And here's the quiet payoff: as your aerobic base builds, resting HR falls, your reserve widens, and every zone shifts. Recalculate monthly and watch the numbers move.

How to actually train in the zones

  • The 80/20 split. Endurance athletes spend roughly 80% of weekly time in Zones 1–2 and 20% in Zones 4–5. The classic mistake is the Zone 3 trap: moderate-hard every day, absorbing fatigue without enough stimulus in either direction.
  • Zone 2 discipline. The talk test is the cheapest governor: full sentences means Zone 2; single words means Zone 4. Most people who "always run easy" actually live in Zone 3.
  • Cardiac drift. Holding the same pace, your HR climbs 5–10 bpm over an hour as you dehydrate and heat up. On long Zone 2 sessions, slow down to stay in band; that's part of the training.
  • Health baseline. For general health, 150 minutes a week of Zone 2–3 activity plus two strength sessions matches the national physical-activity guidelines. No Zone 5 required.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the five heart rate zones?

Zone 1 (50–60% of heart-rate reserve) is recovery. Zone 2 (60–70%) is the conversational aerobic base. Zone 3 (70–80%) is tempo. Zone 4 (80–90%) is threshold, where lactate accumulates. Zone 5 (90–100%) is VO2 max work. Each zone stresses a different energy system, which is why training programs mix them instead of living in one.

Why is Karvonen better than percent of max heart rate?

Because it accounts for resting heart rate, a direct marker of aerobic fitness. Two 40-year-olds share a max of 180, but one rests at 72 and the other at 48. Plain %-of-max gives them identical Zone 2 bands (108–126 bpm), which lands at 45–59% of reserve for the veteran but only 33–50% for the beginner, too easy to stimulate change. Karvonen puts Zone 2 at 137–148 for the beginner and 127–140 for the veteran, each at the same relative effort.

How accurate is 220 minus age?

As a population average it's fine; as a personal number it carries a standard deviation of roughly 10 to 12 bpm, meaning a third of people sit more than 11 bpm off their formula. Tanaka's 208 − 0.7 × age fits better over 40. The gold standard is a measured max: the highest rate you've seen in a ramp test or race finish. Use a formula to start, then replace it with data.

How long should Zone 2 sessions be?

Most coaches treat 45 minutes as the point where Zone 2 work starts compounding, though shorter sessions still count. Frequency beats duration: four 45-minute Zone 2 sessions in a week outperform one three-hour grind, and the classic endurance split is about 80% of weekly time in Zones 1–2 with 20% hard. Zone 2 adaptations (mitochondria, capillaries, fat oxidation) build slowly, over months, and persist.
