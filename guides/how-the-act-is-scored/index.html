How the ACT Is Scored: Composite, Equating, Percentiles

From raw to scaled: equating, not curving

Every section starts as a raw score: the plain count of correct answers. Wrong answers and blanks both score zero, since the ACT has never had a guessing penalty. If you answered 60 of 75 English questions correctly, your raw English score is 60.

That raw count then converts to the 1-36 scale through a table calibrated for your specific test form. This process is equating, and it's the part people get wrong by calling it a curve. A curve would rank you against that day's test takers. Equating works the opposite way: each form is calibrated in advance against reference groups so a raw 60 means the same thing whether you test in September or April, in a strong year or a weak one.

The practical consequence for estimating your score: conversion tables move a couple of raw points between forms. A raw 41 in Math scaled to 24 on one recent form might scale to 23 or 25 on another. Any calculator, including ours, is showing you a representative scale, a band rather than a verdict.

How the composite is built

The composite is the average of your section scores, rounded to the nearest whole number. That's the entire formula: no weights, no essay, no bonus for consistency. ACT rounds halves up, which matters at the margin more than you'd expect.

  • 29, 25, 27, 24 → 105 ÷ 4 = 26.25 → 26
  • 30, 28, 27, 25 → 110 ÷ 4 = 27.5 → 28 (the round-up in action)
  • 31, 34, 30, 33 → 128 ÷ 4 = 32 exactly → 32

The optional Writing test, in the years it existed, never entered the composite; it was reported as its own subscore. Section scores are reported to admissions offices alongside the composite, and placement decisions often hang on a single section rather than the average.

What the 2025 format change did to scoring

Through 2025 the ACT rolled out a shorter, "enhanced" version of the test: online national dates first, then all testers in fall 2025. English dropped from 75 to 50 questions, Math from 60 to 45, Reading from 40 to 36, and the overall test got meaningfully shorter.

The scoring change that matters most: Science became optional. On the classic test the composite averaged four sections; on the enhanced test it averages three, English, Math, and Reading. Science still exists, still takes 40 questions, and is still scored 1-36, but it's reported separately and pairs with your Math score into a STEM score. If you test on the enhanced format, your Science performance cannot raise or lower your composite at all.

What didn't change: the 1-36 scale, the round-the-average composite rule, and rights-only scoring. A 26 from 2024 and a 26 from 2026 still mean the same thing, which was the entire point of the redesign's scoring continuity.

Reading the percentiles

Your score report pairs every score with national ranks, and those ranks are the context that turns a bare number into information. The national average composite has run about 19.5–20 for recent graduating classes, so a 20 splits the country roughly in half. From there the ladder climbs fast at the top and slow in the middle:

Approximate ranks drawn from ACT's published profile reports for recent classes; they drift a point or two year to year. The right target is never a national yardstick anyway, it's the middle-50 percent range that each college publishes for its incoming class.

Benchmarks and superscoring

ACT publishes College Readiness Benchmarks per section: 18 in English, 22 in Math, 22 in Reading, and 23 in Science. Each marks the score associated with roughly a coin-flip chance of a B or better in the matching first-year credit course. They're a useful self-check on section balance: a composite built on a 28 Reading and an 18 Math tells a different story than a balanced 23.

On retakes, many colleges superscore: they combine your best section scores across sittings into a new composite, recalculated with the same round-the-average rule. ACT itself now builds and sends a superscore when you have multiple sittings on file. The consequence for strategy: improving one weak section on a retake can move a superscored composite more than grinding every section equally.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a 26 on the ACT good?

A 26 sits around the 82nd percentile nationally, comfortably above the national average of roughly 19.5-20. It clears the middle-50 range at many state flagships. Whether it's good for you depends on your college list: check each school's published middle-50 composite range, which is a better target than any national yardstick.

Why is my composite higher than my lowest section?

Because it's an average, every section pulls it toward itself. The composite always lands between your highest and lowest section scores, rounded to a whole number. There's no separate composite curve and no essay influence; the average is the entire formula.

Do wrong answers hurt your ACT score?

No. The ACT uses rights-only scoring: a wrong answer and a blank both contribute zero, with no deduction. Your raw score in each section is simply the count of correct answers, so guessing is always better than leaving questions blank when time runs out.

How long do ACT scores take?

Multiple-choice scores typically post online about two weeks after the test date, though peak periods can stretch it to three to eight weeks. Scores arrive section by section, and the composite appears once all sections are in.
