How Does Social Security Decide Your Disability Onset Date?

📄 7 min readUpdated October 19, 2026

Your disability onset date is the day Social Security decides you became unable to work. You propose one on your application. SSA establishes the real one from your records, earnings and insured status, and every month it moves later is a month of back pay you lose.

What is a disability onset date?

It's the day Social Security says you became unable to work. Not the day you were diagnosed, and not the day you applied. Everything downstream hangs on it: the five-month waiting period, your back pay, and when Medicare begins. Four dates get mixed up along the way:

TermWhat it meansWho decides and how
Alleged onset date (AOD)The date you wrote on your applicationYou pick it, usually your last day of work
Established onset date (EOD)The date SSA decides your disability beganThe examiner or judge, from the evidence
Date last insured (DLI)The last day you had enough recent work credits for SSDIOnset must fall on or before it
Potential onset dateThe earliest date the paperwork allowsUsually the day after substantial work ended

You propose the alleged onset date. SSA establishes the real one. They usually match, because most people pick the day they stopped working and the records back it up. When they don't, the established date is almost always later, and that costs you money.

How does SSA decide when my disability began?

The rule SSA follows is Social Security Ruling 18-01p, in force since October 2018. It tells examiners and judges to pick the first date you met the definition of disability, and to base that date on medical and other evidence rather than on your word alone. Three questions decide it:

  • Were you still working? If you earned above the substantial gainful activity level after your alleged date ($1,690 a month in 2026 for most people), SSA won't put your onset before that work ended. A short attempt to go back to work, six months or less, that failed because of your condition is treated differently and doesn't push your date later.
  • What do the records show, and when? SSA looks for the first dated record that supports the level of impairment it has found. If your alleged date is March but the first MRI, specialist visit or hospital stay is in September, expect a fight over those six months.
  • Were you insured on that date? SSDI needs recent work credits, roughly five of the last ten years. If the evidence puts your onset after your date last insured, SSDI is denied, however disabled you are now.

What happens to my back pay if the onset date moves?

Every month that your established date moves later is a month of back pay you don't get, and the first five full months after onset are never paid anyway. Here's one claimant, $1,630 a month, applied in April 2025, approved by a judge in October 2026, with the onset date set five different ways. Back pay runs through September 2026, the month before the decision. The last column is a standard 25% representative fee, capped at $9,200 for decisions on or after November 30, 2024.

What evidence supports an earlier onset date?

Dated paper. An examiner whose file starts in September has nothing to hang a March date on. Here's what tends to work:

What you're provingEvidence that worksWhy it matters
Last day of workPay stubs, W-2, a letter from your employerThe most common anchor; SSA checks it against your earnings record
Diagnosis or eventER visit, hospital admission, imaging report, surgery noteDated records move an onset date; later descriptions don't
Decline over timeTreatment notes before and after your alleged dateShows the condition was disabling, not just present
Age changeBirth certificateTurning 50 or 55 can make you disabled under the grid rules on that exact day
Failed return to workEarnings record, employer statementWork of six months or less that failed because of your condition doesn't move onset

The age row matters more than people expect. SSA's grid rules make it easier to qualify at 50 and again at 55 if you can only do sedentary or light work. A judge who finds you disabled only from your 50th birthday isn't saying your condition changed that day. The rules did. Gaps in treatment hurt too; if you couldn't afford care, show it, because SSA is supposed to consider why the gap exists.

Should I accept a partially favorable decision?

A partially favorable decision approves you but with a later onset date than you alleged. You have 60 days to appeal it to the Appeals Council, and here's the catch: the Appeals Council can review the whole decision, not just the date. In rare cases, people have ended up with the approval itself in question.

So weigh it. Work out the months at stake with the calculator, multiply by your benefit, and compare that against the delay and the risk. A six-month difference on $1,630 is $9,780, less a fee, and worth a serious conversation with your representative. Either way, appealing the date doesn't pause your monthly checks.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between alleged onset date and established onset date?

The alleged onset date is the date you put on your application. The established onset date is the date SSA decides from medical records, work history and insured status. When they differ, the established date is usually later, which reduces back pay.

Can Social Security change my disability onset date?

Yes. Examiners and judges can set a later date if the records don't support yours, if you worked above the substantial gainful activity level after your alleged date, or if a grid rule only applies from a certain birthday. They rarely move it earlier unless you amend your claim and the evidence supports it.

How does the onset date affect SSDI back pay?

Back pay starts with the sixth full month after your onset date and can't reach more than 12 months before your application month. On a $1,630 benefit, moving onset from March 15, 2025 to September 1, 2025 cuts back pay from 13 months ($21,190) to 8 months ($13,040) for a decision in October 2026.

Should I use my last day of work as my onset date?

Usually, yes. It's easy to prove with pay records, and SSA won't set onset before substantial work ended anyway. If your condition forced you to part time earlier, you can allege that earlier date, but you'll need medical records from that period to hold it.

What is a partially favorable SSDI decision?

An approval with a later onset date than you alleged. You can appeal it to the Appeals Council within 60 days, but the Council can review the entire decision, not just the date. Weigh the months at stake against that risk before appealing.
