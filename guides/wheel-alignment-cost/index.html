How Much Does a Wheel Alignment Cost?

🛞 7 min read🧮 Plan break-even math included

Alignment is one of those services with a suspiciously wide price band: the same car can get the same job done for $85 in one town and $165 in another, and shops will happily sell you a $200 "lifetime" version. Here's what alignment actually costs nationally, what moves the number up and down, and the arithmetic that tells you whether a lifetime plan is a deal or a donation.

What does an alignment cost in 2025?

Two prices cover most of the market. A two-wheel (front-end) alignment runs $50–$100 nationally. A four-wheel alignment — the default for most modern cars — runs $100–$180. SUVs and minivans land a bit above the midpoint, trucks and lifted vehicles above that. These ranges hold across national auto-repair cost surveys; your local quote will vary with labor rates and how honest your potholes are.

Why does the price vary so much?

  • Labor rate is the biggest driver. A shop charging $150/hour bills the same hour of rack time very differently from one charging $90. Metro areas skew high.
  • Vehicle type. Heavier vehicles, trucks, and anything lifted needs more setup, and some require extra shims or kit labor.
  • What the service includes. Some shops quote a "check and adjust" that only covers toe; the full job sets camber and caster too. Cheap quotes sometimes skip the thrust angle.
  • Worn parts are not alignment. If a tie rod, control-arm bushing, or strut is shot, that's parts and labor on top. A bent camber situation can add $150–$500 for a kit.

2-wheel vs 4-wheel: which one are you buying?

A two-wheel alignment adjusts the front axle's three angles — camber (tire lean), caster (steering-axis tilt), and toe (tires pointing in or out). That's sufficient when the rear axle is a non-adjustable solid beam, as on many older trucks.

A four-wheel alignment also measures and squares the rear axle and checks thrust angle: the direction the rear wheels actually push the car. If the rear axle is skewed, the car crabs down the road at a slight angle even with a perfectly centered steering wheel — and only the four-wheel service fixes it. Since most modern cars and every SUV with independent rear suspension falls in this camp, expect the $100–$180 service unless you drive something with a beam rear end.

Is a lifetime alignment plan worth it?

Chains sell an unlimited-alignment plan for roughly $150–$250 (Firestone's Lifetime Alignment is the best-known). The math is simple: break-even = plan price ÷ single-visit price, rounded up.

Run a concrete case: an SUV in an average-cost area pays about $137.50 for four-wheel alignments. A $200 plan breaks even at 200 ÷ 137.5 = 1.45 — the second visit. Align twice a year (a reasonable rhythm on rough roads; the plans are built around a 6-month/6,000-mile recommendation) and keep the vehicle four years: pay-per-visit totals 8 × $137.50 = $1,100 against the plan's $200. That's $900 staying in your pocket.

The flip case matters too. A commuter car in a low-cost area doing two-wheel alignments at $64, once a year, over a three-year ownership, spends $191 — barely more than a $170 plan, and one skipped year puts the plan ahead of you. Plans also aren't transferable when you sell, don't cover worn parts, and lifting a vehicle can void them. They're a bet that you'll keep the car and keep hitting potholes.

How do I know I actually need one?

The car tells you, if you're watching:

  • Steering wheel off-center when driving straight — the classic thrust-angle symptom
  • Pulling to one side on a flat road (after you've ruled out tire pressure)
  • Uneven wear: feathering across the tread, or one edge of a tire gone smooth while the other still has pattern
  • Wandering or a steering wheel that doesn't return to center after a turn
  • Any decent pothole or curb strike, new tires, or suspension work — get it checked regardless of symptoms

Here's the kicker on cost: tires run $600–$1,000 a set, and alignment wear isn't a tire-warranty item since it's a vehicle problem, not a defect. Toe off by as little as an eighth of an inch can strip thousands of miles off a set. The $125 service is the cheap half of that trade — which is also why chains can afford to sell you alignments for life.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much is a 4-wheel alignment?

Typically $100-$180 at U.S. shops, with cars near $125 and trucks or lifted vehicles near $150. Dealerships and big-metro shops sit at the top of the range; independent shops in smaller towns at the bottom.

Why does a 4-wheel alignment cost more than 2-wheel?

The four-wheel service squares both axles and checks the thrust angle, not just the front. More angles to measure, more to adjust, more time on the rack — roughly 40-60 minutes versus 30-40 for a front-end alignment.

Are lifetime alignments worth it?

They break even fast: a typical $200 plan against $137 four-wheel alignments pays for itself on the second visit, since 200 ÷ 137.5 = 1.45. If you align twice a year and keep the vehicle four years, the plan saves about $900. If you align once a year and trade cars every three years, it's close to a wash.

How often does a car need an alignment?

Most shops recommend checking alignment every 6,000 miles or once a year, and immediately after a hard pothole or curb strike, new tires, or suspension work. Misalignment eats tires far faster than the $125 service costs.

Can I drive with bad alignment?

Yes, but it's expensive to keep doing. The car will pull, wander, and wear tires unevenly — toe off by an eighth of an inch can strip thousands of miles off a tire's life. It also hurts fuel economy slightly and makes the car tiring to drive. Get it checked within a week or two of noticing signs.
