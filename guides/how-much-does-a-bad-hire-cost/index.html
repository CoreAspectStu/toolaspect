How Much Does a Bad Hire Cost?

⏱️ 6 min read

A commonly cited U.S. Department of Labor estimate puts the cost of a bad hire at up to 30% of the employee's first-year expected earnings. On a $65,000 salary, that's $19,500 — gone into recruiting fees, onboarding hours, paychecks for weak output, and the whole process run again. And that's just the failure case: the screening that's supposed to prevent it has its own price tag, often $1,000+ a month in salaried time that never shows up on a recruiting budget. Here's where the money actually goes.

What does a bad hire actually cost?

The bill arrives in pieces, and most of them never get booked to a "failed hire" line. The table breaks down the standard components:

Cost componentWhat it runsWhy it's easy to miss
Recruiting and hiring~$4,700 average per SHRM benchmarks; more for specialized rolesSplit across job boards, agency fees, and everyone's time
Onboarding and trainingWeeks of manager and peer hoursNobody logs the coaching sessions
Salary for weak outputMonths of pay at partial productivityPayroll looks normal; output doesn't
Replacement30–200% of salary by role levelBudgeted as "backfill," not failure
Team dragColleagues covering, morale, reworkNever quantified, always real

Add them up and the Department of Labor's 30% figure stops sounding aggressive. On a $65,000 hire it implies $19,500; on a $120,000 specialized role, $36,000. The number climbs fastest with tenure — a bad hire who lasts eighteen months has been paid a full salary plus a severance-adjacent exit, and the seat still has to be filled again from zero.

How much does screening cost before anyone's hired?

Recruiting budgets usually track job-board fees and agency retainers, but the biggest quiet cost is screener time. Every applicant who gets a resume review or a phone screen eats real minutes of a salaried person's day. Run the standard math — 100 applicants a month, 20 minutes each, a $35/hour screener — and the pipeline costs 33.3 hours and $1,166.67 a month. That's $14,000 a year, most of it spent before a single interview gets scheduled. And if half the pipeline is unqualified, as it routinely is, $7,000 of that went to people who were never viable.

What does replacing an employee cost by role level?

Replacement cost scales with how hard the seat is to fill. The ranges below are the ones HR teams commonly cite; your accounting will differ, but the shape holds — the more specialized the role, the more of a year's salary it costs to lose it:

Role levelReplacement cost (commonly cited)On a salary of

Notice what this means for screening economics: preventing one failed mid-level hire saves more than the entire annual cost of screening a hundred-applicants-a-month pipeline. The screening budget isn't overhead; it's insurance priced at a fraction of the payout.

Why do bad hires happen in the first place?

Rarely because the candidate lied. More often because the hiring side improvised: the job description was vague, so unqualified applicants flooded in; every resume got a different read depending on the reviewer's energy; the interview admired confidence instead of checking skills. The fixes are unglamorous and they work:

  • Knockout criteria in the posting. State the license, the years, the shift, the travel. Applicants self-select out for free.
  • Structured screening questions. Every applicant answers the same five questions in the same order. Consistency is both fairer and more defensible than vibes.
  • Skills verification. A work sample or a timed exercise beats three rounds of "tell me about yourself."
  • Real reference checks. Ask references for specific deliverables and deadlines, not general praise.

When is it worth automating the first screen?

When the arithmetic turns uncomfortable. Manual resume review runs 5–10 minutes per applicant and varies with reviewer fatigue; phone screens run 15–30 minutes; keyword filters are fast but miss nuance. If your volume sits in the 200–400 rows of the table above, automating the first pass — structured questions asked and scored identically for every applicant, with human time reserved for the shortlist — is the difference between a screener who spends their week reading and one who spends it interviewing. The bottom line

Price the whole pipeline: up to 30% of first-year earnings if the hire fails, roughly $4,700 just to make a good hire, and $14,000 a year in screening time at a hundred applicants a month before anyone signs anything. Cheap insurance against all three looks like a sharper job posting, structured screening, and automation where the volume justifies it. Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a bad hire cost?

A commonly cited U.S. Department of Labor estimate puts the cost of a bad hire at up to 30% of the employee's first-year expected earnings — $19,500 on a $65,000 salary. That covers recruiting, onboarding, salary paid for weak output, the re-hire, and the productivity gap while the seat sits open.

How much does the average hire cost?

SHRM's benchmarking work puts average cost-per-hire around $4,700, with substantially higher figures for executive and specialized technical roles. That is the cost of a hire that works out — a failed hire adds the replacement cost on top.

How much does screening applicants cost per month?

At 100 applicants a month, 20 minutes each, and a $35/hour screener, screening consumes 33.3 hours and $1,166.67 a month — $14,000 a year. If half the pipeline is unqualified, $7,000 of that is spent on applicants who never had a chance.

What does it cost to replace an employee?

Commonly cited replacement-cost ranges run about 30–50% of annual salary for entry-level roles, 50–80% for professional and mid-level roles, and 100–200% or more for executives and highly specialized positions.

How do you avoid a bad hire?

Write knockout criteria into the job posting, screen every applicant against the same structured questions, verify skills before the final interview, and check references with specific questions about deliverables. Consistency matters as much as rigor: the fourteenth resume of the day should get the same read as the first.
