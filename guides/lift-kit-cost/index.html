How Much Does a Lift Kit Cost?

⏱️ 7 min read

A leveling kit — the entry point — runs $250 to $900 installed. A real suspension lift runs $1,000 to $7,000 or more depending on how tall you go and what is wearing it. Body lifts sit in between at roughly $400 to $1,000. And almost everyone forgets the two line items that follow the lift out the door: the $100-to-$150 alignment and the bigger tires the lift exists to clear.

Every row assumes shop labor at a typical $75 to $150 an hour. The alignment after installation adds $100 to $150 on all of them — suspension geometry changes no matter which product you pick.

What's the difference between a leveling kit and a suspension lift?

Trucks leave the factory raked: the rear sits higher so the truck doesn't squat under payload. A leveling kit adds spacers (or slightly taller front springs) to bring the nose level with the tail. Nothing else changes — no new control arms, no dropped subframe. That is why it costs a few hundred dollars instead of a few thousand.

A suspension lift physically relocates the whole front end — new springs or spacers up front, blocks or add-a-leafs out back, and on taller kits, new control arms, track-bar brackets, brake-line extensions, and driveshaft corrections. You gain clearance for 35-to-37-inch tires and real off-road travel. You also gain a parts list that explains the price.

Body lifts are the odd middle child: pucks between the frame and body raise the cab and bed without touching the suspension. Cheap, invisible to the driveline — but the frame stays low, gap guards show, and bumpers may need relocating.

What moves the price?

  • Height. Every inch past four costs disproportionately more, because driveline angles, steering geometry, and braking components start needing attention.
  • Kit quality. Budget brands run roughly half the price of premium off-road names. The budget kit rides like stock, mostly; the premium kit's tuned shocks and progressive springs are what you are actually paying for.
  • Your truck. Leaf-sprung HD trucks and IFS half-tons need different kits than a solid-axle Wrangler, and labor hours follow.
  • Who installs it. Independent 4x4 shops undercut dealer service departments on labor, and install quality matters more on lifts than on most jobs.

How much does it cost by truck?

The aftermarket is not evenly distributed. Jeeps have the deepest parts catalog on earth, which keeps prices down; heavy-duty trucks sit at the other end.

Can you install one yourself?

A leveling kit is a realistic driveway job for a mechanically inclined owner with jack stands, basic hand tools, and a free weekend — two to four hours on most trucks, plus the alignment you cannot skip. Suspension lifts are a different animal: spring compressors, torqued-to-spec fasteners, brake-line and ABS handling, and often pressed components. Shops see plenty of half-finished driveway lift jobs at $150 an hour to finish. If you are honest that this describes you, price the install from the start.

What happens after the lift?

Budget the consequences, not just the kit. The alignment is mandatory. Fuel economy drops — taller tires and changed aerodynamics typically cost one to three miles per gallon. Bigger tires often demand recalibrated speedometers (a $100-to-$300 programmer on most trucks) or your odometer and shift points drift. Insurance usually doesn't care, but tell your agent if the parts value is significant; resale is a wash — lifted trucks sell fine in truck country, poorly elsewhere.

On warranty: federal law (Magnuson-Moss) means a dealer cannot void the whole warranty because of a lift — they have to show the modification caused the specific failure. Keep your receipts anyway.

Don't skip the alignment

Every lift changes front-end geometry. Price the alignment before you book the install.

The bottom line

Level the truck for $250 to $900, or lift it properly for $1,500 to $3,500 on a mild suspension kit — $3,000 to $7,000+ if you want six inches. Add the alignment, add the tires, and add a speedometer recalibration if you go tall. Frequently Asked Questions

How much is a 4-inch lift kit installed?

Typically $1,500 to $3,500 installed on a half-ton truck. A budget-brand kit with an independent 4x4 shop lands near the low end; a premium kit through a dealer or specialty shop runs past the high end. Add the $100-$150 alignment and, usually, bigger tires.

How much does a leveling kit cost?

$100 to $400 in parts and $250 to $900 installed. It is the cheapest way to level the front with the rear and clear up to about 33-inch tires. Even if you install the spacers yourself, budget for the alignment afterward.

Do I need new tires after lifting my truck?

Not technically, but most people buy them. A leveling kit clears roughly 33-inch tires; a 4-to-6-inch lift clears 35s and up. Tires alone add $800 to $2,000, and if the new rubber needs wider wheels, add another $600 to $1,600.

Will a lift kit void my truck's warranty?

No blanket void. Under the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act, the dealer must show the modification caused a specific problem to deny that repair. A lifted truck with a failed infotainment screen is still a warranty claim; a destroyed CV joint after a 6-inch lift and 37s may not be. Keep your receipts and use a reputable installer.

How long does lift kit installation take?

A leveling kit is a 2-to-4-hour job. A full suspension lift is typically one shop day — 8 to 16 labor hours depending on height, with 6-inch kits on the long end because driveline and brake components often get extended or relocated.
