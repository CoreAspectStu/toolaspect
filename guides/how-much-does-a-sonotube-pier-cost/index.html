How Much Does a Sonotube Pier Cost?

⏱️ 5 min read

A round concrete pier starts with a cardboard form tube — $9 to $15 at the box store for a 4-foot length in the common sizes. Fill a 12-inch one four feet deep and you'll mix six 80-lb bags of concrete, about $36 at $6 a bag. Call it $50 per pier, DIY. A six-pier deck runs near $300 in tubes and concrete; past about a yard of total pour, ready-mix takes over and the math flips in your favor.

How much does a concrete form tube cost by size?

Sonotube is the brand that became the generic — the round fiber forms from Sonoco. Home Depot stocks the Quikrete QUIK-TUBE and Sakrete equivalents at friendlier prices, with genuine Sonotube at Menards and contractor supply counters running higher. All of them do the same job: hold a cylinder of wet concrete while it cures.

How much concrete does one pier take?

The volume is a cylinder: π × (diameter ÷ 2)² × depth. One 80-lb bag of premix yields about 0.60 cubic feet. Divide the first by the second and round up — you can't mix a fraction of a bag, and a pier that finishes 2 inches short is a redo.

Bags or ready-mix — where's the crossover?

One cubic yard equals 45 eighty-pound bags. At $6 a bag, that's $270 of bags against a ready-mix price typically in the $150-per-yard range — so bags lose on pure cost immediately, but ready-mix plants add short-load fees below about a yard, and a mixing crew can't hold pace past roughly a bag a minute. The crossover lands near one yard of total pour:

At 20 piers, ready-mix nearly halves the concrete bill and saves a full day of shovel work. Bag prices run roughly $5.50 to $7 at big boxes — the table uses $6 — and pallet quantities sometimes unlock a discount worth asking about.

What else goes in a pier budget?

  • Post bases. A rated galvanized post anchor tying the deck post to the pier runs about $5-$15 per post — cheap insurance against uplift and rot at the concrete face.
  • Setting hardware. Stakes and screws to hold the tube plumb, and a brace or two. A pier poured out of plumb is forever.
  • Flared footing forms. Plastic bell forms that flare the tube's base spread the load over more soil — they hold meaningfully more concrete than the straight cylinder, so re-run the numbers before ordering.
  • Digging. A clamshell post-hole digger handles a dozen 12-inch holes; an auger rental pays for itself past that. Call 811 before any of it.
  • Permit and inspection. Most decks over 30 inches off the ground need one, and the inspector will check depth and diameter against your code's frost line — better to know that number before the tubes are cut.

The bottom line

Budget roughly $50 for a 12-inch × 4-foot pier — a $14 tube plus six bags — and $30-$37 for the smaller diameters. A six-pier deck is about $300 in materials if you mix it yourself; a 20-pier job crosses two yards and belongs on a ready-mix truck. Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a 12-inch sonotube cost?

A 4-foot Quikrete or Sakrete brand 12-inch tube runs $13-$15 at Home Depot — about $3.50 per foot. Genuine Sonotube brand tubes from contractor suppliers run higher, with marketplace listings around $35-$50. Ten-foot lengths for frost-depth piers list at $30-$40 in 12-inch.

How many bags of concrete do I need for one sonotube?

At 4 feet deep: an 8-inch tube takes 3 eighty-pound bags, a 10-inch takes 4, and a 12-inch takes 5.2 bags — round to 6. The math is cylinder volume (π × radius² × height) divided by 0.60 cubic feet of yield per 80-lb bag.

How much concrete does a 6-pier deck need?

Six 12-inch tubes 4 feet deep total about 18.8 cubic feet — 0.70 cubic yards with a 10 percent cushion. That's 35 eighty-pound bags (about $210 at $6 a bag) or a $115 ready-mix order, before any short-load fee the plant adds below one yard.

How deep should sonotube piers go?

In frost climates, code typically requires the pier bottom at or below the local frost line, which runs from 12 inches in warm zones to 48 inches or more in the northern tier. Your building department sets the number — call before you dig, and call 811 first for buried utilities.

Can you leave a sonotube in the ground?

Yes — the tube is designed to stay in place below grade and slowly deteriorate there, which is fine. Cut or snap off the above-grade portion after the concrete cures if the pier will be exposed, and grade soil away from the cardboard so it isn't wicking water.
