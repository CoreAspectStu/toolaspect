How to File a Diminished Value Claim

💡 10-15K searches/mo💰 CPC: $15-40⏱️ 6 min read

Your car was repaired perfectly and it's still worth less. The accident on its vehicle-history report shaves real money off resale, and in most states you can make the at-fault driver's insurance pay that gap. Most people never ask, which is exactly why insurers rarely volunteer it. Here's the process, the math, and the letter.

Step 1: Finish repairs first

A diminished value claim needs a completed repair file: the final invoice with line items, photos of the damage before and during the work, and the total paid. That invoice is your proof of severity, which drives the multiplier in the formula and the credibility of the whole demand. Claims filed before repairs finish invite the adjuster to wait you out.

Step 2: Run the 17c formula

The industry's default math, from Georgia's State Farm v. Mabry decision: take 10% of your car's pre-accident value, multiply by a damage multiplier (1.00 severe structural down to 0.25 minor), and by a mileage multiplier (1.0 under 20k miles down to 0.0 past 100k).

Step 3: Send the demand letter

Address it to the at-fault driver's insurer, claim number in the subject line, and include: the repair invoice, photos, two or three comparable listing pairs (wrecked-history vs clean, same year and model, showing the price gap), your calculation with both 17c and appraisal-range numbers, and a specific demand with a deadline, 30 days is customary. Keep the tone clerical. The letter is a business document, not a grievance.

Step 4: Handle the pushback

The first response is usually a form rejection or a lowball. The second letter is where claims get real: cite Mabry (binding in Georgia, persuasive everywhere), restate the comparable-sales evidence, and hold your number with a small concession if the file supports it. If the insurer stalls past your deadline, two escalations exist: an independent DV appraisal ($200-500, worth it on late-model cars) or small-claims court, where the ceiling is typically $5,000-$10,000 and insurers often settle rather than send counsel.

Timing and traps

  • Statutes run 2-6 years for property damage in most states, but comparable-sale evidence ages fast; file within weeks of repair.
  • Your own insurer isn't the target. Collision coverage fixes the car; the at-fault carrier pays the lost value.
  • Leased and financed cars still qualify; the claim belongs to the owner, so check your contract.
  • High-mileage cars get zero from 17c (the mileage multiplier is 0.0 past 100k); skip straight to an appraisal basis.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much can I get from a diminished value claim?

The 17c formula typically yields $1,000-2,500 on a $20,000-40,000 vehicle, and independent appraisals often support 15-30% of pre-loss value on late-model cars with structural damage, well above 17c. A $25,000 car with major damage at 35,000 miles claims $1,500 under 17c; the same car might support $3,750-7,500 on an appraisal basis. Real settlements usually land between the two.

Do I file a diminished value claim with my own insurer?

Generally no; you file against the at-fault driver's liability coverage, because diminished value is the consequence of their negligence. Your own collision coverage repairs the car but doesn't cover its lost resale value. Georgia (State Farm v. Mabry) even requires insurers to consider first-party DV in some contexts, but nationally the third-party claim is the standard path.

What documentation does a diminished value claim need?

Four documents carry the claim: the complete repair invoice with parts and labor detail, photos of the damage before and during repair, comparable listings showing the price gap between wrecked-history and clean vehicles of your model, and your calculation (17c math or an appraisal report). A specific dollar demand letter ties it together. Claims filed within weeks of completed repair get taken more seriously.
