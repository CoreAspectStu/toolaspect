Raw Dog Food Cost: What 2-3% of Body Weight Really Costs

🦴 8-15K searches/mo cluster⏱️ 7 min read

Raw feeding has one rule that sizes every meal: adult dogs eat 2-3% of their body weight a day. That single percentage turns into a grocery bill fast. Here's the math at three price tiers, what puppies and big breeds do to it, and where the money actually goes when you pay $9 a pound.

How much raw food does a dog eat per day?

Portions scale straight off the scale reading. At 2.5%, the middle of the adult range, a 40-lb dog eats 1 lb (16 oz) a day, a 60-lb dog eats 1.5 lb (24 oz), and an 80-lb dog eats 2 lb (32 oz). The low-activity end (2%) and the very-active end (3%) put a 60-lb dog anywhere from 19.2 to 28.8 oz a day.

Puppies blow the curve. A 20-lb puppy at 14 weeks eats 8% of body weight, about 1.6 lb a day, because they're building a skeleton. By 6 months the rate halves to roughly 4%, and by a year most dogs land in the adult 2-3% band for good. Giant breeds ride the puppy curve longer, sometimes to 18 months.

Every meal then splits into the prey-model ratio: 80% muscle meat, 10% edible bone, 10% secreting organs, with liver as half the organ share. That 60-lb dog's 24 oz becomes 19.2 oz of meat, 2.4 oz of bone, and 2.4 oz of organ, 1.2 oz of it liver.

What does that cost per month?

Run the same 60-lb dog at 2.5% through a month: 1.5 lb a day is 45 lb of food. The price per pound decides everything else.

Why is premium raw so expensive?

Two line items: freezing and shipping. Direct-to-consumer brands ship frozen food on dry ice to your door, and that cold chain costs $6-$9 a pound before the meat itself is premium-grade. You're also paying for formulation: the better commercial diets are complete-and-balanced to AAFCO profiles, so the calcium-to-phosphorus balancing is done for you.

DIY raw is cheaper because you become the supply chain. Chicken quarters at $1.10-$1.50/lb carry the bone load, turkey necks and pork shoulder fill in muscle meat, and liver plus kidney from any butcher runs $2-$3/lb. The honest costs are freezer space, an hour of portioning a month, and the discipline to keep the 80/10/10 average over time rather than every bowl.

Do bigger dogs cost proportionally more?

Slightly less than proportionally, because big dogs sit at the low end of the percentage band. An 80-lb dog at 2% eats 1.6 lb a day, 48 lb a month; at 3% that's 2.4 lb and 72 lb. Annual volume lands between 584 and 876 lb. Small dogs invert the rule: a 10-lb dog often needs 3.5% or more, so per pound of dog, the little ones eat you out of house and home.

How do I keep the bill down without shorting nutrition?

  • Buy bone-in in bulk. Chicken quarters and turkey necks deliver the 10% bone bucket at the lowest cost per pound of anything in the diet.
  • Mix tiers. Many feeders run DIY for the 80% muscle-meat bucket and buy commercial only for organ blends, where precise balancing matters most.
  • Price-match the DTC brands. Premium raw subscription prices swing 20-30% between "first order" and "autopay" tiers; the autopay discount is usually the real price.
  • Watch body condition, not ounces. If ribs disappear, cut the percentage a quarter point. It's cheaper than a weight-loss diet later.

Portion math and calorie-based feeding converge on the same answer for most dogs; if your dog has a medical condition, a growing puppy's exact needs, or you're switching from a therapeutic kibble, loop in your vet on the transition. Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to feed a dog raw food per month?

A 60-lb dog at the standard 2.5% of body weight eats 45 lb a month. DIY grocery raw at $3-$5/lb runs $135-$225; pet-store frozen patties at $5-$7 run $225-$315; premium direct-to-consumer raw at $6-$9 runs $270-$405 with shipping included. Over a year that spans $1,643 to $4,928.

Why do raw feeders use percent of body weight instead of calories?

Raw diets don't come with a feeding chart, because there's no bag: the food is meat, bone, and organ whose calorie density varies by protein and fat trim. Percent of body weight is the practical proxy the raw-feeding community settled on, and it tracks closely with the RER x life-stage math veterinarians use for kcal. Adjust by a quarter point up or down based on your dog's ribs and waistline.

Is DIY raw really cheaper than commercial raw?

Usually about half the price. Chicken quarters at $1.10-$1.50/lb and bulk organ at $2-$3/lb average out near $3-$4/lb finished, versus $6-$9/lb for premium delivery brands. The trade-offs are freezer space, monthly prep sessions, and balancing calcium-to-phosphorus yourself instead of letting the manufacturer do it.

Does a raw diet cost more than kibble?

Yes, several times over. Premium kibble for a 60-lb dog runs roughly $40-$80 a month; the same dog on commercial raw runs $225-$405. Even DIY raw at $135-$225 is close to double. What raw buyers are paying for is ingredient control, and the bill is the number-one reason people quit, so price a full year before switching.
