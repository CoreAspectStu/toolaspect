How Much Can My Truck Tow? GVWR, GCWR & Tongue Weight, Explained

The three ratings that decide everything

Every towing question comes down to three factory ratings, and people mix them up constantly:

RatingWhat it capsWhere it lives
GVWRMax weight of the loaded truck alone — fuel, people, cargo, and tongue weight all countDriver's door jamb sticker
GCWRMax weight of truck + trailer + everything combinedOwner's manual towing chart
Hitch ratingMax trailer weight and max tongue weight the receiver itself carriesStamped on the hitch

Payload — the number people actually run out of — is derived: payload = GVWR − curb weight. The tire sticker usually prints it as "The combined weight of occupants and cargo shall not exceed X lb." Everything you add to the truck, including the trailer's tongue pressing down on the ball, spends that budget.

The three-line math

  1. Powertrain ceiling: max trailer = GCWR − curb weight − passengers − cargo.
  2. Axle ceiling: max trailer = (GVWR − curb − passengers − cargo) ÷ tongue percentage.
  3. Your number: the smaller of the two, capped by the hitch rating.

Tongue weight is 10-15% of the loaded trailer for bumper-pulls (aim for 12%) and 15-20% for fifth-wheels and goosenecks. Dividing by a percentage is why payload hurts so much: at 12%, every 100 lb of payload supports only about 833 lb of trailer, so a toolbox and a passenger can cost you 1,000 lb of towing.

A worked example: the typical half-ton

Take a crew-cab half-ton — GVWR 7,350, GCWR 17,000, curb weight 5,500 — with 700 lb of family and firewood aboard, pulling a travel trailer at 12% tongue:

  • Payload = 7,350 − 5,500 = 1,850 lb
  • Available for tongue = 1,850 − 700 = 1,150 lb
  • Max trailer by payload = 1,150 ÷ 0.12 = 9,583 lb
  • Max trailer by GCWR = 17,000 − 5,500 − 700 = 10,800 lb
  • Real limit: 9,583 lb — payload-bound, with the hitch (10,000 lb) never even entering the conversation

Now check the trailer you actually own. An 8,000-lb loaded trailer puts 960 lb on the ball (12%); people plus tongue = 1,660 lb against the 1,850-lb payload — legal, with 190 lb to spare, and the 14,200-lb combined weight sits 2,800 lb under GCWR. Tow it confidently.

Then the counterexample: a 15,000-lb fifth wheel at 18% tongue drops 2,700 lb onto the truck. That's 1,550 lb more than the 1,150 lb left after passengers — 850 lb over the truck's entire payload, before you load a single suitcase. No hitch or accessory fixes that; fifth wheels belong behind 2500/3500-class trucks with 3,000+ lb of payload.

Configuration swings every column: diesel engines add weight (cutting payload) but add cooling and torque (raising tow ratings); 4WD, big cabs, and sunroofs all eat payload. Two trucks wearing the same badge can differ by thousands of pounds, which is why the stickers on YOUR truck are the only numbers that matter.

Tricks that don't work

A weight-distributing hitch makes a heavy bumper-pull steer and brake properly, and your owner's manual probably requires one above 5,000 lb or so — but it redistributes weight, it doesn't create payload. Airbags level the ride without raising GVWR one pound. "Adding a leaf" stiffens springs while the axles, brakes, and frame keep their original ratings. Engineers set these numbers for the whole system; aftermarket parts can't renegotiate them.

What genuinely helps: move cargo from the truck bed into the trailer (it spends trailer capacity instead of payload), travel with lighter tanks, and weigh the rig at a public cat scale — around $15 — to learn what your combination actually weighs. Frequently Asked Questions

Is towing capacity just GVWR minus curb weight?

No — that difference is payload, the weight the truck can carry, not pull. Towing capacity comes from GCWR: gross combined rating minus the truck's actual loaded weight. Payload still decides the outcome, though, because the trailer's tongue weight spends payload, and payload runs out long before the drivetrain does on most half-tons.

Why is my real towing limit lower than the advertised number?

Advertised max-tow figures assume a stripped truck, a 150-lb driver, and often a specific axle ratio. Add a crew cab, 4WD, a bed full of gear, and a family, and every pound comes straight out of towing headroom. At 12% tongue weight, every 100 lb of cargo in the truck cuts your payload-limited trailer max by about 833 lb.

What percentage of trailer weight should sit on the hitch?

Conventional bumper-pull trailers tow safely at 10-15% tongue weight, with about 12% the sweet spot — a 6,000-lb trailer should press 600-900 lb on the ball. Fifth-wheel and gooseneck trailers need more: 15-20%. Too little tongue weight makes the trailer sway; too much overloads the rear axle and lifts the front.

Can I increase my truck's towing capacity?

Not meaningfully. A weight-distributing hitch improves handling and many manufacturers require one above a threshold, but no bolt-on raises GVWR or GCWR — those are engineered ratings for axles, brakes, frames, and cooling. The only real fixes are towing with a lighter load, carrying cargo in the trailer instead of the truck, or using a truck with higher ratings.

What happens if I exceed payload or GCWR?

Physically: the rear axle overloads, the front tires lighten, steering and braking distances suffer, and heat builds in the transmission. Legally and financially: an overloaded combination can mean citations, and an insurer can dispute a claim after an accident involving a load beyond the door-jamb ratings. Weigh the rig loaded at a cat scale — about $15 — before a long trip.
