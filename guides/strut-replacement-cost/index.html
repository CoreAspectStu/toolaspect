Shocks or struts — what's on your car?

The words get used interchangeably and the bills are nothing alike. A shock absorber is a damper only: a tube that bleeds off spring motion. A strut is structural, the spring sits on it and the corner's alignment geometry lives in its mounting holes.

  • Shock pair: 0.8–1.5 hours, no alignment usually needed on solid axles. $344–$715 installed.
  • Strut pair, bare: 2.5–3.5 hours because the spring has to come off the old strut under compression, plus an alignment. $734–$1,274 with mounts.
  • Strut pair, quick strut: 1.6–2.2 hours because the assembly arrives with spring and mount together. $757–$1,335.

Most modern cars run struts up front and either struts or springs-with-shocks out back. Your quote should say which, because the labor line depends on it.

Quick struts: worth the parts premium?

A complete assembly (Monroe Quick-STRUT, Gabriel ReadyMount, KYB Strut-Plus are the common lines) includes the coil spring, upper mount and spring seats. Parts run $130–$300 more per pair. What you buy with that: roughly an hour less labor per axle, brand-new springs instead of 100,000-mile originals, and no compressed spring waiting to hurt somebody in the shop.

On a front pair at $130/hr the totals nearly wash out, $734–$1,274 bare versus $757–$1,335 as assemblies. Given that the mounts are wear items you'd be buying anyway, most honest shops quote quick struts by default on ordinary cars. The bare-strut route makes sense when the springs are nearly new (post-collision, for instance) or when OE-only applications make assemblies expensive.

Why the alignment is not optional

Struts carry camber and affect toe. Disassemble the corner and the angles move; bolt it back and they don't return exactly. Drive on it out of spec and you eat a $600 pair of tires while the car wanders. An alignment runs $80–$200, and it's priced separately in most shops, so a quote without it isn't wrong, it's incomplete. Exception: rear shocks on a solid-axle truck or SUV. Nothing in that job moves alignment angles, which is one reason truck owners keep riding soft for cheap.

Reading a quote that looks off

  • Single strut quoted: wear happens in pairs. Ask why, and expect the answer to be collision damage.
  • Used or "remanufactured" struts: dampers don't remanufacture well. New economy-tier is usually the better cheap option.
  • Labor under 2 hours for a bare strut pair: either they're quoting assemblies or they're not doing the mounts. Ask which.
  • Labor over 4 hours for a pair: some European cars with adaptive dampers justify it, a Camry doesn't.
  • No alignment line at all: fine if they say "get it aligned elsewhere," misleading if they imply it's unnecessary.

Make the job last

Struts die by miles and by roads: expect 50,000–100,000 miles, sooner on pothole cities. Pair them with new mounts (the clunk you hear at 80,000 is usually the mount's bearing, not the damper), keep the alignment current so tires don't cup, and if you're already paying labor on the corner, ask whether anything else in there is due. Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to replace struts?

A pair of struts runs $545-$875 installed with standard parts at a $130/hr shop rate, before mounts and alignment. Add strut mounts ($70-$160 in parts) and the $80-$200 alignment the job requires, and a front-axle job lands at $734-$1,274. J.D. Power puts front struts at $300-$700 per strut installed and RepairPal's shock or strut replacement average is $1,119-$1,328, both consistent with these bands.

What's the difference between shocks and struts on a repair bill?

A shock is just a damper, a tube that controls spring motion, and swapping a pair bills 0.8-1.5 hours. A strut is a structural part that carries the spring and the corner's geometry, so replacing a pair takes 2.5-3.5 hours bare or 1.6-2.2 as complete assemblies, and needs an alignment after. That's why a shock pair costs $344-$715 installed while a strut pair runs $734-$1,274 with mounts and alignment.

Are quick struts worth the extra parts money?

Usually. A complete assembly includes the spring, mount and seats, costing about $130-$300 more per pair, but it cuts labor by roughly an hour per axle and eliminates spring compression, the riskiest step of the job. At $130/hr a front pair comes out nearly even: $734-$1,274 bare versus $757-$1,335 as quick struts.

Can I replace struts myself?

If you own a quality spring compressor and respect it, a strut job is a driveway-scale repair on many cars, and quick struts make it safer by arriving pre-assembled. Budget an alignment afterward either way. The corner carries the brake, the steering and the car's weight, so a compressor slip or a forgotten torque spec is not a cheap lesson.

How do I know my struts are bad?

The classic signs: the car bounces two or three times after a bump instead of settling once, the front dives hard under braking, tires cup or scallop, and you hear clunks over potholes. Oil streaks running down the strut body mean the seal is gone. Expect 50,000-100,000 miles from a set, and less on cars that live on rough roads.
