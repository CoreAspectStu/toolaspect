How Much Concrete Do I Need? Yards, Bags and Slab Math

🧮 7 min readUpdated September 30, 2026

To figure out how much concrete you need, multiply length × width × thickness in feet, divide by 27 to get cubic yards, and add 10% for waste. A 10 × 10 ft patio at 4 in thick needs 1.23 yd³, or about 1.36 yd³ with waste. That's 62 bags of 80 lb mix, so a small truck load starts to make sense.

How do you calculate how much concrete you need?

Concrete is sold by volume, so every estimate comes down to length × width × thickness. The catch is units. Length and width are usually in feet, thickness is in inches, and ready-mix is priced in cubic yards. So the full formula runs in three steps:

  1. Convert the thickness to feet. Divide inches by 12. A 4 in slab is 0.333 ft.
  2. Multiply to get cubic feet. A 10 × 10 ft patio at 4 in is 10 × 10 × 0.333 = 33.3 cu ft.
  3. Divide by 27 to get cubic yards. One cubic yard is 3 ft × 3 ft × 3 ft, which is 27 cu ft. So 33.3 ÷ 27 = 1.23 yd³.

Then add waste. Our concrete calculator defaults to 10%, which takes that patio to 36.7 cu ft, or 1.36 yd³. That's the number to order, not the 1.23.

Why pad it? Forms bow a little, the ground is never perfectly flat, and some concrete ends up on your boots. Coming up short is the expensive mistake: a cold joint where fresh concrete meets a setting slab, or a second delivery.

How many bags of concrete do you need?

Bagged mix is sold by weight, but what matters is how much concrete it makes once you add water. The usual yields for standard concrete mix are:

  • 80 lb bag: about 0.60 cu ft, so 45 bags per cubic yard
  • 60 lb bag: about 0.45 cu ft, so 60 bags per cubic yard
  • 40 lb bag: about 0.30 cu ft, so 90 bags per cubic yard

Should you use bags or order a truck?

The break-even point is roughly one cubic yard, which is 45 bags of 80 lb mix. Below that, bags almost always win. Above 2 yards, a truck almost always wins. In between, it depends on three things:

  • Your local prices. Check the per-bag price at your store and multiply by the bag count. Then ask a ready-mix plant for its price per yard and its short-load fee. Many plants charge extra for small orders, and that fee can be more than the concrete itself on a 1 yard job.
  • Labor. Hand-mixing more than 30 or 40 bags in a day is a lot, even with a rented mixer.
  • Access. If the truck can't get close to the pour, add a pump or a wheelbarrow crew to the bill.

If you order a truck, have forms, reinforcement and helpers ready before it arrives. Ready-mix is usually expected in place within about 90 minutes of batching.

How thick should a concrete slab be?

For most home projects, the choice is between 4 and 6 inches:

  • 4 in: patios, walkways, shed floors, basement and garage floors that only see foot traffic and cars. This is the typical minimum for a slab on grade.
  • 5 to 6 in: driveways and slabs that carry heavier vehicles, RVs, or a hot tub. The extra thickness is cheap insurance against cracking under point loads.

Base prep matters as much as thickness. A slab poured on soft, uncompacted soil will crack no matter how thick it is. Your local building code sets the actual minimum, so check it before you set forms.

How do you calculate concrete for footings and posts?

Footings use the same formula, but the width and depth are usually in inches, so convert both. A 20 ft wall footing that's 16 in wide and 8 in deep is 20 × (16 ÷ 12) × (8 ÷ 12) = 17.8 cu ft. With 10% waste, that's 0.72 yd³ or 33 bags of 80 lb mix. That's the calculator's footing default.

Round posts and tube forms use the cylinder formula: π × radius² × height. The radius is half the diameter, in feet. A 12 in tube that's 4 ft tall holds π × 0.5² × 4 = 3.14 cu ft, a bit over five 80 lb bags.

What are the most common concrete estimating mistakes?

  • Measuring thickness at the edge only. A slab you think is 4 in may be 5 in in the middle, which is 25% more concrete. Check depth in several spots.
  • Forgetting thickened edges. Many slabs have a deeper perimeter, often 8 to 12 in, to stiffen the edge. Calculate that strip separately as a footing and add it.
  • Skipping waste. 5% works for a small form-poured pad with tight forms. For slabs on dug soil, use 10%. Running out halfway through a pour costs far more than a leftover wheelbarrow of concrete.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much concrete do I need for a 10x10 slab?

A 10 × 10 ft slab at 4 in thick is 33.3 cu ft, or 1.23 cubic yards. With 10% extra for waste and uneven ground, order about 1.36 yards. In bags, that's 62 bags of 80 lb mix or 82 bags of 60 lb mix.

How many 80 lb bags of concrete make a yard?

About 45. An 80 lb bag of standard concrete mix yields roughly 0.60 cu ft, and a cubic yard is 27 cu ft, so 27 ÷ 0.60 = 45 bags. With 60 lb bags (0.45 cu ft each) it takes 60, and with 40 lb bags it takes 90.

How many square feet does a yard of concrete cover?

It depends on thickness. One cubic yard covers 81 sq ft at 4 in, 64.8 sq ft at 5 in and 54 sq ft at 6 in. Divide 324 by the thickness in inches to get the square footage for any depth.

Is it cheaper to buy bags or ready-mix concrete?

For jobs under about 1 cubic yard, bags are usually cheaper and simpler. Above 2 yards, ready-mix almost always wins once you count labor. In between, compare your store's per-bag price against the plant's price per yard plus any short-load fee.

How much extra concrete should I order?

Order 5 to 10% more than the calculated volume. Use 5% for small pours with rigid forms and a flat base, and 10% for slabs on excavated soil, where depth varies. For larger slabs, round up to the supplier's next ordering increment.
