How to Measure Countertops for Square Feet and Slabs

🧮 7 min readUpdated September 29, 2026

To measure countertops, multiply each section's length by its depth in inches and divide by 144 to get square feet. A standard counter is 25.5 in deep, so a 10 ft run is 21.3 sq ft. Add up every section, count L-shaped corners only once, add the backsplash if it's the same material, then add 10 to 15% waste for stone.

How do you calculate countertop square footage?

Countertops are priced by the square foot, and the math for a rectangle is simple: length in inches × depth in inches ÷ 144. A 10 ft run is 120 in long. At the standard 25.5 in depth, that's 120 × 25.5 ÷ 144 = 21.3 sq ft.

If you're working in feet, it's length in feet × (depth in inches ÷ 12). Same answer. Our countertop calculator takes the length in feet and the depth in inches, so you can measure the way most people do.

Do this for every separate piece: each wall run, the island, a peninsula, a bar top. Then add them up.

How deep is a standard countertop?

Base cabinets are 24 in deep, and the countertop overhangs the front by about 1 to 1.5 in. That's where the 25 to 25.5 in figure comes from. The overhang keeps drips off the cabinet doors and gives your fingers something to grab.

  • Standard base cabinet run: 25 to 25.5 in deep, 36 in high.
  • Island with seating: typically 36 to 48 in deep, depending on cabinets and overhang.
  • Bathroom vanity: usually 21 or 22 in deep.

For an island with stools, the National Kitchen and Bath Association's planning guidelines call for 24 in of width per seat and 15 in of knee space at a standard 36 in counter height (12 in at a 42 in bar height). So a 7 ft island seats three comfortably, and you'd plan roughly 15 in of overhang on the seating side.

How do you measure an L-shaped countertop?

This is where most homemade estimates go wrong. If you measure both legs of an L along the wall, from each end into the corner, you count the corner square twice.

Instead, measure the first leg along the wall into the corner. Then measure the second leg from the front edge of the first leg to its end. At 25.5 in deep, the double-counted corner is 25.5 × 25.5 ÷ 144 = 4.5 sq ft. That's not a disaster, but on stone at $60 or more per square foot it adds a few hundred dollars to a quote that isn't real.

Same rule for U-shapes: measure the back wall fully, then both side legs from its front edge. Or add up all the wall lengths and subtract the depth once for each inside corner.

How much waste should you add for countertops?

More than you'd think for natural stone, less for everything else.

MaterialTypical wasteWhy
Granite, marble, quartzite10 to 15%Veining has to line up at seams, and stone can crack during cutting
Quartz (engineered)About 10%Uniform pattern, but cutouts and seams still eat material
Butcher blockAbout 10%Sold in fixed lengths, so short runs leave offcuts
Laminate5 to 10%Cut from sheets, and pattern matching rarely matters

For our U-shape example, waste changes the order like this: 63.9 sq ft at 0%, 70.3 at 10%, 73.5 at 15% and 76.7 at 20%. It's two slabs in every case, which is the point. For stone, the slab count matters more than the square footage.

How big is a granite or quartz slab?

There's no single standard, and it varies by quarry and brand. Typical ranges:

  • Granite: often around 9 to 10 ft long by 5 to 6 ft wide, so roughly 45 to 60 sq ft. The calculator uses 50 as a middle figure.
  • Quartz, standard: commonly about 120 × 55 in, or roughly 46 sq ft.
  • Quartz, jumbo: commonly around 130 × 65 in, or roughly 59 sq ft.

Jumbo slabs can save a seam on a long island. A 10 ft island that's 42 in deep won't fit on a standard 120 × 55 in slab with room to spare, but it fits on a jumbo in one piece. Ask your fabricator which sizes they stock and enter that in the slab size field.

What adds to the cost beyond square footage?

Square footage gets you in the ballpark, but a real quote has more lines on it:

  • Edge profile, priced per linear foot. A basic eased edge is often included, while ogee or bullnose costs more. That's why the calculator estimates edge length.
  • Cutouts for sinks, cooktops and faucet holes, usually a flat fee each.
  • Thickness. Stone comes in 2 cm (about ¾ in) or 3 cm (about 1¼ in). 3 cm is the common kitchen choice and doesn't need a plywood deck underneath.
  • Overhang supports. Long unsupported overhangs on an island usually need brackets or corbels. Where that line falls depends on material and thickness, so ask the fabricator.
  • Tear-out and disposal of the old tops.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate countertop square footage?

Multiply length by depth in inches and divide by 144. A 10 ft run is 120 in, so at 25.5 in deep it's 120 × 25.5 ÷ 144 = 21.3 sq ft. Measure each section separately, don't double-count corners on L and U shapes, and add the backsplash if it's the same material.

How many square feet of countertop is in an average kitchen?

Most kitchens have between 30 and 70 sq ft of countertop, including the island. An 18 ft L-shaped run at 25.5 in deep is 38.3 sq ft before backsplash, and a 7 × 4 ft island adds another 28 sq ft.

How many slabs of granite do I need?

Add 10 to 15% waste to your total square footage and divide by the slab size, then round up. With 50 sq ft slabs, 44.3 sq ft plus 15% waste is 50.9 sq ft, so you need two slabs. Seam placement and veining can change this, so confirm with your fabricator.

What is the standard countertop depth?

Kitchen countertops are usually 25 to 25.5 in deep, which covers a 24 in base cabinet plus a 1 to 1.5 in front overhang. Bathroom vanity tops are usually 21 to 22 in deep. Islands vary, often 36 to 48 in.

Is the backsplash included in countertop square footage?

Only if it's cut from the same material. A 4 in stone backsplash along 18 ft adds 6 sq ft; a full-height 18 in splash adds 27 sq ft. If you're tiling above the counter instead, leave it out and estimate the tile separately.
