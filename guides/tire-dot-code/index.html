How Old Are My Tires? Read the DOT Date Code

🛞 10-25K searches/mo💰 CPC: $2-6⏱️ 7 min read

Every tire built since 2000 wears its birthday on the sidewall. The last four digits of the DOT code are week + year of manufacture: 2419 means week 24 of 2019, a tire built in mid-June 2019 — about 7 years 2 months old as of late August 2026. That number matters more than most drivers expect, because manufacturers including Michelin and Continental put a 10-year age limit on their tires regardless of tread, and many retailers draw the line at 6 years. Here's how to find the code, read it in seconds, and know when a "fine-looking" tire is actually due for the bin.

Where is the DOT code hiding?

Find the raised letters DOT on the sidewall and read the character string that follows — 11 or 12 characters in a full code, encoding the plant, tire size, and manufacturer. The final four are the date. The catch: the complete DOT number is stamped on only one sidewall, and carmakers mount that side inward about half the time. If the outer sidewall shows a stubby partial code, the full string is on the tire's inner face — photograph it from beside the fender with a phone flash, or crawl partway under with the parking brake set.

One more wrinkle from the used-car lot: sellers sometimes mount tires so the date faces in, deliberately. If you can't find a date code on any visible sidewall, treat that as information.

Reading the date code: week, then year

Four digits, first two = week, last two = year since 2000. The exact day isn't stamped, so a code maps to the start of a build week:

DOT codeMeansBuilt (approx.)
0525Week 5, 2025Jan 29, 2025
1921Week 19, 2021May 7, 2021
2419Week 24, 2019Jun 11, 2019
3615Week 36, 2015Sep 3, 2015
3206Week 32, 2006Aug 6, 2006

Three digits instead of four? The tire predates 2000: two week digits plus one year digit for the 1990s, marked with a small triangle stamp. A 161 with the triangle is week 16 of 1991. There is no scenario where a 3-digit tire belongs on the road in 2026.

Do tires actually expire?

Not on any federal calendar — NHTSA sets no expiration date, and tires aren't stamped with one. But the industry's own guidance functions like one:

  • Tire manufacturers (Michelin, Continental, and most others): replace within 10 years of the date of manufacture regardless of tread or appearance, and get an annual inspection by a tire specialist once a tire passes 5 years.
  • Retailers (Discount Tire among them): 6 years is the conservative replacement mark, particularly in hot states where heat accelerates aging.
  • Carmakers: some go further than the tire brands — a handful of owner's manuals call for tire replacement 6 years after the vehicle's in-service date. Your manual's number wins for your car.

Why the limits when the tread looks deep? Aging is an inside job. Ozone, UV, and repeated heat cycles dry out the rubber and degrade the steel and nylon belts sandwiched inside the casing until the structure lets go — a failure mode tread depth can't see. Heat is the accelerant, which is why Phoenix ages out tires years before Minneapolis does and why the spare, cooked under a trunk lid for a decade, is so often the worst tire on the car.

Miles or years: which limit hits first?

Both clocks run and whichever expires first decides. A typical all-season tire lasts roughly 45,000-60,000 miles, which for an average 12,000-mile-a-year driver is 4-5 years — comfortably inside the age limits. The year-clock cases are the opposite: the garage-queen convertible with 4,000 miles on 2016 tires, the trailer that sits loaded all summer, the daily driver that only does 6,000 miles a year. Deep tread on a 9-year-old casing is a coin you shouldn't flip; budget the replacement and sleep better.

Buying new tires? Fresh rubber is part of the deal: insist on a code within the last year or two. A "new" tire sitting in a warehouse since 2022 has already spent 20% of its safe life in the dark. Buying used tires? Read the code first

The used-tire market runs on tread depth, and that's exactly backwards — price the age, then the tread. A half-tread 3-year-old tire at $40 can be fair value; a 7/32-inch 7-year-old at the same price is paying new-tire money for the risk. Walk away from anything inside 18 months of the 10-year limit, any tire with a 3-digit code, and any seller who can't produce the DOT side of the sidewall. Match codes across a pair too: a 2019 and a 2023 on the same axle will age out two years apart, which is fine, but a 2008 and a 2023 pair is someone else's problem becoming yours.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I find the DOT code on my tires?

Look for the letters DOT stamped on the sidewall, then read to the end of the character string that follows. The last four digits are the date code. The full DOT number is stamped on only one sidewall — if the side you can see shows just a few characters, the complete code is on the tire's inner side, so kneel by the fender or photograph it from under the car.

How old are my tires if the code says 2419?

2419 means week 24 of 2019 — the tire was built in mid-June 2019, making it about 7 years 2 months old as of late August 2026. First two digits are always the week, last two are the year since 2000.

How many years do tires last?

In miles, a typical all-season tire lasts 3-5 years of average driving (roughly 45,000-60,000 miles). In calendar age, tire manufacturers including Michelin and Continental set 10 years from date of manufacture as the hard limit, with annual professional inspections after year 5 — and many retailers recommend replacing at 6 years, especially in hot climates. Whichever limit the tire hits first wins.

Why do tires fail with plenty of tread left?

Tread measures the outside; aging happens inside. Ozone, UV, and heat cycles dry out the rubber compounds and degrade the steel and nylon belts layered inside the tire until the structure can't hold together — which is why an old low-mileage tire can fail with deep tread. Heat accelerates everything, so desert-climate tires age on a faster clock.
