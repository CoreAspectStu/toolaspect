The 4.0 GPA Scale, Explained (With Real Conversions)

🎓 ~60K searches/mo (est.)💰 CPC: $1–3⏱️ 7 min read

The 4.0 scale is the exchange rate between letters, percentages, and the number colleges actually read. It looks linear until you notice its cliffs: one percentage point at a band edge is worth 0.3 or 0.4 grade points, and the A−/B+ boundary is the most expensive line in American education. Here's the full scale, the conversion method that avoids the classic mistake, and what weighted numbers really tell an admissions office.

This 13-band table is the version most transcript evaluators treat as standard. Variations exist — some schools cap A+ at 4.0, some count it 4.3, some skip minuses entirely — but the bands above are the ones conversions default to when the transcript key is silent.

The conversion mistake almost everyone makes

Averaging the percentages first, then converting. Percent-to-GPA is not linear, so the order changes the answer. The right method: band each course into its letter, convert to points, then average the points.

Worked: five courses — 92, 85, 78, 95, 88. Band by band: A− (3.7), B (3.0), C+ (2.3), A (4.0), B+ (3.3). Sum 16.3, divide by 5: 3.26. The shortcut route (average 87.6% → "that's a B+" → 3.3) lands 0.04 high and gets worse as grades spread — a 99 and a 77 average to 88 (B+, 3.3) but band honestly to A and C+ (3.15). Band first. Always.

Where the cliffs are

Three single-point jumps do most of the damage:

  • 86 → 87: B to B+, +0.3. And 89 → 90: B+ to A−, +0.4 — the steepest step on the scale.
  • 92 → 93: A− to A, +0.3, and the difference between "highest honors" cutoffs met and missed at many schools.
  • 82 → 83: B− to B, +0.3 — invisible mid-scale, but it moves a 3.0-average student the same distance.

Over a 40-course transcript, one consistently-cliff-adjacent grader can shift a cumulative GPA by 0.1 without any student learning differently. That's not a reason to complain to teachers; it's a reason to know the bands and grade-shop wisely at course selection time.

Weighted GPA: the same grades, bigger numbers

Weighted scales pay a bonus for harder classes — typically +1.0 for AP/IB and +0.5 for honors — so an A in AP Calculus is 5.0, not 4.0. The point is signaling course rigor: a 3.8 earned in all-honors classes and a 4.0 earned in grade-weighted electives are different products, and the bump lets transcripts say so in a single number.

What colleges do with it is the part families miss: most recalculate unweighted on their own scale using your transcript key. The weighted number still matters — for scholarships with a weighted cutoff, for class rank, and as a rigor signal — but a 4.4 weighted doesn't outrank a competitor's 3.9 unweighted once both land in the same recalculation spreadsheet. What "good" means on the scale

Benchmarks by destination, roughly: 2.0 (C average) is the academic-eligibility floor for much of NCAA and many programs' continuation; 3.0 clears most employer and scholarship screens; 3.5 is the soft threshold for honors programs and merit aid at many state schools; 3.8+ is competitive-everywhere territory, where the rest of the application does the deciding. Context beats the raw number — a 3.4 from a notoriously hard grader beats a 3.7 from a school known for inflation, and admissions readers know their feeder schools.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a 3.0 GPA equivalent to?

A B average — 83-86% on the standard 13-band scale. The 3.0 line matters more than most grade points because employers, scholarships, and NCAA eligibility frequently use exactly 3.0 as a screen. A 2.9 and a 3.0 are the same letter family on paper, but one clears the filter and one doesn't.

How do I convert my percentage grades to GPA?

Band each percentage into its letter, take the letter's point value, then average the points. Example: 92, 85, 78, 95, 88 convert to 3.7, 3.0, 2.3, 4.0, 3.3 — average 3.26. Never average the percentages first: percent-to-GPA is not linear, and averaging first skews the result versus banding each course.

What GPA is 85 percent?

An 85 is a B, worth 3.0. The band runs 83-86, so 83 through 86 all count the same — but 87 jumps to B+ (3.3) and 82 falls to B- (2.7). The single percentage point at each band edge is worth 0.3 or 0.4 grade points, which is why rounding policies genuinely move class ranks.

Do colleges look at weighted or unweighted GPA?

Mostly unweighted, recalculated on the college's own scale from your transcript — which is why a 4.4 weighted and 3.8 unweighted from the same student both exist. Weighted GPA still matters as context (it shows course rigor) and for scholarships that specify either. Report what each application asks for, and expect the recalculation.

Can GPA be higher than 4.0?

On weighted scales, easily: an A in an AP course adds 1.0, making 5.0 the ceiling at most schools, 5.3 where A+ counts 4.3, and a few dual-enrollment programs reach 6.0. On the unweighted 4.0 scale, no — 4.0 is the cap regardless of A versus A+.
