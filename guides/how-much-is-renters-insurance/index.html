How Much Is Renters Insurance? 2025 Average Costs

💡 33K searches/mo💰 CPC: $16⏱️ 6 min read

Renters insurance costs about $195 a year in 2025, roughly $16 a month for a standard policy with $30,000 of property coverage and $100,000 of liability. Where you live moves that number a lot: North Dakota renters average around $115 a year while Louisiana renters pay closer to $390.

What does the average renter pay in 2025?

The $195 national average describes a baseline policy: $30,000 of personal property coverage, $100,000 of liability, and a $500 deductible. That's the starter package most insurers quote first. Bump the coverage or shrink the deductible and the price climbs, though never as fast as you'd expect, because a chunk of every premium is fixed overhead.

State matters more than any policy choice. Severe-weather states dominate the expensive end of the table, since the same hurricanes and hail that wreck roofs also destroy the belongings inside. Here's how the country breaks down:

What makes renters insurance more expensive?

Five things do most of the damage:

  • Location. The single biggest factor. A Louisiana renter pays more than three times what a North Dakota renter pays for the same policy, and even within a state, a high-crime zip code costs more than a quiet one.
  • Coverage amount. Going from $30,000 to $50,000 of property coverage raises a typical premium about 30%. More stuff, more risk.
  • Deductible. A $500 deductible is the priciest option. Choosing $1,000 saves about 8%, and $2,000 saves about 15%.
  • Credit and claims history. In most states insurers score your credit, and a prior claim can raise your rate for three to five years.
  • The building itself. Older wiring, no sprinklers, no deadbolts, and no smoke detectors all push the price up. Some insurers also charge more for certain dog breeds.

Is $30,000 of coverage enough?

For most renters, yes, but check rather than assume. Walk through your place with your phone, open every closet, and estimate what it would cost to replace everything new: furniture, electronics, clothes, kitchen gear, the bike in the hallway. A studio with modest furnishings might total $15,000. A family in a three-bedroom can pass $50,000 without owning anything fancy.

Watch the sub-limits too. Standard policies cap certain categories, often around $1,500 for jewelry theft and similar amounts for firearms, watches, and collectibles. If you own an engagement ring or a serious camera setup, schedule those items separately. It usually costs a few dollars per $1,000 of declared value and removes the cap entirely.

How can I lower my premium?

Renters insurance is already cheap, but a few moves make it cheaper without gutting the protection:

  1. Raise the deductible. Moving from $500 to $2,000 saves about 15%, roughly $29 a year on an average policy. Only do it if you could genuinely cover $2,000 tomorrow.
  2. Bundle with auto. Most insurers discount both policies 5% to 10% when you combine them. If you're not sure what your car coverage should cost in the first place, the car insurance estimator gives you a baseline before you negotiate.
  3. Claim the safety discounts. Smoke detectors, deadbolts, a security system, even being a non-smoker can each shave a few percent. Ask for the full discount list; agents rarely volunteer it.
  4. Pay annually. Monthly installment fees quietly add $5 to $12 a year on some policies. One payment skips them.
  5. Shop every couple of years. Renters policies are commodity products, and loyalty is rarely rewarded. Fifteen minutes of quotes can beat years of small rate creep.

One thing not to do: skip the coverage entirely to save $195. A single burst pipe or apartment fire can wipe out more than a decade of premiums, and the liability protection alone has bailed out plenty of renters whose overflowing tub ruined the ceiling below.

Where does renters insurance fit in your budget?

It's the cheapest policy most people will ever own, which makes it a good first stop in an insurance checkup. Once it's sorted, the bigger-ticket questions are worth the same treatment: whether your car coverage matches your state's real costs, and whether anyone depends on your income enough to justify term life. The life insurance needs calculator answers that second one with actual numbers instead of a rule of thumb. Together, all three policies for a typical young renter often total less than $100 a month, and they cover the three ways a bad week becomes a bad decade.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does renters insurance cover my roommate's belongings?

Not unless they're named on the policy. A standard policy covers you, your spouse, and relatives who live with you. An unrelated roommate needs their own policy, and that's usually the better setup anyway: separate policies mean separate claims histories, and at $10 to $20 a month each, there's little savings in sharing.

Does my credit score affect renters insurance?

In most states, yes. Insurers use a credit-based insurance score, and the gap between excellent and poor credit can mean paying 30% to 60% more for the same coverage. California, Massachusetts, and Michigan restrict the practice. If your credit has improved since you bought your policy, ask your insurer to rerun it.

Is renters insurance cheaper if I bundle with auto?

Usually. Most large insurers knock 5% to 10% off both policies when you bundle renters with auto coverage. On a $195 renters policy and a $1,900 auto policy, that's roughly $105 to $210 a year back. Just compare the bundled total against the cheapest standalone quotes, since a big discount on an overpriced base rate is no bargain.

What's the difference between replacement cost and actual cash value?

Actual cash value pays what your stuff was worth used, so a five-year-old couch might net $150. Replacement cost pays what it costs to buy new, and it usually adds about 10% to your premium. On a $195 policy that's roughly $20 a year, and it's almost always worth it after a major loss.
