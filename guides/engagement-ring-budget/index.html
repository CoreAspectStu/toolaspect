How Much Should You Spend on an Engagement Ring?

💍 35K searches/mo💰 CPC: $2–8⏱️ 6 min read

Every ring counter conversation starts with a number, and most people walk in carrying one they got from an ad. Here's the honest version: US couples average $4,600 on an engagement ring (The Knot 2025 Jewelry & Engagement Study), the "two months' salary" rule is a De Beers campaign from the 1980s, and lab-grown diamonds have quietly cut the price of the identical product by 70-80%. Set your budget with all three facts in view.

What people actually spend

The Knot's 2026 study of thousands of couples puts the average ring at $4,600, and recent annual surveys have bounced between roughly $4,600 and $6,000. Against a median US household income of about $80,600 (Census Bureau, 2024), that's roughly 6% of a year's gross earnings. Averages hide the spread, though: a meaningful share of rings are under $2,000, and a small number of five-figure purchases drag the mean upward. The typical buyer is not spending two months of anything.

Where the two-months rule came from

De Beers spent the 20th century attaching a price signal to love. During the Great Depression the campaign suggested one month's salary; by the 1980s the suggestion had crept to two. There was never data behind it, just a century of consistently excellent advertising. Japan's diamond engagement ring tradition, for context, was built by the same campaigns in barely 20 years. So if you feel a pull toward a specific percentage of your salary, notice whose arithmetic that is — then decide with your own.

The lab-grown reset

The biggest change in ring budgeting since the slogan itself: lab-grown diamonds are chemically identical to mined ones and now cost 70-80% less at equal specs. Roughly a third of US engagement-ring stones were lab-grown by the mid-2020s, and the share keeps climbing.

The trade-off is resale: neither natural nor lab-grown rings are investments, but natural stones hold some value while lab-grown prices keep falling. If you're keeping the ring forever — the usual plan — the lab-grown discount is almost pure savings.

A worked example: $65,000 salary

Run the classic rule: $65,000 × 2 ÷ 12 = $10,833, comfortably $8,667–$13,000. That's 16.7% of a year's gross income, or $903 a month saved for twelve months. As a natural stone it buys about a 1.5-carat round at G-H/VS-SI specs; the same look lab-grown runs near $2,700, or the full $10,833 carries something pushing 3 carats. Against the $4,600 national average, the rule's number is 2.4× — that gap is the slogan premium, and declining it is free. Add the tail costs: about $109–$217 a year to insure (1-2% of value), plus sales tax at checkout.

A saner way to pick the number

  1. Start from cash, not from a rule. What you can pay without financing is the budget. A ring that arrives with 20% interest attached costs more than the bigger stone would have.
  2. Price the stone she/he/they will actually wear. Some people want size, some want sparkle, some want a vintage setting. The 4Cs trade against each other; cut matters most for brilliance.
  3. Price the lab-grown twin. If you can't tell them apart — nobody can, by eye — decide whether the origin is worth thousands to you. Either answer is fine; make it consciously.
  4. Keep 10% back. Sales tax, resizing, and insurance's first year hide at the edges of every purchase.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the average engagement ring cost?

The Knot's 2025 Jewelry & Engagement Study puts the average at $4,600. Recent annual studies have ranged from roughly $4,600 to $6,000, so anything in that band is a normal spend. The average is pulled up by a small share of five-figure purchases; plenty of perfectly good rings cost $2,000 or less, especially lab-grown.

Is the two months' salary rule real?

It's an advertising slogan, not a norm. De Beers marketed "one month's salary" starting in the 1930s and nudged it to two months in 1980s campaigns. Actual US spending averages about $4,600 — under one month of gross pay for the median household, and closer to half that against take-home.

How much cheaper are lab-grown diamonds?

Typically 70-80% below natural for the same carat, color, and clarity. They are chemically and optically identical — the difference is origin and resale value. A $6,000 natural 1-carat budget becomes a $1,200-1,800 lab-grown stone, or the same money carries 2.5-3 carats.

What carat size do most people buy?

US center stones cluster around 1 to 1.5 carats in industry surveys, with 1 carat the most common single choice. Region matters less than budget: the same $4,600 average buys a bigger stone in a lab-grown or fancy-shape configuration than in a round natural.

Should the ring budget come out of savings or financing?

Savings, whenever the timeline allows. Store financing at typical rates adds 10-20% to the true cost, and rings are not investments — natural diamond resale usually recovers a fraction of retail. If timing forces financing, a 0% intro-APR card paid off inside the window beats a store installment plan.
