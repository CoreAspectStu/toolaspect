How the MBA ROI Calculator Works

Most "is an MBA worth it" content compares tuition to a salary bump and calls it a day. That misses the largest line item: for a full-time program, the two years of income you give up. This calculator prices the whole decision.

The formulas

Forgone salary = current salary with raises, summed over the program years: at $75,000 and 3% raises, year one gives up $77,250 and year two $79,567.50 — $156,818 total (assume you'd have kept earning during the program for online/part-time formats; set % forgone to 0). Total investment = tuition + other costs − employer help + forgone salary. Annual uplift = post-MBA salary − current salary (kept flat for simplicity; if the MBA also accelerates your raises, the real ROI beats this estimate). Break-even = investment ÷ uplift. NPV discounts the uplift stream at your discount rate for the career horizon, then subtracts the investment — money next decade is worth less than money now, and NPV respects that.

How to use it

Pick your format first — the presets in the tables above give realistic tuition ranges. Pull your program's real cost of attendance, not just tuition: fees, health insurance, and relocation matter. Set the post-MBA salary from your target role's posted ranges, not the school's marketing median, and if you're already ten years into your career, use a "no MBA" trajectory you actually believe. The discount rate defaults to 5%; use 7% if you'd otherwise be investing aggressively.

A worked example — full-time

$110,000 tuition and fees, $30,000 of living costs above your current burn, two years out of a $75,000 job with 3% raises ($156,818 forgone). Total investment: $296,818. The target role pays $115,000 — a $40,000 annual uplift. Break-even lands at 7.4 years after graduation. Over a 10-year horizon the simple net gain is $103,183; discounted at 5%, the NPV is $12,052 — positive, but thin enough that the program's ranking, your financing (student loans at 7%+ would eat it), and your actual offer matter enormously.

A worked example — online

Same person, $40,000 online MBA while working: $5,000 of books and fees, zero forgone salary. Investment: $45,000. A more modest $12,000 bump (promotion into management) breaks even in 3.8 years and carries a 10-year NPV of $47,661 at 5%. Less upside than a full-time switch into consulting — and far less risk.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is an MBA worth it financially?

Depends entirely on the salary delta and who pays the opportunity cost. Benchmark full-time case: $110,000 tuition plus $30,000 other costs plus $156,818 of two years' forgone salary (with raises) makes a $296,818 total investment. Against a $40,000 salary bump, that breaks even in year 8 and nets about $103,000 over 10 years — positive, but not a windfall. Cut the program cost to a $45,000 online MBA while working and the same salary bump pays back in 3.8 years.

How much does an MBA cost?

Published 2025-26 ranges: top full-time programs run about $150,000-$200,000 in tuition and fees for two years — closer to $340,000-$390,000 all-in once living costs and forgone salary count; state flagship full-time MBAs about $50,000-$100,000; online MBAs $15,000-$60,000 at state schools, $100,000+ at a few private brands; executive MBAs $80,000-$200,000, often partly employer-funded.

What is the average MBA salary?

Recent GMAC corporate recruiter surveys put the median US starting salary for full-time MBA hires at roughly $115,000-$125,000, against about $75,000-$85,000 for bachelor's-only hires at the same employers. The premium shrinks outside consulting, tech, and finance, and it compounds most for career-switchers who land in higher-paying functions.

What is the biggest cost of a full-time MBA?

Usually the salary you stop earning, not the tuition. Two years at $75,000 with 3% raises is $156,818 of forgone income — more than many programs' entire tuition. That's why online and part-time MBAs dominate on ROI: you keep the paycheck, and the investment drops to tuition alone.

Do part-time and online MBAs pay off faster?

Yes, structurally. A $40,000 online MBA with a $12,000 salary bump is a 3.8-year payback and about $47,661 positive NPV over 10 years at a 5% discount rate, because there's no forgone salary. The catch: employer sponsorship and internal promotions drive most of those bumps, so your current employer's ceiling matters as much as the degree.

What salary jump do I need to make an MBA worth it?

Divide your total investment by the years you'll work after graduating. A $296,818 full-time investment over a 10-year horizon needs about $29,700 a year of extra salary just to break even, before discounting — closer to $38,500 once you discount future raises at 5%. Anything above that is real return. The calculator below runs your exact numbers.
