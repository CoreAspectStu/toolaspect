Frequently Asked Questions

Is BMR the same as RMR?

Almost, and the two get used interchangeably. BMR is measured under strict lab conditions, fasted and lying still in a temperature-neutral room. RMR (resting metabolic rate) is measured under looser conditions and comes out slightly higher. For practical purposes, treat formulas labeled either way as the same estimate.

Does BMR include digestion and exercise?

No. BMR covers only resting functions: brain, heart, kidneys, breathing, temperature regulation. Digestion adds roughly 10% of intake on top (the thermic effect of food), and activity adds the rest. Stack all three and you get TDEE, total daily energy expenditure.

Why is my BMR different on different websites?

Usually because they use different equations on the same body. The 1919 Harris-Benedict formula and its 1984 revision still power many sites, and they run roughly 5% higher than Mifflin-St Jeor for most people, a gap of 100 kcal/day or more. Fitness trackers add their own estimates on top of either formula.

Can your BMR be too slow?

Genuinely low BMR from a medical cause, like untreated hypothyroidism, exists but is uncommon. For most people the difference between a 'fast' and 'slow' metabolism is lean mass and daily movement, both of which respond to training. If unexplained weight change comes with fatigue or other symptoms, that's a doctor conversation, not a formula problem.

How the Twitch Bit Calculator Works

Twitch pays streamers one cent per bit cheered in their channel, so the math is deliberately simple: streamer value = bits × $0.01. Enter a bit count and the calculator shows the dollar value to the streamer, along with the cheer level thresholds and what the buyer typically pays for that many bits. Everything runs locally in your browser — no login, no data collection.

The buyer's side is where confusion arises, because bits are sold in bundles whose per-bit price is slightly above one cent at small sizes and dips below it at the largest tiers. The bundle prices above are Twitch's typical published list prices; check the current in-app pricing when it matters, since promotions and regional pricing change the effective rate.

The economics: a viewer pays Twitch for bits, and Twitch passes one cent per bit to the streamer when it is cheered. That means Twitch's gross margin on bits is the difference between the bundle price and the payout — approximately half at typical list prices, before payment processing and revenue share with the streamer's other arrangements. Cheering is therefore best understood as a tip with a platform premium attached, not a one-to-one currency transfer.

Payout mechanics matter to streamers: bit revenue accumulates in the dashboard and is paid out through the standard Twitch payout system once the account meets the minimum payout threshold (historically $100, with faster lower thresholds available for a fee). Bits are one of several monetization streams alongside subscriptions, ad revenue, and off-platform tips, and streamers commonly compare bits to direct tips precisely because direct tips avoid the bundle markup.

Cheer Levels and Alerts

Beyond money, bits have ceremony. Cheering with larger amounts unlocks animated badges and chat effects, and many streamers set up channel point bonuses or on-screen alerts at thresholds. Common milestone conventions include 100 bits for a standard alert animation, 1,000 bits for a larger display, and 5,000/10,000 bits for the top-tier effects — but the exact alert behavior depends entirely on each streamer's configured overlays, not on Twitch itself. The calculator reports these thresholds so you know what a given cheer will look like in a typical channel.

Bulk bits also carry badge seniority: cheering displays a badge whose color and longevity reflect cumulative bits cheered in that channel, which is a status signal regulars care about. None of this changes the underlying one-cent-per-bit payout.

Bits in Context Against Other Support Methods

Viewers choosing how to support a channel usually weigh three routes: bits, subscriptions, and off-platform tips. A 500-bit cheer costs the buyer roughly $6-$7 at list bundle pricing and delivers $5.00 to the streamer. A Tier 1 subscription costs about $4.99 monthly and typically pays the streamer roughly half. A $5 off-platform tip through a third-party service transfers most of the money but takes the viewer outside the Twitch experience and may incur its own processing fees.

None of these routes is strictly dominant. Bits keep the interaction inside chat with badges and alerts, which is part of what the premium pays for; subscriptions are recurring, which streamers value for predictability; tips maximize the transferred share. Streamers themselves often say the mix matters more than any single channel, and the calculator's purpose is to make the arithmetic behind one of those channels transparent before you cheer.

For streamers tracking income, the practical habit is to log bit revenue separately from subscriptions and payouts in the dashboard, because the payout cycles and reporting differ, and tax reporting covers all of it as self-employment income where applicable.

A Brief Note on Bit History

Bits arrived in 2016 as Twitch's in-platform currency, taking aim at third-party tipping services by anchoring tips to chat ceremony — badges, animations, leaderboards — that outside services could not replicate. The one-cent-per-bit payout has stayed fixed since launch, while bundle pricing and promotions have shifted over time, which is why the buyer's cost column and the streamer's value column in the table above never quite match.

For streamers, the fixed cent value is a rare point of simplicity in platform monetization: unlike ad revenue, which swings with rates and seasons, and subscriptions, which churn month to month, a bit is always worth exactly what the calculator says. The planning implication is straightforward — bit income can be projected from cheer history with no rate uncertainty, making it one of the easier revenue lines to budget.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much is 1,000 bits worth? $10.00 to the streamer. Bits pay one cent each, so any bit count converts by moving the decimal two places.

What do bits cost the buyer? Slightly more than face value in most bundles and slightly less in the largest ones — typical list prices run about $19.95 for 1,500 bits and about $126 for 10,000, but promotions shift these.

Do streamers get all the bits' value? They receive one cent per bit; the rest of the bundle price is Twitch's. Roughly half the retail price reaches the streamer.

How do bits compare to subscriptions? A Tier 1 subscription costs about $4.99 monthly and typically pays the streamer roughly half after platform and processing splits; a 500-bit cheer pays $5.00 flat. Direct tips via third parties usually transfer the highest share to the streamer.

When do streamers get paid? Bit revenue is paid through Twitch's standard payout cycle once the minimum payout threshold is met.
