How the Tire DOT Age Decoder Works

Since 2000, every tire sold in the US carries a DOT serial ending in four digits that encode the week and year it was built. That code is the only honest answer to "how old are my tires" — mileage and tread depth tell you nothing about the rubber's calendar age, and calendar age is what fails.

The formula

Weeks 1-2 digits of the code = week of the year; last 2 digits = year since 2000. The approximate build date is January 1 of that year plus (week − 1) × 7 days — the exact day within the week isn't stamped, so the decoder reports the start of the build week. Age is the difference between your check date and that build date, in years and months. The verdict bands: under 5 years, normal service; 5 to 6, inspect annually; 6 to 10, plan replacement — you're past the conservative mark; 10-plus, replace now regardless of tread.

How to use it

Crouch at the sidewall, find "DOT", and read the last four characters of the code — 11 or 12 characters total including plant and size codes. Type them in. The tool also accepts a custom check date if you're pricing a used car you inspected last month or working a maintenance log. Check all four tires plus the spare: they rarely share a birthday, and the oldest one sets the clock.

A worked example

You're shopping a used car in August 2026 and the tires read 2419. Week 24 of 2019 begins June 11, 2019, so the tires were 7 years 2 months old — well past the 6-year conservative mark (June 2025) but inside the 10-year window (replace by June 2029). The right move: budget one set of tires into the purchase within the next year or two, and check the spare before you drive home, since a 20-year-old code like 3206 (week 32 of 2006) is a decoration, not a spare.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I read the DOT code on my tires?

Find the letters DOT on the sidewall and read the last four digits of the string that follows — those four are the date code. The first two digits are the week and the last two are the year: 2419 means the 24th week of 2019, which lands in mid-June 2019. The full DOT string is stamped on only one sidewall, so if you see a short code, check the tire's inner side.

How old are my tires from the DOT code?

Subtract the manufacture year from the current year and adjust for the week. A tire stamped 2419 (week 24 of 2019) was about 7 years 2 months old as of late August 2026. The decoder above does this for any code, including your spare.

Do tires have an expiration date?

No law sets one, and tread depth alone doesn't decide it. Major manufacturers including Michelin and Continental advise replacing tires no later than 10 years from date of manufacture, with an annual inspection by a tire specialist once a tire passes 5 years — even if the tread looks fine. Many retailers, including Discount Tire, recommend replacement at 6 years as the conservative mark, especially in hot climates.

Is a 10-year-old tire safe to drive on?

No — replace it. Rubber compounds harden and internal steel and nylon belts degrade with age and heat cycles, and older tires fail at higher rates in studies of tire-related crashes. The 10-year limit applies from the date of manufacture regardless of tread depth or miles driven, and it includes the spare, which often ages out first because it's rarely replaced.

What does a 3-digit DOT date code mean?

The tire was made in the 1990s. Before 2000, the date code used three digits: two for the week and one for the year of that decade, with a small triangle stamped after it to mark the 1990s. Any 3-digit code means a tire at least 26 years old today — a showpiece, not a road tire.

Why do all four tires show different DOT dates?

Tires are made in batches and installed in pairs, so a few weeks or months of spread across an axle set is normal and harmless. What matters is the oldest tire: age out the set on the oldest code's clock, and expect a shop to recommend replacing all four once two need replacing on an AWD vehicle.
