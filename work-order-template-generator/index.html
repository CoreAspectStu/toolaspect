How the Work Order Template Generator Works

A work order is authorization, record, and receipt in one page: it says what work is approved, captures what was actually done, and totals what it cost in labor and parts. The math is simple, which is exactly why it should be automatic — nobody's multiplying qty × unit on a clipboard at 4:45 on a Friday.

The formula

Labor amount = hours × rate, summed across labor lines. Parts amount = qty × unit cost, summed across parts lines. Subtotal = labor + parts. Tax = subtotal × rate. Grand total = subtotal + tax. The generator recalculates every field live, mints sequential WO-YYYY-NNNN numbers so paper and spreadsheet never fork, and exports the same data three ways: CSV for import into a CMMS, Excel (.xls) for planners, PDF via print for the clipboard.

How to use it

Fill the header (the asset field is the one that matters most — it's the key your maintenance history hangs on), enter labor as hours and rate, add part lines as needed, and set your tax rate or zero it for internal work orders. Export CSV for records, Excel for planning, or print for the field. The QR turns the printed copy into a doorway back to this form.

A worked example

The defaults, as loaded: 3.5 hours × $85 = $297.50 labor. Parts: 2 pleated filters × $12.99 = $25.98, one belt × $45.00 = $45.00, 4 screws × $3.75 = $15.00, parts subtotal $85.98. Subtotal $383.48, tax at 7% = $26.84, grand total $410.32. Add a half hour of overtime at time-and-a-half ($127.50) and labor climbs to $361.25 and the total to $478.54 — overtime is where WO budgets quietly double, which is why it gets its own line.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should a work order include?

Eight fields minimum: a unique WO number, the date, the asset or location, the priority, who requested it, the task description, the labor lines (hours x rate), and the parts lines (qty x unit cost), plus a tax line and grand total if you bill internally or externally. Space for cause-of-failure and follow-up notes is what turns a form into maintenance data.

What is the difference between a work order and an invoice?

A work order authorizes and records work before and during the job — it says what will be done, by whom, with what parts. An invoice bills for completed work afterward. A completed work order with labor and parts totals is exactly the backup an invoice needs, which is why the two documents share so much structure.

What are the types of work orders?

The standard five: preventive (scheduled PM from a checklist), corrective (repair after something fails), predictive or condition-based (triggered by a sensor reading or oil analysis), emergency (safety or production threat, respond now), and improvement or project work. Healthy programs push most hours into the preventive bucket — a common KPI target is 70-80% planned work.

How do I number work orders?

One sequence, never reused: a year prefix plus a counter — WO-2026-0147 tells you the year at a glance and sorts cleanly. The auto-number button here mints WO-YYYY-NNNN numbers so paper and spreadsheet never fork into duplicate numbers, which is the fastest way to lose a maintenance history.
