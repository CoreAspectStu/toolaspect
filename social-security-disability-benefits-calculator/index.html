How the SSDI Calculator Works

Social Security Disability Insurance isn't charity, and it isn't welfare. It's insurance you already paid for. Part of every paycheck's FICA tax bought work credits, and those credits fund a benefit that kicks in when a medical condition keeps you from working for at least a year. Most people 31 and older need 20 credits earned in the 10 years before the disability began, which works out to roughly five of those years spent working. Younger workers need fewer.

The formula

Your payment has nothing to do with how severe your condition is. It comes from your earnings record. SSA takes your best earning years, adjusts them for historical wage growth, and averages them into a monthly figure called your AIME (average indexed monthly earnings). The 2025 formula then counts 90% of the first $1,226 of AIME, 32% of the amount between $1,226 and $7,391, and 15% of anything above that. The total is rounded down to the nearest dime, an SSA convention, and becomes your primary insurance amount, or PIA. Bend points shift every year with the national wage index, so this page reflects 2025.

How to use it

If you've checked your Social Security statement recently, AIME mode gives the closest estimate. Otherwise switch to income mode and enter your average annual earnings; the calculator divides by 12 as a stand-in for AIME. That shortcut skips the wage indexing and 35-year averaging SSA actually does, so treat it as a ballpark. Tick the family box to see the household estimate, and enter any current work earnings to check yourself against the substantial gainful activity limit: above $1,620 a month in 2025 ($2,700 if you're blind), SSA generally won't approve a new claim.

A worked example

Say your AIME is $5,000. The first $1,226 counts at 90%, which is $1,103.40. The remaining $3,774 sits in the 32% band and adds $1,207.68. Together that's $2,311.08, and the round-down-to-the-dime rule trims it to $2,311.00 a month, or $27,732 a year. That lands well above the 2025 average disabled-worker benefit of roughly $1,580. If a spouse or children also qualify on your record, the 1.5× family estimate here comes to $3,466.50 a month. The 1.5 multiplier is the conservative low end of the 150% to 180% range used on the retirement side; SSDI's family maximum uses a different formula and can come in lower for modest earners.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does SSDI pay per month?

The 2025 average for a disabled worker is about $1,580 a month, and the maximum is $4,018. Most recipients land somewhere between $1,000 and $2,500. Your exact amount comes from your lifetime earnings record, not from how severe your condition is or what your bills look like.

How is SSDI calculated?

Social Security averages your highest-earning years, adjusted for wage growth, into a monthly figure called your AIME. The 2025 formula then counts 90% of the first $1,226 of AIME, 32% of the amount between $1,226 and $7,391, and 15% of anything above that. The total, rounded down to the nearest dime, is your monthly benefit.

What's the most SSDI will pay?

$4,018 a month in 2025. Getting there takes earnings at or above Social Security's taxable maximum ($176,100 in 2025) for most of a 35-year career, so very few people receive the full amount. The cap adjusts each year with the cost-of-living increase.

Can I work while on SSDI?

A little. Earning more than $1,620 a month in 2025 ($2,700 if you're blind) counts as substantial gainful activity and generally makes you ineligible. Once approved, you also get a trial work period: nine months (any month over $1,160 counts as one) where you can test working while keeping full benefits.

What's the difference between SSDI and SSI?

SSDI is insurance you earned through payroll taxes, so the amount depends on your work history and it brings Medicare after 24 months. SSI is need-based for people with little income and under $2,000 in assets ($3,000 for couples), paying up to $967 a month in 2025 with Medicaid in most states. Some people qualify for both at once.
