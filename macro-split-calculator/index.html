How the Macro Split Calculator Works

Calories are the budget; macros are how you spend it. Protein and carbs cost 4 calories a gram, fat costs 9, and alcohol (the forgotten fourth macro) costs 7. This calculator does the division both ways people actually plan: by percentage split, or by anchoring protein to body weight and letting carbs take the leftovers.

The formulas

Grams = calories × percentage ÷ calories-per-gram. So protein grams = calories × P% ÷ 4, carb grams = calories × C% ÷ 4, fat grams = calories × F% ÷ 9. The grams must re-add to your calorie total, and the calculator shows that check line every time. In per-pound mode: protein grams = weight × g/lb, fat grams = calories × F% ÷ 9, and carbs = (calories − protein×4 − fat×9) ÷ 4.

How to use it

Start from your calorie target: if you don't have one, the calorie deficit calculator or calorie calculator will produce it from your stats. Pick a preset or dial custom percentages; the grams update as you type. Training hard? Anchor protein first (per-pound mode) and treat carbs as the performance lever that's left over.

Worked example 1: percentage split

2,400 calories at 40/30/30. Carbs: 2,400 × 0.40 ÷ 4 = 240g. Protein: 2,400 × 0.30 ÷ 4 = 180g. Fat: 2,400 × 0.30 ÷ 9 = 80g. Check: 240×4 + 180×4 + 80×9 = 960 + 720 + 720 = 2,400. Exact, no rounding drift, because 30% of 2,400 divides cleanly by both 4 and 9.

Worked example 2: protein anchored to weight

A 180-lb lifter cutting on 2,224 calories wants 0.8 g/lb of protein and 30% of calories from fat. Protein = 180 × 0.8 = 144g (576 cal). Fat = 2,224 × 0.30 ÷ 9 = 74g (668 cal, using 74.1g rounded). Carbs get what's left: (2,224 − 576 − 667) ÷ 4 = 245g. That's a 44/26/30 split in percentage terms, and the extra carbohydrate is exactly why anchor-protein mode beats a fixed ratio for people who train.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I convert macro percentages to grams?

Multiply your daily calories by each percentage, then divide by calories per gram: 4 for protein, 4 for carbs, 9 for fat. On 2,400 calories at 40% carbs, 30% protein, 30% fat, that's 2,400 × 0.40 ÷ 4 = 240g carbs, 2,400 × 0.30 ÷ 4 = 180g protein, and 2,400 × 0.30 ÷ 9 = 80g fat. The grams always have to add back to your calorie total: 240×4 + 180×4 + 80×9 = 2,400.

What is the best macro split for fat loss?

Protein is the lever that matters. Set protein at 0.7 to 1 g per pound of body weight to protect muscle and control hunger, put fat at 20 to 35% of calories for hormones and fat-soluble vitamins, and let carbs fill the rest. For a 180-lb man eating 2,224 calories, a 35/35/30 split works out to about 195g protein, 195g carbs, and 74g fat.

What is the healthiest macro ratio?

The National Academies' Acceptable Macronutrient Distribution Ranges allow protein 10–35%, carbs 45–65%, and fat 20–35% of calories, and any split inside those bands can be healthy. The band that fits you depends on training, preference, and what you'll actually eat. Extremely low carb (keto's 5–10%) sits outside the AMDR and is a therapeutic tool more than a default.

How much protein do I need to build muscle?

The evidence-based range for building muscle is about 1.4 to 2.0 g per kg of body weight (0.6 to 0.9 g per lb) per day, spread across 3 or 4 meals with 20 to 40g each. More than that isn't harmful for healthy kidneys, but returns flatten. A 180-lb lifter lands around 115 to 160g a day; the 2.2 g per lb crowd is paying for expensive urine.

Do alcohol calories count in the split?

Alcohol is the fourth macro at 7 calories per gram, and tracking apps usually log it separately. It's not essential to fit it into the P/C/F split, but those calories do come out of your budget. A practical habit: count each drink as roughly the calories on the label and pull them from your carb or fat allowance for the day.

Should my macros change on rest days?

Keep protein identical every day; muscle repair happens on rest days. Carbs can float with activity, dropping 50 to 100g on a rest day and rising before hard sessions, while fat stays roughly steady. Some people prefer one flat number for simplicity, which is fine; the weekly average drives results more than daily precision.
