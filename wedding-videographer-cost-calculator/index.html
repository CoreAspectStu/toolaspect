How the Wedding Videographer Cost Calculator Works

Videography prices the same way photography does: hours times a market rate, with add-ons stacked on top. The difference is what's behind the rate. A videographer's shoot day is the smaller half of the job; the edit consumes 30-60 hours per wedding. This calculator prices your combination from hourly rates that vary by market, layers the add-ons, and shows what a same-studio photo bundle would save.

The formula

Package = hours × hourly rate for your market, plus each add-on's flat price. Hourly rates: $150-$200 in small towns and rural areas ($175 typical), $225-$275 in mid-size metros ($250 typical), and $325-$450 in major metros ($375 typical). A second videographer adds $300-$700 ($450 typical), drone coverage $200-$500 ($350), social edits $150-$400 ($250), and a full-length film $400-$1,200 ($700). Tick the bundle box and the calculator applies the 10-25% discount hybrid studios typically give on combined photo-video bookings: 10% at the low end, 15% typical, 25% at the top.

How to use it

Set coverage to the arc you want on film — prep through the first dances is the standard 8; couples who only care about vows, toasts, and the highlight often buy 6 and stop coverage after dinner. Pick your market honestly, tick the add-ons you're considering, and use the bundle box only if you'd genuinely book one studio for both crafts. The low and high estimates bracket the realistic quote range, not a negotiation floor.

A worked example

A San Francisco couple wants 10 hours, a second videographer, and drone coverage of an outdoor ceremony. Coverage: 10 × $375 = $3,750 typical for a major metro. Add-ons: +$450 and +$350. Typical total: $4,550, with the range running $3,750 (low rates on everything) to $5,700 (top of every band). That's 98% above the $2,300 national average, which is exactly what you'd expect in one of the country's priciest markets.

Flip it: 6 hours, mid-size metro, no add-ons. 6 × $250 = $1,500 typical, range $1,350-$1,650. That's a real number for a talented two-person studio's highlight package — and it keeps vows, toasts, and first dances on film for less than a third of what the couple paying $4,550 spends.

Now the bundle. If that San Francisco couple books photography and videography from one hybrid studio, the 15% typical discount takes $4,550 down to about $3,868 — a $683 saving, with 10% ($4,095) and 25% ($3,413) as the outer bounds. Worth it only if the studio's photography portfolio matches what a dedicated photographer would deliver for the money.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a wedding videographer cost?

Couples who add videography spend about $2,300 on average, per The Knot's Real Weddings Study, and only around a third of couples hire one at all. For the standard 8-hour package, expect roughly $1,400 in a small town, $2,000 in a mid-size metro, and $3,000 in NYC/LA/SF-class markets. Add-ons like a second videographer ($300-$700) or drone coverage ($200-$500) push the final bill higher.

Is a wedding videographer worth the cost?

For most couples who book one, yes. Video is the only vendor product that captures vows, toasts, and speeches with sound, and industry surveys consistently flag skipped videography as one of the most common post-wedding regrets. If the full package strains the budget, a 6-hour highlight-only package at $1,350-$1,650 in a mid-size metro keeps the moments that matter on film.

How much does a second videographer cost?

Most studios add a second videographer for $300-$700, with $450 typical. Two cameras matter even more in video than photography: one stays on the couple during vows while the other covers reactions, and the editor can cut between angles instead of relying on a single static shot. Above 120 guests, or with a Catholic-length ceremony, it's close to mandatory for a good edit.

Why does wedding videography cost almost as much as photography?

You're paying for editing, not filming. An 8-hour shoot becomes 30-60 hours of post-production: syncing audio from ceremony mics, color correcting multiple cameras, cutting the highlight film, and exporting delivery formats. Studios also carry more gear per shooter (bodies, audio, lights, drones). That's why per-hour coverage rates of $150-$450 still land the typical 8-hour package near $2,000 in a mid-size metro.

Should I book photo and video from the same studio?

Hybrid photo-video studios typically discount the combined booking 10-25%, which on a mid-size-metro 8-hour video package saves $200-$500. The trade-off: you're locked into one team's style on both products, and the best photographer in your market may not employ an equally strong videographer. If both portfolios clear your bar, take the bundle. If not, book separately.

How do I save money on wedding videography?

Trim coverage before quality: 6 hours covering prep through toasts beats 10 hours that end after open dancing. Skip raw footage (usually $200-$800), skip the full-length documentary edit ($400-$1,200) unless grandparents will actually watch it, book a newer studio building its reel, and marry Friday or Sunday, when many studios discount 10-15%.
