How the Tire Size Calculator Works

Tire sizes look like part numbers but they're pure geometry. Decode them once and every comparison on this page becomes arithmetic.

The formula

Sidewall height = width × aspect ratio ÷ 100 (225 × 0.65 = 146.25mm). Overall diameter = wheel diameter + 2 × sidewall, all in one unit: 17 in × 25.4 + 2 × 146.25mm = 724.3mm, or 28.52 inches. Circumference = π × diameter = 89.6 inches, and revs per mile = 63,360 ÷ 89.6 = 707. The swap's effect on your dash: actual speed = indicated × (new diameter ÷ old diameter), same ratio for odometer distance.

How to use it

Type both sizes in — the code from your current sidewall and the size you're considering. The verdict line grades the swap: within 1% is ideal, within 3% acceptable, beyond that expect speedometer drift you can feel and electronics that may notice. This calculator covers the metric P-metric system (205/55R16 style); letter-type and flotation sizes (like 31x10.50R15) need converting first.

A worked example

Upgrading from 225/65R17 to 235/65R17 — same wheel, 10mm wider, same profile. New sidewall: 235 × 0.65 = 152.75mm. New diameter: 431.8mm + 305.5mm = 737.3mm (29.03 in), which is +1.8% over the original 724.3mm.

What that does: revs per mile drop from 707 to 695 (12.5 fewer rotations every mile). At an indicated 60 mph you're actually traveling 61.1 mph; every 100 miles the odometer records is really 101.8. Acceptable on paper — and a real speedometer calibration question at a 10-over traffic stop. The calculator exists so this is a decision, not an accident.

Frequently Asked Questions

What do the numbers in a tire size mean?

In 225/65R17: 225 is the section width in millimeters, 65 is the aspect ratio (sidewall height as a percent of width, so 65% of 225 = 146.25mm), R means radial construction, and 17 is the wheel diameter in inches. Overall diameter = 17in + 2 × 146.25mm converted to inches = 28.52 inches, or 724mm.

How much can tire diameter differ before it's a problem?

Stay within 3% of the original overall diameter. Within 1% is ideal — speedometer and odometer error stay under 1 mph at 60. Beyond 3%, speedometer error becomes noticeable, odometer accuracy drifts, ABS and traction-control logic can misbehave, and gearing changes enough to affect acceleration and fuel economy.

Will bigger tires make my speedometer read wrong?

Yes. The speedometer counts wheel revolutions, so a taller tire travels farther per revolution than the system assumes. Going from 225/65R17 to 235/65R17 (+1.8% diameter) makes an indicated 60 mph an actual 61.1 mph — you're speeding while the dash says you aren't. Shorter tires understate actual speed the same way.

Can I use a 235 tire instead of a 225?

Same wheel diameter, often yes: 235/65R17 is only 1.8% taller than 225/65R17, inside the 3% guideline. But width needs wheel and clearance room — the 235 is 10mm wider and needs an appropriately wide rim (a 7-inch wheel handles both), plus clearance at full lock and suspension compression. Check a fitment guide for your exact vehicle before buying.

What is plus sizing?

Plus sizing keeps overall diameter constant while shrinking sidewalls on bigger wheels: 205/55R16 to 225/45R17 is +0.38% and 225/40R18 is +0.84% — all three turn the axle at nearly the same rate. You get sharper steering response and a shorter, stiffer sidewall; ride quality and tire price usually pay for it.

Do wider tires hurt fuel economy?

Usually a little. Wider tires weigh more and roll with more resistance — expect a 1 to 3% fuel-economy penalty for a common one-or-two-step width increase, more if you choose an aggressive tread. Diameter changes matter too: taller tires effectively gear the car taller, which can help highway economy and hurt around-town response.
