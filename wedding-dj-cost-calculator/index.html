How the Wedding DJ Cost Calculator Works

DJ quotes look simple until the add-ons land. Nearly every professional prices a 4-hour reception base, then charges for the hours, rooms, and lighting your specific wedding needs. This calculator assembles that bill the way a DJ's contract would.

The formula

Total = tier base price + (extra hours × $150) + sum of checked add-ons. The low and high ends swap the base to the tier's floor and ceiling and price extra hours at $100 and $250 respectively, the common overtime bounds. The band line pulls typical 4-hour pricing by piece count — $2,200 for a trio up to $7,000 for an 8–10 piece — and shows the difference against your DJ total.

How to use it

Pick the tier that matches the DJs you're actually emailing; most couples comparing three quotes are looking at standard professionals. Set the hours honestly — a 6 PM to midnight reception is six hours, not four, and that extra $300 is the most commonly forgotten line in music budgets. Check only the add-ons on your shortlist, then flip the band toggle to see what live music would cost you instead.

A worked example

A couple books a standard professional ($1,400 for four hours), extends to six hours for a full evening (+2 × $150 = $300), and wants ceremony sound with MC work ($300) plus uplighting in the reception hall ($350). The total: $2,350, with quotes likely ranging $1,850 to $3,150 across their shortlist. The 5-piece band they also loved quotes $4,000 for the same hours — $1,650 more, which is most of a photography package. At 120 guests, the DJ runs about $20 per guest for the entire night's music.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a wedding DJ cost?

Couples pay an average of $1,800 for a wedding DJ, per The Knot's 2026 Real Weddings Study. The base 4-hour package runs $1,000 to $2,000 with a standard professional, $500 to $1,200 with an emerging or part-time DJ, and $1,500 to $3,500 with a premium full-time company. Most final bills land above the base once hours and add-ons are counted.

How much do wedding DJs charge per extra hour?

Most DJs bill overtime at $100 to $250 per hour, with $150 the common midpoint. Booking six hours instead of four typically adds $300. Overtime requested on the wedding night itself is often priced at the top of that range or higher, so lock the hours in the contract up front.

Is a band or DJ cheaper for a wedding?

A DJ, usually by a wide margin. Live bands average $4,500 nationally while DJs average $1,800. A typical 5-piece band quotes around $4,000 for four hours against roughly $2,350 for a DJ loaded with add-ons — about $1,650 difference. Even a 3-piece band at $2,200 runs close to a premium DJ with lighting.

What's included in a standard wedding DJ package?

A base package almost always covers the 4-hour reception, professional sound system, one or two planning consultations, MC duties (introductions, toasts, schedule cues), and a basic dance-floor light. Setup and teardown are included but usually don't count against your paid hours. Travel beyond about 30 miles, ceremony sound, and uplighting are the most common add-on charges.

How far in advance should I book a wedding DJ?

Peak-season Saturdays in metro areas book 8 to 12 months out, and the best-reviewed DJs go first after venues. Off-peak and Friday or Sunday dates give you 4 to 6 months of breathing room. Booking early also locks the current year's pricing, which typically rises 3 to 5 percent annually.

Do wedding DJs charge for setup and teardown?

Rarely as a separate line — setup and teardown are normally bundled into the package price and don't eat your dance time. What can cost extra is same-day access restrictions: if the venue only allows a 1-hour load-in for a system that takes 2 hours to build, the DJ may bill for the idle hour or a second assistant.
