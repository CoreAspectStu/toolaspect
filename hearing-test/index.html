Frequently Asked Questions

What does full care board include?

Feed and hay, daily stall cleaning, fresh water, daily turnout, and basic husbandry like holding for the farrier at many barns. You show up and ride. Not included at most barns: lessons, blanketing, supplements, private paddock access, trailer parking, and anything the vet or farrier bills directly.

Why is board so much more expensive near cities?

Land. A barn near a major metro pays metro real-estate prices for acreage, and competition for stalls is fierce because riders outnumber facilities. In New Jersey, Southern California, and South Florida, full care commonly runs $800 to $2,000-plus a month, while the same care in rural farm country can cost $400 to $650.

Can I negotiate horse board prices?

Sometimes, but not the sticker rate. Ask instead about paying quarterly in advance for a discount, a reduced rate for self-maintaining your stall, work-off arrangements (a few hours of chores a week in exchange for board credit), or an off-season rate. Barns are more flexible on terms than on the base number.

Is self-care board actually cheaper?

On paper yes, often $100 to $400 a month versus $500-plus for full care. In practice you buy your own hay and bedding, feed twice a day every day, and clean the stall yourself. For one horse, hay alone runs roughly 146 fifty-pound bales a year, so at $8 a bale that's about $1,168, plus bedding and your time. The savings are real but smaller than the rate gap suggests.

What does this hearing test measure?

This test plays pure tones at frequencies from 125 Hz up to 15 kHz, one ear at a time, and records the highest frequency you report hearing. Most healthy young adults hear up to about 15–17 kHz; sensitivity to high frequencies naturally declines with age, which is what the fun "hearing age" figure estimates.

How accurate is an online hearing test?

A browser test cannot replace an audiogram from an audiologist. Results depend heavily on your headphones, background noise, and volume calibration — that's why the calibration step matters. Treat the result as a rough indicator: if you consistently cannot hear frequencies below 8 kHz, or one ear scores much worse than the other, book a professional hearing check.

Why can I hear high frequencies in one ear but not the other?

Small differences between ears are normal, but a large gap can indicate noise damage or infection in the weaker ear. Loud concerts, headphones at high volume, and workplace noise are the most common causes of high-frequency loss. If the gap is obvious, get both ears checked properly.
