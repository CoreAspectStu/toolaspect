How does this color blindness test work?

Each plate is a field of randomly sized dots in red, orange, and green families, all matched for brightness. A number is drawn inside the dot field using dots of a slightly shifted hue. People with normal red-green vision can pick out the number; people with protanopia or deuteranopia (the most common color vision deficiencies) struggle to separate the number from the background because their eyes compress those hues together.

What do the results mean?

Scoring most plates correctly suggests normal red-green color vision. Missing several plates suggests a possible red-green deficiency — roughly 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women have one. Many people live with mild color deficiency without ever noticing; it usually affects distinguishing reds/greens/browns, not everyday vision quality.

Is an online test as good as an optometrist's?

No. Screen brightness, color settings, and room lighting all affect browser-based plates. Treat this as a quick screen: if you fail several plates — or you've always suspected you see colors differently — an optometrist can run calibrated Ishihara books and anomaloscope tests for a real answer.
