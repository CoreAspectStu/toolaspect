About the Drywall Calculator

This drywall calculator estimates how many sheets of drywall a project needs, along with joint compound, screws, and material cost, from the total wall and ceiling area. Enter your measured area (length × height for each surface, minus large openings), pick a sheet size, set a waste factor, and the tool rounds every material quantity up to whole purchasable units the way a supplier would.

Drywall estimating comes down to simple area math, but the details — sheet orientation, waste allowance, fastener schedules, and mud coverage — are where budgets slip. This page walks through the formulas the calculator uses, a worked example you can check by hand, a material reference table for common sheet sizes, and answers to the questions contractors and DIYers ask most.

The standard sheet thicknesses are 1/2 inch for walls and 5/8 inch for ceilings and fire-rated assemblies. A 4×8 sheet covers 32 square feet, a 4×10 covers 40, and a 4×12 covers 48 — the calculator uses these exact areas and always rounds sheet counts up, because you cannot buy a partial sheet.

How the Calculator Works

The calculator converts your entry into a purchase quantity in three steps. First it applies the waste factor: purchasing area = measured area × (1 + waste percentage). The default 10% covers cuts, breakage, and offcuts around openings — the allowance most trade references recommend for residential hanging.

Second it divides purchasing area by the coverage of one sheet (32, 40, or 48 ft²) and rounds up: sheets = ceil(area × (1 + waste) ÷ sheet area). Third it estimates consumables using published coverage rules of thumb: joint compound at roughly 0.05 lb per square foot of drywall (about 1 lb per 20 ft²), which is the taping-and-finishing average cited by major compound manufacturers, and screws at about one per square foot, which matches the common 12-inch on-center fastener schedule on 16-inch stud spacing.

Cost is simply sheets × price per sheet. If you want a fuller budget, add fasteners, tape, corner bead, and compound at your local prices — the table below gives typical quantities to anchor those estimates. A practical note on fasteners and tape: a 4×8 sheet on 16-inch centers takes about 28–32 screws at a 12-inch field schedule, and a 500-foot roll of paper tape covers roughly 16 sheets' worth of seams. Corner bead runs one length per inside or outside corner; measure your openings and wall junctions and add 10% for cut waste. These consumables are cheap but running out mid-job stalls the hang.

Finally, remember that hang and finish are separate labor phases: hanging runs roughly 1 labor hour per sheet while taping and finishing runs about 1.5 hours per sheet across three coats plus sanding. The calculator prices material only — multiply your sheet count by those field planning figures to sanity-check installer quotes before you sign a labor line item.

Worked Example: 12×12 Room

A 12 ft × 12 ft room with 8 ft ceilings has 4 walls × (12 × 8) = 384 ft². Applying 10% waste gives 422 ft² to purchase. Dividing by a 4×8 sheet (32 ft²): ceil(422 ÷ 32) = 14 sheets. At $15 per sheet that is $210 in board.

Joint compound: 384 × 0.05 ≈ 19 lb — one 20 lb box of setting compound or two buckets of premixed. Screws: about 384, so a 1 lb box (≈300 screws) plus a backup; buying 500 covers the job with spares. Verify your own room by measuring each wall and subtracting any opening larger than a sheet's worth of scrap allowance. Ceilings deserve a separate pass. Ceiling board is typically hung perpendicular to the joists with 5/8-inch (or sag-resistant half-inch) board to prevent droop, and working overhead both slows the job and increases breakage. Estimators often run walls at 10% waste but ceilings at 12–15%. If your 384 ft² includes a ceiling, splitting the entry into two calculations gives a sharper materials list.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many sheets of drywall do I need for a 12×12 room?

A 12×12 room with 8-foot ceilings has 384 ft² of wall. With a 10% waste factor you need ceil(422 ÷ 32) = 14 sheets of 4×8, 11 sheets of 4×10, or 9 sheets of 4×12 drywall.

How much joint compound per sheet of drywall?

The commonly used planning figure is about 0.05 lb of compound per square foot of drywall, or roughly 1.6 lb per 4×8 sheet for tape, fill, and skim coats. A 20 lb box therefore covers about 400 ft² of hung board.

How many drywall screws per sheet?

At 12-inch spacing on studs 16 inches on center, plan on about one screw per square foot — roughly 32 per 4×8 sheet, 40 per 4×10, and 48 per 4×12. Screws are sold by the pound; a 1 lb box holds roughly 300 screws.

Should I subtract doors and windows from the wall area?

For accuracy yes, but many pros leave small openings in the total as a natural waste allowance. If you subtract openings, keep the 10% waste factor; if you leave them in, 5% is often enough for simple layouts.

Is 4×12 drywall better than 4×8?

4×12 sheets cover 48 ft² versus 32 ft², so a job takes about a third fewer sheets and has fewer butt seams to finish. The trade-off is weight and handling: a 4×12 half-inch board is heavy, needs a second person or a lift, and demands a truck or delivery. Most DIYers stick with 4×8.
