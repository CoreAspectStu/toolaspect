🐕 Dog Crate Size for a French Bulldog

24 inches by the tape, 30 by the shoulders — and the airline catch

An adult French Bulldog needs a 24-inch crate by the standard rule: a typical Frenchie measures 19 inches nose to tail base, 19 + 4 = 23, and the 24-inch crate covers it — including height, with a 20-inch-tall interior clearing a 16-inch-tall dog's 19-inch standing need. The exception is width: Frenchies over about 28 pounds or with broad chests usually need a 30-inch crate, which is 3–4 inches wider inside. And air travel has a rule of its own — most airlines ban flat-faced breeds from cargo entirely, so flying a Frenchie means an in-cabin carrier, not a crate.

French Bulldogs are the breed where width beats length. The length rule says 24 inches for nearly every adult; the shoulder test is what moves a Frenchie to the 30. If your dog hesitates at the 24's door or has to turn sideways, that's your answer.

Frenchies reach adult length by 5 to 6 months, one of the fastest crate runways of any breed — the divider comes out early, but keep it snug until house training is solid, since small dogs are famously harder to house train than large ones.

The Airline Problem French Bulldog Owners Must Know

Here's where crate sizing and breed biology collide: French Bulldogs are brachycephalic — flat-faced — and their compressed airways make heat and respiratory stress in a cargo hold genuinely dangerous. After several widely reported deaths, most major airlines now refuse snub-nosed breeds in cargo altogether, Frenchies included. So the 300-series kennel that a weight chart suggests for a 25-pound dog is, for this breed, mostly useful for car travel and vet visits, not flights.

Flying with a Frenchie normally means an in-cabin soft carrier under the seat, generally up to about 18 inches long and 20 pounds of combined dog-plus-carrier weight, with airline-specific limits. That's a different product from a crate, bought to the airline's dimensions, not the breed's. Check the specific carrier's snub-nosed policy in writing before you book — policies change, and a gate agent's ruling is final.

How to Measure Your French Bulldog for a Crate

With most breeds you need two numbers. With a Frenchie you need three, because width is the one that usually decides it. Use a soft tape with the dog standing on a flat floor. A treat held just in front of the nose helps.

  • Length: tip of the nose to the base of the tail. Frenchies have a short screw or stub tail, so there's nothing extra to allow for.
  • Height: floor to the top of the head, with those bat ears standing up. The ears add a surprising inch or two.
  • Width: straight across the widest part of the chest and shoulders. Compare it with the inside width of the crate and its door, not the outside dimensions on the box.
  • Add the margins: length plus 4 inches for floor, height plus 3 inches for headroom. Then check that the shoulders fit through the door without the dog having to twist.

For the typical 19-inch-long, 16-inch-tall adult, the length rule gives 23 inches and the height rule gives 19, so the 24-inch crate passes both. If that same dog has a 13- or 14-inch chest and squeezes through the door, the 30-inch crate is the kinder choice.

Keeping a French Bulldog Cool in the Crate

Heat is the real crate risk for this breed. Brachycephalic dogs can't pant off heat as well as long-nosed dogs, so a crate that would be fine for a Beagle can get dangerous for a Frenchie on a warm day.

  • Put the crate in a shaded spot away from sunny windows, radiators and heating vents.
  • If you drape a blanket over a wire crate to make it den-like, leave at least one side and the front uncovered.
  • Keep water available for longer stretches. A bowl that clips to the crate door won't tip over.
  • Never leave a Frenchie crated in a parked car, even for a few minutes.

Loud, raspy breathing, heavy panting, lots of drooling or gums that look dark or bluish are warning signs. Get the dog somewhere cool and call a vet.

How Long Can a French Bulldog Stay in a Crate?

The numbers below come from Humane World for Animals' crate training guide (the organization formerly known as the Humane Society of the United States). Puppies get their age in months plus one hour at most, and puppies under 6 months shouldn't be crated for more than three or four hours at a time. Adult dogs shouldn't be crated for more than 4 to 5 hours during the day.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size crate does a French Bulldog need?

A 24-inch crate for most adults. A typical Frenchie measures about 19 inches from nose to tail base and stands 16 inches tall; 19 plus 4 is 23 inches, and the 24-inch crate covers it, with a typical 20-inch interior height clearing the 19 inches a 16-inch-tall dog needs. Broad-chested Frenchies over 28 pounds usually need the 30-inch crate for shoulder room.

Should I get a 24 or 30-inch crate for my French Bulldog?

Try the width test: if your Frenchie's shoulders feel tight going through the 24-inch crate's door or the dog is over about 28 pounds, take the 30. Length alone says 24 — Frenchies are short-bodied — but the breed is barrel-wide for its length, and the 30-inch crate is 3 to 4 inches wider inside. There's no harm in the 30 for an adult; skip it only for puppies in house training.

Can French Bulldogs fly in cargo?

Almost never. Most major airlines ban brachycephalic (flat-faced) breeds, including French Bulldogs, from cargo holds because their airways make heat stress dangerous. Flying a Frenchie usually means an in-cabin soft carrier that fits under the seat — typically about 18 inches long — which is a different product from a crate. Check the specific airline's snub-nosed policy before booking anything.

What size crate for a French Bulldog puppy?

Buy the adult 24-inch crate with a divider panel and give the puppy its length plus 4 inches: about 16 inches of floor at 8 weeks, 18 at 3 months, and the full crate by 5 to 6 months, when most Frenchies reach adult length. The breed matures fast, so the divider phase is shorter than with a retriever.

How long can a French Bulldog stay in a crate?

Humane World for Animals (formerly the Humane Society of the United States) says puppies shouldn't be crated longer than their age in months plus one hour, puppies under 6 months no more than three or four hours at a time, and adult dogs no more than 4 to 5 hours during the day. So an 8-week-old Frenchie gets about 3 hours, and an adult still needs a midday break.

Wire or plastic crate for a French Bulldog?

Wire for home. A wire crate has the best airflow, which matters for a flat-faced breed that overheats easily, and it usually comes with a divider panel for puppies. Plastic kennels are fine for short car trips and vet visits if you keep them out of the sun. For flying, most airlines only allow Frenchies in the cabin, so you'll need a soft-sided under-seat carrier instead.
