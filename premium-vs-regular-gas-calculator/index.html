Octane measures knock resistance, not energy or cleaning power. Higher octane lets an engine run more aggressive timing without knocking; it doesn't burn hotter, cleaner, or longer.

How the Premium vs Regular Calculator Works

There are really two questions hiding in "is premium worth it," and they have opposite answers depending on one word in your owner's manual: recommended or required. This calculator runs your exact numbers for both.

The formula

Annual cost = (annual miles ÷ mpg) × price per gallon, computed for each grade. The mpg on premium can be adjusted with the percentage field, which matters only if your engine actually responds to octane. The break-even check: premium pays for itself only when mpgGain% > (pricePremium ÷ priceRegular − 1) × 100.

How to use it

Set the manual setting first. If your car runs on regular or merely recommends premium, leave the mpg change at 0, because AAA's instrumented testing found no meaningful benefit in those cars. If premium is required and you're weighing regular, enter a small mpg drop as a negative number (say −2) to model the knock-sensor penalty honestly.

A worked example

A 28 mpg sedan running on regular, 12,000 miles a year, with regular at $3.20 and premium at $3.85. That's 428.6 gallons a year, so premium costs 428.6 × $3.85 = $1,650 versus 428.6 × $3.20 = $1,371. Premium adds $279 a year (with this $0.65 gap), and with zero mpg or power benefit, every dollar of it is waste.

Flip to a premium-required car: 25 mpg on premium at $4.35, or 24.5 mpg on regular at $3.75 (a 2% penalty). Premium: 480 gallons × $4.35 = $2,088. Regular: 489.8 gallons × $3.75 = $1,837. Regular saves $251 a year. And the break-even bar: at $4.35 vs $3.75, premium is 16% pricier, so premium would need a 16% mpg gain just to tie, far beyond any knock-sensor penalty.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is premium gas worth it?

Only if your owner's manual says premium is required. AAA's testing found no benefit to premium in vehicles designed for regular, including ones that merely recommend premium: no horsepower, fuel economy, or emissions improvement. At 12,000 miles a year, 28 mpg, and a $0.60 price gap, premium costs about $257 a year for nothing.

What happens if I put regular gas in a car that requires premium?

Modern engines have knock sensors and will retard timing to protect themselves, so occasional regular is generally fine with a small loss of power and mpg. Avoid it for towing, hauling, or extreme heat, and expect a 1-3% mpg drop. The calculator shows the trade: if regular is 60 cents cheaper, that mpg drop usually still saves money over the year.

Does premium gas give better mileage?

Not in an engine designed for regular octane. Octane rating measures knock resistance, not energy content. In a premium-recommended (not required) car, AAA measured no meaningful fuel economy difference. In premium-required engines, running regular can drop mpg slightly, which is why the break-even calculation matters.

How much more does premium gas cost per year?

Take the price gap, divide by your mpg, and multiply by annual miles. At 12,000 miles a year: a 22 mpg vehicle burns about 545 gallons, so a $0.60 gap costs $327 a year; a 28 mpg vehicle burns about 429 gallons and pays $257; a 34 mpg car pays $212.

Does premium gas clean your engine?

No. Detergent additives are required in all grades by federal law, and TOP TIER certification applies to regular too. If you want better cleaning, buy TOP TIER-branded regular, not premium.

When does premium actually pay for itself?

Only when the mpg gain beats the price ratio. If premium costs $4.35 and regular $3.75, premium is 16% more expensive, so premium would need to deliver more than a 16% mpg improvement to break even. No realistic octane effect comes close in a car that isn't premium-required.
