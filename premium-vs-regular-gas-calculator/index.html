How the Premium vs Regular Calculator Works

There are really two questions hiding in "is premium worth it," and they have opposite answers depending on one word in your owner's manual: recommended or required. This calculator runs your exact numbers for both.

The formula

Annual cost = (annual miles ÷ mpg) × price per gallon, computed for each grade. The mpg on premium can be adjusted with the percentage field, which matters only if your engine actually responds to octane. The break-even check: premium pays for itself only when mpgGain% > (pricePremium ÷ priceRegular − 1) × 100.

How to use it

Set the manual setting first. If your car runs on regular, leave the mpg change at 0, because AAA's instrumented testing found no meaningful benefit in those cars. If it recommends premium, try a small gain such as 2.7, the average AAA measured in those cars under demanding use. If premium is required and you're weighing regular, enter a small mpg drop as a negative number (say −2) to model the knock-sensor penalty honestly.

A worked example

A 28 mpg sedan running on regular, 12,000 miles a year, with regular at $3.20 and premium at $3.85. That's 428.6 gallons a year, so premium costs 428.6 × $3.85 = $1,650 versus 428.6 × $3.20 = $1,371. Premium adds $279 a year (with this $0.65 gap), and with zero mpg or power benefit, every dollar of it is waste.

Flip to a premium-required car: 25 mpg on premium at $4.35, or 24.5 mpg on regular at $3.75 (a 2% penalty). Premium: 480 gallons × $4.35 = $2,088. Regular: 489.8 gallons × $3.75 = $1,837. Regular saves $251 a year. And the break-even bar: at $4.35 vs $3.75, premium is 16% pricier, so premium would need a 16% mpg gain just to tie, far beyond any knock-sensor penalty.

What If Your Car Only Recommends Premium?

This is the one group where AAA did measure a benefit. Its 2016 tests covered cars built for regular, and they found no significant gain in horsepower, fuel economy or emissions. Its December 2017 tests covered six vehicles that recommend premium but don't require it. On average, fuel economy improved 2.7% and horsepower 1.4%, with the gains mostly showing up under hard use such as towing, hauling and rapid acceleration. Typical driving showed less. AAA concluded that the gains didn't justify the cost for most drivers, and it advised weighing your own driving habits. Here's that math on a 28 mpg car at 12,000 miles and the late-October prices:

How Do You Tell Which Group Your Car Is In?

Check the owner's manual and the fuel door label, and read the exact words. "Premium required" or "use only" means use premium, as AAA advises, because the engine's design depends on it and it may matter for warranty coverage. "Premium recommended" means the engine can adjust to regular. If it says 87, you've already got your answer. In a few high-altitude states you'll see 85 sold as regular, and if your manual asks for 87, it's safest to stick with that unless the manual says lower is acceptable.

Top Tier is a separate question. It's a detergent standard and not an octane grade, so you can buy Top Tier regular. AAA's tests found Top Tier gasoline left 19 times fewer engine deposits than non-Top Tier fuel, which makes it a better use of an upgrade than octane you don't need.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is premium gas worth it?

Only if your owner's manual says premium is required, or if you want a small power gain in a car that recommends it. AAA's testing found no horsepower, fuel economy, or emissions improvement in vehicles designed for regular. At 12,000 miles a year, 28 mpg, and a $0.60 price gap, premium costs about $257 a year for nothing.

What happens if I put regular gas in a car that requires premium?

Modern engines have knock sensors and will retard timing to protect themselves, so occasional regular is generally fine with a small loss of power and mpg. Avoid it for towing, hauling, or extreme heat, and expect a 1-3% mpg drop. The calculator shows the trade: if regular is 60 cents cheaper, that mpg drop usually still saves money over the year.

Does premium gas give better mileage?

Not in an engine designed for regular octane. Octane rating measures knock resistance, not energy content. In premium-recommended cars, AAA measured an average 2.7% gain, mostly under hard use, which doesn't cover the higher price. In premium-required engines, running regular can drop mpg slightly, which is why the break-even calculation matters.

How much more does premium gas cost per year?

Take the price gap, divide by your mpg, and multiply by annual miles. At 12,000 miles a year: a 22 mpg vehicle burns about 545 gallons, so a $0.60 gap costs $327 a year; a 28 mpg vehicle burns about 429 gallons and pays $257; a 34 mpg car pays $212.

Does premium gas clean your engine?

No. Detergent additives are required in all grades by federal law, and TOP TIER certification applies to regular too. If you want better cleaning, buy TOP TIER-branded regular, not premium.

When does premium actually pay for itself?

Only when the mpg gain beats the price ratio. If premium costs $4.35 and regular $3.75, premium is 16% more expensive, so premium would need to deliver more than a 16% mpg improvement to break even. No realistic octane effect comes close in a car that isn't premium-required.

How much more is premium gas than regular right now?

About a dollar a gallon nationally. EIA's October 26, 2026 averages were $4.269 for regular and $5.275 for premium, a gap of $1.006, or 23.6%. Local gaps vary, so use your own station's prices in the calculator.

Is premium worth it if my car says premium recommended?

Usually not for the money. AAA measured average gains of 2.7% in fuel economy and 1.4% in horsepower in such cars, mostly under hard use. At a 23.6% price gap that doesn't come close to paying for itself, though you may still want the power when towing or hauling.
