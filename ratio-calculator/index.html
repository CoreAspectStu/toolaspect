About the Ratio Calculator

A ratio compares two quantities by division, showing how many times one value contains another. This calculator simplifies ratios to their lowest terms, scales them up or down, and solves for a missing value in a proportion — the workhorse equation a/b = c/d where any three values determine the fourth.

Ratios appear everywhere: recipes (2 parts flour to 1 part water), mixing fuel (50:1 two-stroke oil), screen dimensions (16:9), map scales, and financial metrics like the debt-to-income ratio lenders use. Written with a colon (3:4), a slash (3/4), or the word 'to' (3 to 4), they all mean the same fraction.

Simplifying and scaling ratios

To simplify a ratio, divide every term by their greatest common divisor. The ratio 48:64 simplifies to 3:4 because both terms share the factor 16. Simplified form makes comparison easy: is 24:36 a better value than 30:42? Simplified, they are 2:3 and 5:7 — not equal, as cross-multiplication confirms (24 × 42 = 1008 and 36 × 30 = 1080 differ; simplified forms 2:3 ≈ 0.667 and 5:7 ≈ 0.714 make the difference visible at a glance).

Scaling a ratio preserves it: 3:4, 6:8, 30:40, and 300:400 are all the same relationship. This is why recipes scale cleanly — a 2:1 flour-to-water ratio works for one loaf or ten — and why map scales read like 1:25,000, meaning one unit on the map equals 25,000 of the same units on the ground.

Proportions and the golden ratio

A proportion states that two ratios are equal, and it is solved by cross-multiplication: if a/b = c/d then a·d = b·c. If 3 pencils cost $1.50, then 7 pencils cost x, where 3/1.50 = 7/x gives x = $3.50. Every missing-value ratio problem reduces to this one step.

Ratios versus percentages and rates

A ratio compares two quantities of any kind; a percentage is a ratio normalized to 100; a rate is a ratio with different units, like miles per hour or price per ounce. Unit-price shopping is rate comparison: a 12 oz box at $3.00 is $0.25/oz, while 16 oz at $3.84 is $0.24/oz — the bigger box wins on rate even though it costs more in total.

One caution: ratios hide size. A 10:1 win-loss record sounds dominant until you learn it is 10 wins and 1 loss versus 400 wins and 40 losses — the second record's 10:1 came against far more trials. Always ask what the absolute quantities are before comparing ratios alone, since small denominators make ratios volatile.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I simplify a ratio?

Divide both terms by their greatest common divisor. For 48:64, the GCD is 16, so the simplified ratio is 3:4. A quick method: factor both numbers, cancel the shared primes, and multiply what remains — exactly like simplifying a fraction.

How do I solve for a missing value in a proportion?

Cross-multiply. If a/b = c/d, then a·d = b·c. For example, if 5 notebooks cost $7.50, then 8 notebooks cost x where 5/7.50 = 8/x; cross-multiplying gives 5x = 60, so x = $12.00.

Are the ratios 3:4 and 30:40 the same?

Yes. Multiplying both terms of a ratio by the same non-zero number does not change the relationship — 3:4, 30:40, and 300:400 all equal 0.75 when expressed as a fraction. This invariance under scaling is the defining property of ratios.

What is the difference between a ratio and a proportion?

A ratio compares two quantities; a proportion is an equation stating that two ratios are equal. '3:4' is a ratio. '3:4 = 30:40' is a proportion. Proportions are what you solve when a ratio problem contains an unknown.

How do I convert a ratio to a percentage?

Divide the first term by the sum of the terms (for a share of a whole) or by the second term (for a relative comparison), then multiply by 100. A 3:4 split of a whole is 3/7 ≈ 42.9% and 4/7 ≈ 57.1%. A 3:4 advantage is 3/4 = 75%.

Why do lenders care about my debt-to-income ratio?

The DTI ratio measures monthly debt payments against gross monthly income. Under US lending guidelines used for qualified mortgages, a DTI at or below 43% has traditionally been an important threshold, because a higher ratio signals that income is heavily committed to debt service. It is a direct application of ratio comparison to credit risk.
