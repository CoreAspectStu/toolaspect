Quick answer: First-year costs run $1,580 for a rabbit, $1,240 for a guinea pig pair, $480 for a hamster, and $1,430 for a bearded dragon (2025-26 national prices). After setup, expect $30-$75 a month in food and bedding plus $50-$150 exotic vet visits. Lifetimes over a full lifespan: hamster ≈ $1,180, guinea pair ≈ $5,590, rabbit ≈ $9,950, bearded dragon ≈ $9,350. The pet-store "starter kit" price is the beginning, not the budget.

How the Small Pet Cost Calculator Works

The pet store sells you the animal for $20 and the real bill starts arriving after: the right-size enclosure, exotic vet care, and a decade of hay or bugs. This calculator prices the full ownership arc — first year, steady state, and lifetime — so "cheap pet" claims survive contact with arithmetic.

The math

First year = acquisition + habitat setup + one-time medical (spay/neuter, clearances) + initial vet visit + 12 months of food and bedding + annual vet + annual extras. After year one: monthly × 12 + annual vet + extras. Lifetime = first year + (annual × (lifespan − 1)). Swap any number for your own quotes; the species dropdown just loads honest starting points.

How to use it

Pick your species, then adjust the setup line to the enclosure you're actually buying — the minimums (a 40-gallon tank for a hamster, 120 gallons for an adult bearded dragon, an exercise pen rather than a cage for a rabbit) cost more than pet-store kits, and the kits get replaced within a year anyway. The one-time medical field matters most for rabbits, where spay/neuter is standard care, not optional.

A worked example

A bonded pair of guinea pigs: $70 adoption ($35 each), $180 for a proper C&C cage setup, $120 for an initial pair vet check, then $60 a month in hay, pellets, greens, and bedding plus $150 of annual vet care. First year: $1,240. Steady state: $870 a year. Over a typical 6-year life: 1,240 + 870 × 5 = $5,590, or about $78 a month all-in. The pet store's "$80 starter kit" covered about 6% of the actual commitment.

Now the rabbit on the same math: $50 adoption, $250 setup, $350 spay, $65 a month, $150 vet a year. First year $1,580, then $930 a year, and with rabbits living 10 years, $9,950 lifetime — a hamster's whole life costs less than the rabbit's first year, while the rabbit ultimately prices like a cat.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a rabbit cost?

About $1,580 the first year and $930 a year after that, with a typical 8-12 year lifespan running close to $10,000 all-in. Adoption runs $25-$75, an exercise-pen setup $150-$300, and spay or neuter $200-$500 — which is non-negotiable, since it prevents cancer and spraying. Hay, pellets, and veggies run $50-$80 a month.

How much do guinea pigs cost?

Plan on about $1,240 the first year for a bonded pair — they're social animals that shouldn't live alone, so budget two of everything. Two adoptions at $35 each, a C&C cage setup around $180, and $60 a month in hay, pellets, veggies, and bedding adds up to roughly $870 a year after that, about $5,590 over a typical 5-7 year life.

What's the cheapest small pet?

A hamster, at roughly $480 the first year and $350 a year after. They live alone (housing two means fighting, not company), the setup is a $60-$120 large tank or cage, and food plus bedding runs about $25 a month. Bearded dragons are the opposite: $1,430 the first year and $720 a year after, mostly insects and electricity.

Is a bearded dragon expensive to keep?

More than people expect: about $1,430 the first year with the 120-gallon adult enclosure, UVB and heat fixtures, and thermostat, then $720 a year after — $45 a month of insects and greens plus vet care and twice-yearly UVB bulb replacement. Over a 10-15 year lifespan that approaches a rabbit's lifetime cost.

Do small pets need vet care?

Yes, and it's priced as exotic care, which runs $50-$150 a visit — often more than a cat exam. Rabbits and guinea pigs need exotics-savvy vets (their digestive systems fail fast when anything goes wrong), and an emergency GI-stasis case in a rabbit can hit four figures. Budget $150 a year routine, and treat pet insurance or an emergency fund as part of the real cost.

What hidden costs surprise new small-pet owners?

Three: the right enclosure is two to three times bigger than the pet-store kit (a 40-gallon tank for a hamster, 120 gallons for an adult beardie, an exercise pen for a rabbit); chewables are a permanent budget line (rabbits and guinea pigs destroy hides, cords, and baseboards); and pet-store starter kits are usually undersized, so most buyers replace them within a year.
