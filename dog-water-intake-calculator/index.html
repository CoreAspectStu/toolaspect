How much water should a dog drink? The vet-standard daily range

A dog needs about 50–60 mL per kg of body weight per day (Merck Veterinary Manual maintenance range) — roughly 0.8–1 oz per pound. That's 1,134–1,361 mL (38–46 oz) for a 50-lb dog, 454–544 mL (15–18 oz) for a 20-lb dog, and about a cup total for a 10-lb cat counting food moisture. Puppies sit near 1 oz per pound. Heat and hard exercise push needs up ~50%; wet food covers part of the requirement (75% moisture). Drinking more than ~100 mL/kg (1.5 oz/lb) for days on end is a polydipsia flag — call your vet.

How the Dog Water Intake Calculator Works

Daily water needs scale with metabolic body weight, and the maintenance figure vets use is 50–60 mL per kilogram per day. This calculator starts there, applies the adjustments that matter in real life, and then separates total intake from what actually comes out of the bowl — because food moisture does part of the work.

The formula

Total = weight in kg × (50–60 mL) × activity × weather × growth factor (1.2 for puppies and kittens). Food credit = wet-food cups × 227 g × 75% moisture ≈ 170 mL per cup. Drinking water = total − food credit. The polydipsia threshold is 100 mL/kg — above it, sustained, is a reason to see a vet.

How to use it

Enter your pet's weight (pounds or kilograms), pick the life stage, and set activity and weather honestly — the multiplier for a hot July day with a fetch session is not the same as a mild Sunday. If your pet eats wet food, include it: two cups a day covers a meaningful share of the requirement, and the drinking-water number drops accordingly. That's expected, not under-drinking.

A worked example

A 50-lb (22.7-kg) adult dog at baseline: 22.7 × 50 = 1,134 mL to 22.7 × 60 = 1,361 mL per day — that's 38.3 to 46.0 oz, about 4.8 to 5.8 cups. Make her an active dog (×1.25) and the range rises to 1,417–1,701 mL (47.9–57.5 oz). Feed two cups of wet food and about 340 mL of that arrives in the food, leaving 1,077–1,360 mL to drink. For scale: the polydipsia line for this dog is 2,268 mL (76.7 oz) — drinking that much for several straight days is the pattern that warrants a phone call, not one sunny afternoon at the dog park.

Cats use the same per-kg math with one twist: a 10-lb cat's 227–272 mL is TOTAL intake, and a wet-food cat may drink almost nothing from the bowl and still be perfectly hydrated. A kibble-only cat has to get all of it from drinking, which is why vets push wet food for feline urinary health.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much water should a dog drink per day?

The veterinary maintenance range is about 50-60 mL per kg of body weight per day (Merck Veterinary Manual) — roughly 0.8 to 1 oz per pound. A 50-lb dog needs 1,134-1,361 mL (38-46 oz, or about 5-6 cups); a 20-lb dog, 454-544 mL (15-18 oz). Working in the heat can push needs 50% higher.

How much water should a puppy drink?

Growing puppies drink at or above the top of the adult range per pound — a common rule of thumb is about 1 oz per pound of body weight daily, roughly 20% above the adult baseline. A 20-lb puppy needs around 544-653 mL (18-22 oz), served fresh and often, since young puppies should always have water available rather than one big bowl a day.

Does wet food change how much a dog drinks?

A lot. Wet food is about 75% water, so a cup (227 g) delivers roughly 170 mL of moisture, while dry kibble is only around 10%. A dog eating two cups of wet food a day can meet a third or more of its water needs from food alone and will drink proportionally less from the bowl — that's normal, not a warning sign.

How much should a cat drink per day?

Cats need about the same 50-60 mL/kg/day as dogs, but it's total intake from all sources: a 10-lb cat needs roughly 227-272 mL (about 1 cup) counting food moisture. Cats evolved as desert hunters and have a weak thirst drive, which is why vets often prefer wet food — a dry-food cat must do almost all its drinking from the bowl and runs a higher risk of chronic mild dehydration.

How much water is too much for a dog?

Consistently drinking more than about 100 mL per kg (1.5 oz per lb) per day counts as polydipsia and warrants a vet visit — for a 50-lb dog that's over 2,268 mL (77 oz) day after day. Excess thirst and urination are early signs of diabetes, kidney disease, Cushing's, or a uterine infection in unspayed females. One hot day at the lake doesn't count; a week of empty bowls does.

Why is my dog drinking so much water all of a sudden?

Short-term spikes are usually explainable: heat, exercise, salty treats, or new dry food. If heavy drinking lasts more than two or three days, pair it with the other signs — accidents indoors, weight loss, bigger appetite or none — and call your vet. Bring the numbers: how much the bowl drops per day is genuinely useful diagnostic information.
