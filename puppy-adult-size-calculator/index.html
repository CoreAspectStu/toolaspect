How the Puppy Size Calculator Works

Puppy growth follows an S-curve: fast and fairly straight until roughly half of adult weight, then a long decelerating tail. That shape is why "divide weight by age and multiply by 52" works only in a narrow window, and why the growth-curve percentage method is the better default.

The formula

Predicted adult weight = current weight ÷ (share of adult weight at this age). The share comes from the table above, interpolated between rows for your puppy's exact age (a 14-week medium pup sits between the 12- and 16-week rows). The result is a low-to-high band because the underlying data is a range, not a line.

How to use it

Enter your puppy's current weight in pounds and their age in weeks — both from the same day, ideally from a vet-visit scale. Pick the size class they're headed for. If you genuinely don't know (mixed breed, mystery dad), choose "Not sure" and the calculator runs all four classes and shows you the full spread. Under 8 weeks the bands get wide fast, so predictions before two months are ballpark only.

A worked example

Take a 16-week-old medium-breed puppy (a Beagle cross, say) weighing 13 lb. At 16 weeks a medium pup is 52–62% of adult weight, so the math is 13 ÷ 0.62 = 21.0 lb on the fast-developed end and 13 ÷ 0.52 = 25.0 lb on the slow end. Prediction: 21–25 lb, with a midpoint around 23 lb.

The cross-checks tell the same story at a glance. Doubling the 4-month weight gives 13 × 2 = 26 lb, just above the band's top. The 52-week rule spits out 13 ÷ 16 × 52 = 42 lb — that's the ceiling effect in action: past about 12 weeks, medium puppies grow slower than that straight line assumes, which is exactly why this calculator anchors on the growth-curve table instead.

Now a mixed-breed case: 18 lb at 12 weeks, unknown mix. The small-class curve is ruled out on sight — a sub-25-lb adult would be further along than 18 lb at 12 weeks — and the giant curve's own math says 67–82 lb, which contradicts a 90+ lb adult, so it drops out too. That leaves the medium and large curves, spanning 39–60 lb, and the calculator shows you exactly which classes survived so you can lean on paw size, skin looseness, and whatever the rescue or a DNA test says about the parents.

Frequently Asked Questions

How big will my puppy get?

Divide your puppy's current weight by the share of adult body weight their age and size class should be at. A 16-week-old medium-breed puppy is about 52-62% of adult weight, so a 13-lb pup at that age lands near a 21-25 lb adult. Toy and small breeds run ahead of that curve, giant breeds behind it.

When do dogs stop growing?

Toy and small breeds reach adult weight around 9-11 months, medium breeds around a year, large breeds at 15-18 months, and giant breeds anywhere from 18 to 24 months. Large-breed dogs keep filling out (chest, head, muscle) for a couple of months after the scale stops climbing.

Is the puppy weight times 52 rule accurate?

The rule that says divide weight by age in weeks and multiply by 52 tracks giant-breed puppies reasonably well between about 10 and 20 weeks. For smaller classes it overprojects once growth starts to slow, so treat it as a loose ceiling rather than a prediction. The growth-percentage method is more reliable for most dogs.

How do I predict a mixed-breed puppy's adult size?

Run the growth-percentage math across all four size classes, then throw out the classes that contradict their own result. An 18-lb 12-week-old of unknown mix lands on 39-60 lb: too heavy for the small curve, and the giant curve predicts a sub-90-lb adult, which rules itself out. Paw size, loose skin, and a DNA-test breed guess narrow what's left.

Do puppies double their weight at 4 months?

Close. Most puppies are roughly half their adult weight at 16 weeks, so doubling the 4-month weight is a decent quick check for medium breeds. It runs hot for small breeds and cold for giants, which are closer to a third of adult weight at that age.

When should I switch my puppy to adult food?

When growth is nearly done: around 9-12 months for small and medium breeds, and 12-18 months for large and giant breeds, following your vet's read on body condition. Large-breed puppies specifically need controlled calcium and calorie levels while growing, so don't rush the switch.
