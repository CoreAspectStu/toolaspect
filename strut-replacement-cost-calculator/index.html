How the Strut Cost Calculator Works

Struts are structural: the spring and the damper live in one unit that carries the car's weight. That's why strut labor runs two to three times a plain shock job, and why an alignment belongs in the budget once the corner comes apart.

The formula

Job total = parts + (labor hours × shop rate) + alignment. Parts come from the tier table for your axle and assembly type; labor hours per pair are 2.5–3.5 bare versus 1.6–2.2 for quick struts, since pre-assembled units skip the spring compression. New mounts add $70–$160 in parts and 0.3 hours per axle, and the alignment line is $80–$200.

How to use it

Pick the axle, your parts tier, and whether you're quoting bare struts or complete assemblies. Set the labor rate to your shop's number, independents typically run $90–$150/hr and dealers $120–$190. Leave both boxes checked for a realistic out-the-door estimate; uncheck alignment on a solid-axle shock job to see why trucks are cheaper to keep riding well.

A worked example

A front strut pair with standard bare struts at $130/hr: parts run $220–$420, plus $70–$160 for mounts. Labor at 2.5–3.5 hours plus 0.3 for the mounts bills $364–$494. Add the $80–$200 alignment and the job totals $734–$1,274, midpoint about $1,004. That's the number to test a real quote against.

Now all four corners on quick struts, same tier and rate. Parts run $680–$1,210 plus $140–$320 for mounts (two axles), and labor drops to 3.6–4.7 hours total, billing $468–$611. With alignment the job lands at $1,368–$2,341. Notice what the quick struts did: parts went up, hours went down, and the total barely moved, you're mostly buying the shop out of the riskiest step of the job.

Reading the estimate you get

  • One corner quoted, not a pair: ask why. Matched pairs are the norm for wear; single-corner jobs make sense mostly for collision damage.
  • No alignment line: the corner geometry just changed. The alignment cost calculator prices $80–$200 separately, so know whether it's in the quote or coming after.
  • Bare strut with reused mount: mounts are wear items and cheap while the corner is apart. Reusing a 100,000-mile mount to save $70 usually buys a comeback clunk.
  • Unhappy tires: cupped or scalloped wear means the dampers stopped controlling the springs a while ago, expect the tire conversation too.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to replace struts?

A pair of struts runs $545-$875 installed with standard parts at a $130/hr shop rate, before mounts and alignment. Add new strut mounts and the alignment that the job requires and the same front-axle job lands at $734-$1,274. Published averages agree: J.D. Power puts front struts at $300-$700 per strut installed, and RepairPal's shock or strut replacement average of $1,119-$1,328 corresponds to the premium-parts end with add-ons.

Should struts be replaced in pairs?

Yes, almost always. Struts wear together, and a new stiff strut opposite a worn soft one makes the car corner unevenly and can upset electronic stability control. The parts discount on buying a pair is real too. The exception is collision damage on a nearly new car, where replacing one strut of a young matched pair is defensible.

Are quick struts (complete assemblies) worth it?

Usually yes. A complete pre-assembled strut with the spring, mount and seat already together costs more per part but cuts labor from 2.5-3.5 hours to 1.6-2.2 hours per pair, and it removes the spring-compression step, which is the riskiest part of the job. At $130/hr the totals come out close: $734-$1,274 bare versus $757-$1,335 as assemblies on a standard front pair with mounts and alignment.

Do you need an alignment after replacing struts?

Yes for strut-suspension corners. Taking the strut apart changes camber and toe, so an alignment ($80-$200) belongs in the budget. Rear shock absorbers on trucks and SUVs with solid axles often don't affect alignment, which is why the calculator treats shock pairs differently from strut pairs.

How long do struts last?

Roughly 50,000-100,000 miles. Signs they're done: the car keeps bouncing two or three times after a bump, cupped tire wear, nose dive under braking, clunking over potholes, or visible oil leaking down the strut body. Age counts too, a 12-year-old car with 40,000 miles can have tired struts since the seals age regardless of miles.
