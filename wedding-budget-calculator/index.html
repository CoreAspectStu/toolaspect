How the Wedding Budget Calculator Works

A wedding budget fails in two directions: a total with no plan, which quietly overspends line by line, or a spreadsheet so detailed nobody maintains it. This calculator takes the middle path — one total, one guest count, and twelve allocations built from what real couples actually spend.

Where the percentages come from

The Knot's 2026 Real Weddings Study tracks what couples paid across vendor categories, and the shares are remarkably stable year to year: the reception bundle (venue plus catering) runs just over half the budget, photo and video around 10%, and everything else fights for the remainder. Those published shares are the defaults here, applied to whatever total you enter.

How to use it

Enter your total budget and guest count. The per-guest number is your reality check against the $292 national average — under $200 and something has to give (usually the list or the venue). Pick your region to see your budget as a share of the typical wedding there: the same $30,000 is comfortable in the Midwest and roughly 60% of a Mid-Atlantic average. Then treat the allocation table as a negotiation grid, not a rulebook.

A worked example

A $30,000 budget with 100 guests allocates as follows: venue and rentals $8,700 (29%), catering cake and drinks $7,200 (24%), photo and video $3,000 (10%), florals and decor $2,700 (9%), music $1,800 (6%), attire and beauty $1,800 (6%), rings $1,500 (5%), planner $1,200 (4%), guest entertainment $900 (3%), transportation $600 (2%), stationery $300 (1%), and officiant $300 (1%). The twelve lines sum exactly to $30,000, and the reception alone claims $15,900.

Per guest, that's $300 — 3% over the national average, or about $800 total. The fastest rebalance: ten fewer guests at the $80-per-plate catering average frees $800 of food plus the associated bar, rentals, and invitations, without touching a single vendor's price.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the average wedding budget in the US?

The Knot's 2026 Real Weddings Study puts the average at $34,200, or $292 per guest across an average of 117 guests. That average hides a huge spread: $17,100 for weddings of 50 guests or fewer, $27,200 for 51 to 100, and $43,300 for over 100. Region matters too, from about $29,000 in the Midwest to $48,400 in the Mid-Atlantic.

How should I split my wedding budget?

Start from the percentages real couples land on: venue and rentals about 29%, catering cake and drinks 24%, photo and video 10%, florals and decor 9%, music 6%, attire and beauty 6%, rings 5%, planner 4%, guest entertainment 3%, transportation 2%, stationery 1%, and officiant 1%. The reception bundle (venue plus catering) is just over half the total, so locking those two numbers first anchors everything else.

How much should I budget per wedding guest?

The national average works out to $292 per guest. Catering alone averages $80 per person, and that's before bar, cake, rentals, and invitations, which is why the per-guest total runs three to four times the plate price. If your budget divided by your guest count falls well under $200, trimming the list or the venue tier usually closes the gap faster than squeezing every vendor.

What's the biggest wedding expense?

The reception. Venue and rentals take roughly 29% of the average budget and catering, cake, and drinks another 24%, so the party as a whole claims just over half of everything couples spend. The single largest vendor line is the venue itself, averaging $12,900 in the 2026 study.

What costs are not included in the average wedding budget?

The $34,200 average covers the wedding day itself. The engagement ring (average $4,600), rehearsal dinner ($2,800), and honeymoon ($5,500) are tracked separately, as are wedding-party gifts and attire. If you're planning the full engagement-to-honeymoon arc, those lines can push total spending past $45,000.

Can I plan a wedding on a $15,000 budget?

Yes, but the percentages have to bend. A $15,000 budget at the $292 national average covers about 50 guests, or 65 guests at a leaner $230. Couples who pull it off usually pick an off-peak date, a non-traditional venue (restaurants, parks, backyards), a daytime brunch reception, and a single photographer instead of a photo-plus-video team.
