How the Flat Tow Setup Cost Calculator Works

It's a straight stack: base plate + tow bar + braking + lighting + extras + labor. Pick a tier for each component (the values in each dropdown sit at the middle of that component's retail band), adjust the extras line, and choose DIY or shop labor. The parts and labor split updates with the total, and the dolly comparison tells you whether your build undercuts a $2,800 typical dolly buy.

How to use it

Price the base plate for your exact vehicle first — that's the line that varies most, because some cars need a unique bracket and some have none available at all. Match the tow bar brand to the base plate brand so the couplers mate. If your state and the states you cross require supplemental braking (most do above a weight threshold nearly every car exceeds), keep that line in the stack; skipping it saves $600-$1,500 and costs you compliance and stopping distance.

A worked example

The typical build: $550 bolt-on base plate, $1,000 self-aligning RV-mounted tow bar, $1,200 proportional portable braking (the Blue Ox Patriot class of unit), $180 diode lighting kit, $120 in safety cables and a charge-line kit, and $450 of shop labor. Total: $3,500, of which $3,050 is parts. Against a $2,800 dolly buy you're $700 over on day one, but every hookup after that takes five minutes instead of fifteen, with no trailer to store or register — and the portable braking unit moves to your next car, while a dolly strap set does not travel that way.

Trim the same stack to entry parts and your own wrenches — $450 welded base plate, $600 car-mounted bar, $750 portable inertial braking, $80 magnetic lights, $90 of cables — and the total lands at $1,970. That's the real floor for a legal four-down setup with braking.

Before you buy anything

Confirm the car is towable four-down in the owner's manual's recreational towing section, and confirm your motorhome's hitch rating covers the car's weight with the tow bar and base plate added. The towing capacity calculator handles the motorhome side of that check, and the towing cost calculator prices the roadside-tow side of the sport. If the car isn't flat-towable, the dolly and trailer columns above become your actual comparison.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a complete flat tow setup cost?

Between $1,970 and $4,800 for most passenger cars. A budget DIY build (entry base plate, basic tow bar, portable inertial braking, magnetic lights) runs about $1,970 in parts. The typical build RV owners land on — standard base plate, self-aligning tow bar, proportional portable braking, diode lighting, shop install — totals about $3,500. Premium hardware with a dealer install reaches $4,800 and beyond.

What does a flat tow kit include?

Four pieces: a vehicle-specific base plate bolted to the car's frame ($400-$800), a tow bar connecting the car to the motorhome's hitch ($400-$1,700), a supplemental braking system that actuates the towed car's brakes ($600-$1,700), and tow lighting ($40-$250) plus safety cables and wiring. Buy the base plate and tow bar from the same brand family so the couplers match, or budget about $120 for an adapter.

Is a supplemental braking system required by law?

It depends on the state. Most states require the towed vehicle to have working brakes above a weight threshold, commonly somewhere between 1,500 and 3,000 pounds, which nearly every towed car exceeds. A few states have no auxiliary brake requirement, but RV safety advocates and many insurers expect one anyway. Check your own state's rule and every state you'll drive through, not just your home state.

Is flat towing cheaper than a tow dolly or trailer?

Up front, sometimes not: a flat-tow setup runs $1,970-$4,800 while a tow dolly costs $1,500-$3,500 and an open car trailer $2,000-$5,000. Flat towing wins on per-trip effort: hooking up takes minutes, there's no dolly or trailer to store and maneuver at the campground, and nothing extra to register. Renting a dolly at $50-$75 a day beats buying if you tow fewer than about three weeks total.

Can any car be flat towed?

No. Flat towing with all four wheels down requires a drivetrain that tolerates it, and the only authority is the owner's manual's recreational towing section. Many AWD vehicles and cars with twin-clutch automatic transmissions can't be flat towed at all, while some need a transmission lube pump or a fuse pulled. Check before buying hardware — the base plate is the cheapest line, but it's still $400-$800 you don't want to waste on a car that can't be towed.

Does flat towing put miles on the car?

Usually not. Most modern cars log miles electronically with the ignition off, and flat-tow setups tow with the key in accessory, so the odometer typically stands still. A few older vehicles with mechanical odometers do roll up miles. It also wears the tires — they're rolling the whole time — which is why tire condition is part of seasonal flat-tow maintenance.
