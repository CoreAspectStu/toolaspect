How the Pet Rent Calculator Works

Pet charges come in three flavors that are easy to confuse at a leasing office: monthly rent that never comes back, a one-time fee that never comes back, and a one-time deposit that does (if your pet doesn't chew the baseboards). Adding them up over a full lease — not just hearing them one at a time — is what tells you what an apartment really costs with a pet.

The formula

Upfront = (fee + deposit) × pets. Monthly = rent × pets. Total = upfront + monthly × months. Net = total − deposit × pets (the part you get back). Effective monthly cost = net ÷ months. That last number is the honest one: it converts one-time fees into the monthly drain they really are.

How to use it

Pull the three numbers from the lease or the listing's pet policy — they're usually quoted per pet, and most properties charge per animal. Enter your lease length (12 months is typical; use 24 if you expect to renew, since one-time fees don't repeat but monthly rent does). Leave the deposit at zero if your building only charges a fee.

A worked example

One dog, $35 a month pet rent, a $300 fee, and a $400 deposit on a 12-month lease. Upfront at signing: $700. Monthly: $35. Total across the year: $1,120. If the deposit comes back, the net cost is $720, which works out to $60 a month — the pet quietly costs as much as a nice pet insurance premium or a decent phone bill.

Two cats stack differently: $25/month each plus a $250 fee each with no deposit. Upfront $500, monthly $50, total $1,100, nothing refundable. Same order of magnitude, and every renewal year adds another $600. Over a three-year stay, that first apartment's $1,960 in gross pet charges ($1,560 net if the deposit refunds) keeps compounding quietly — worth knowing before you fall in love with the unit.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the average pet rent per month?

About $35.65 a month nationally in 2025, with a typical range of $10 to $60. Large-city buildings average slightly more than small-city ones ($37 vs $35), and some premium buildings charge $50 to $100 for large dogs. Pet rent is per pet at most properties, so two pets usually double it.

What's the difference between pet rent, a pet fee, and a pet deposit?

Pet rent is monthly and never comes back. A pet fee is one-time and non-refundable. A pet deposit is one-time and refundable if your pet doesn't damage the unit, subject to the same itemized-deduction rules as your security deposit. Over a two-year lease, monthly pet rent almost always costs more than either one-time charge.

How much are pet fees for apartments?

The average one-time pet fee runs about $315 nationally, with a typical range of $150 to $500 per pet. Refundable pet deposits cluster around $250, usually $200 to $500. Larger dogs and aggressive breeds often trigger the top of the range, and some buildings cap total charges per apartment rather than per pet.

Is pet rent legal in every state?

Monthly pet rent is legal in nearly every state and is not capped in most of them, including California. What states regulate is deposits: in California, for example, pet deposits now count inside the one-month security deposit cap that took effect in 2024, which is why many landlords there shifted to pet rent instead.

Do I have to pay pet rent for an emotional support animal?

No. Under the federal Fair Housing Act, assistance animals — including emotional support animals with valid documentation — are not pets. Landlords cannot charge pet rent, pet fees, or pet deposits for them, though they can still bill you for actual damage the animal causes and can require reliable documentation of the disability-related need.

Can a landlord charge pet rent for two pets?

Usually yes — pet rent, fees, and deposits are quoted per pet at most properties, so two pets typically means double the monthly charge and double the one-time charges. A few buildings charge a reduced rate for the second animal or cap total pet charges, and some weight or breed limits restrict which pets count at all. It's a fair question to ask before applying.
