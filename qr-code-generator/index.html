How QR Codes Work

QR (Quick Response) codes are two-dimensional barcodes invented in 1994 by Masahiro Hara at Denso Wave, a Toyota subsidiary. Originally designed for tracking automotive parts, they store data in a grid of black and white squares called modules. Each code contains position detection patterns (the three large squares in corners), alignment patterns, timing patterns, and data modules encoded using Reed-Solomon error correction.

Modern QR codes can encode up to 7,089 numeric characters, 4,296 alphanumeric characters, or 2,953 bytes of binary data. They use error correction levels (L, M, Q, H) that allow the code to remain scannable even when partially damaged or obscured — up to 30% of the code can be unreadable and still function at the highest correction level.

Every smartphone camera can scan QR codes natively. On iPhone, the Camera app detects QR codes automatically. On Android, Google Lens or the default camera app handles scanning. No third-party app is required on modern devices.

QR Code Types Explained

URL QR Codes

The most common type — encodes a web address that opens in the phone's browser when scanned. Perfect for linking to websites, landing pages, app downloads, or specific content. Always include the full URL with https:// for best compatibility.

WiFi QR Codes

Encodes network credentials in a standard format (WIFI:T:WPA;S:NetworkName;P:password;;) that phones can read to instantly connect to WiFi. Eliminates the need to type complex passwords — guests simply scan and connect. Works with WPA, WPA2, WEP, and open networks.

vCard QR Codes

Encodes contact information in the vCard 3.0 format (BEGIN:VCARD). When scanned, the phone prompts to save the contact directly to the address book. Ideal for business cards, networking events, and conference materials.

Plain Text QR Codes

Encodes any arbitrary text — messages, notes, serial numbers, or instructions. The simplest format, useful when you need to share text that doesn't fit a specific category.

Common Uses for QR Codes

  • Restaurant menus: Replace physical menus with scannable links to digital versions, reducing costs and enabling real-time updates.
  • Business cards: Add a vCard QR code so contacts can save your details with one scan instead of manual entry.
  • WiFi sharing: Let guests connect to your network instantly without typing a long WPA key.
  • Product packaging: Link to instruction manuals, warranty registration, or product authenticity verification.
  • Event tickets: Encode ticket IDs for quick check-in and fraud prevention at venues.
  • Print advertising: Bridge physical and digital by adding QR codes to flyers, posters, and billboards.
  • Payment links: Direct customers to payment pages or digital wallets for frictionless transactions.
  • App downloads: Create a single QR code that routes to the correct app store based on the device.

QR Code Best Practices

To ensure your QR codes work reliably, follow these guidelines:

  • Test before printing: Always scan your QR code with multiple devices before deploying it in print. A code that works on your phone may fail on older devices.
  • Use sufficient size: The minimum recommended size is 2×2 cm (0.8×0.8 inches) for scanning at close range. For posters or billboards, scale proportionally to the expected scanning distance.
  • Maintain contrast: Use dark colors on light backgrounds. Black on white is the safest choice. Avoid inverted colors (light on dark) as some scanners struggle with them.
  • Keep it clean: Don't overlay logos, text, or decorative elements on the code itself. The quiet zone (white margin around the code) should be at least 4 modules wide.
  • Use error correction: Our generator uses level M (15% recovery). For outdoor or high-wear applications, consider regenerating with a shorter URL to increase error correction density.
  • Shorten URLs: Shorter URLs produce simpler, faster-to-scan codes. Use a URL shortener before generating the QR code for best results.

Privacy and Security

This QR code generator runs entirely in your browser using JavaScript. Your data — whether URLs, WiFi passwords, or contact details — never leaves your device or passes through any server. There is no server-side processing, no logging, and no tracking of what you encode. This makes it safe for sensitive applications like encoding WiFi credentials or personal contact information.

When scanning QR codes in the wild, always check the URL before visiting. Malicious QR codes can redirect to phishing sites, so look for HTTPS and verify the domain matches your expectation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are QR codes free to create?

Yes. Our QR code generator is completely free with no signup required. You can create unlimited QR codes for URLs, text, WiFi, and vCards. There are no watermarks, hidden fees, or trial limitations.

Do QR codes expire?

Static QR codes like the ones generated here never expire. The data is encoded directly in the code pattern itself — as long as the code is physically intact and scannable, it will work forever. Some commercial services offer dynamic QR codes that redirect through a server, and those may expire if the subscription lapses.

Can QR codes be customized with colors?

Yes. Our generator lets you choose any foreground color. For best scannability, use dark colors on white backgrounds and ensure strong contrast. Avoid light colors like yellow or pale blue on white.

What size should my QR code be?

For print, the minimum is 2×2 cm for close-range scanning. For posters viewed from 1-2 meters, use at least 5×5 cm. For billboards, scale based on the viewing distance. Digital QR codes on screens can be any size.

Can I use QR codes commercially?

Yes. QR codes are an open ISO/IEC 18004 standard with no licensing restrictions. You can use codes generated here for any commercial purpose including product packaging, advertising, and business cards.

Why won't my QR code scan?

Common causes: insufficient contrast, code too small for the distance, physical damage or smudging, poor lighting, or an old device. Try regenerating at a larger size with black-on-white coloring. Also ensure the quiet zone (white border) around the code is at least 4 modules wide.

Can a QR code contain a logo?

While it is technically possible to overlay a logo in the center of a QR code (the error correction allows up to 30% damage), it reduces reliability. For business-critical applications, we recommend keeping the code clean and placing the logo adjacent to it.

How much data can a QR code store?

Up to 7,089 numeric characters, 4,296 alphanumeric characters, or 2,953 bytes of binary data. However, more data means a denser, harder-to-scan code. For URLs, use a shortener to keep codes simple.
