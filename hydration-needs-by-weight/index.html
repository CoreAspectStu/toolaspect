How the Hydration Calculator Works

"8 glasses a day" was never a personalized number. Water needs scale with body size, climb with sweat, and stretch in heat. This calculator starts from weight and layers on the two biggest adjusters: exercise minutes and climate.

The formula

Baseline (oz) = body weight (lb) × 0.5, which is the same as roughly 30–35 mL per kg in metric. Add 0.4 oz per minute of exercise (12 oz per half hour). Multiply the total by 1.1 in hot or humid weather, or 1.05 in dry air or at altitude where you lose more to breathing and dry sweat. Pounds convert at 1 kg = 2.2046 lb, and 1 US fluid ounce = 29.57 mL.

How to use it

Enter your weight in either unit system, your typical daily exercise minutes, and the closest climate match. The result shows your target four ways, because nobody eyeballs liters at a water fountain. If you live somewhere with real seasons, run it twice: your January and July numbers can differ by 20 oz.

A worked example

A 180 lb runner training 45 minutes on a humid August morning: baseline is 180 × 0.5 = 90 oz, exercise adds 45 × 0.4 = 18 oz, for 108 oz before the weather. The 1.1 humid multiplier takes it to 118.8, call it 119 oz. That's 3.5 L, about 15 cups, or seven 16.9 oz bottles spread across the day. Compare the same runner on a rest day in November: 90 oz, full stop, nearly a full liter less.

One honest caveat: body weight formulas estimate, they don't measure. The field test that beats them is weighing yourself before and after a hard hour: each pound lost is about 16 oz of actual sweat.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much water should I drink a day based on my weight?

A common rule is half your body weight in ounces: a 150 lb person targets 75 oz (2.2 L) and a 200 lb person 100 oz (3.0 L). In metric that works out to roughly 30 to 35 mL per kg. Add 12 oz for every 30 minutes of exercise, and about 10% more in hot or humid weather.

Does coffee count toward daily water intake?

Yes. Caffeinated drinks are mildly diuretic, but the fluid far outweighs the effect, and regular coffee drinkers build tolerance to it. Coffee, tea, milk, juice, soup, and the roughly 20% of intake that comes from food all count toward your day. Water is just the cheapest way to get there.

Is 64 oz of water a day enough?

For a smaller body at rest, often yes. But the 8×8 rule ignores weight entirely: 64 oz is well above target for a 110 lb adult and short for a 220 lb one, whose baseline is 110 oz before exercise. Institute of Medicine adequate intakes for total water run 2.7 L (91 oz) for women and 3.7 L (125 oz) for men, including food.

Can you drink too much water?

Yes, though it takes real effort. Drinking several liters in a couple of hours dilutes blood sodium into hyponatremia, which is dangerous and occasionally fatal in endurance events. Pace intake to thirst plus reasonable scheduling, and if you're sweating for hours, replace electrolytes along with fluid, not gallons of plain water on top.

How much extra water do you need when exercising?

Roughly 12 to 16 oz per 30 minutes of sweaty activity, so an hour-long workout adds about a liter. Weigh yourself before and after a hard session: each pound lost is about 16 oz of sweat, which tells you your personal sweat rate far better than any formula.

How do I know if I'm drinking enough?

The cheapest check is urine color: pale straw means you're fine, dark amber means drink more. Thirst is a late signal that already implies mild deficit. Headaches, fatigue, and lightheadedness in the afternoon can be dehydration wearing a disguise, especially in hot offices or dry winter air.

This calculator estimates needs for healthy adults using published rules of thumb. Heart failure, kidney disease, and medications like diuretics come with real fluid limits. If any of those apply, follow your clinician's target instead.
