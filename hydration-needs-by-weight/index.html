Daily water target from your body weight, workouts, and climate

A common rule of thumb is half your body weight in ounces of water a day: 75 oz (2.2 L) at 150 lb, 90 oz (2.7 L) at 180 lb, 100 oz (3.0 L) at 200 lb. Add about 12 oz for every 30 minutes of exercise, and another 10% in hot or humid weather. It's an estimate, not an official standard: the Institute of Medicine's adequate intakes are set by sex, not weight, at 2.7 L for women and 3.7 L for men, counting all drinks plus food.

How the Hydration Calculator Works

"8 glasses a day" was never a personalized number. Water needs scale with body size, climb with sweat, and stretch in heat. This calculator starts from weight and layers on the two biggest adjusters: exercise minutes and climate.

The formula

Baseline (oz) = body weight (lb) × 0.5, which is the same as roughly 30–35 mL per kg in metric. Add 0.4 oz per minute of exercise (12 oz per half hour). Multiply the total by 1.1 in hot or humid weather, or 1.05 for dry air or high altitude. Those multipliers are the calculator's own allowances. Pounds convert at 1 kg = 2.2046 lb, and 1 US fluid ounce = 29.57 mL.

How to use it

Enter your weight in either unit system, your typical daily exercise minutes, and the closest climate match. The result shows your target four ways, because nobody eyeballs liters at a water fountain. If you live somewhere with real seasons, run it twice: at the default 180 lb and 30 minutes, the hot-weather result is 10 oz higher than the temperate one.

A worked example

A 180 lb runner training 45 minutes on a humid August morning: baseline is 180 × 0.5 = 90 oz, exercise adds 45 × 0.4 = 18 oz, for 108 oz before the weather. The 1.1 humid multiplier takes it to 118.8, call it 119 oz. That's 3.5 L, about 15 cups, or seven 16.9 oz bottles spread across the day. Compare the same runner on a rest day in November: 90 oz, full stop, nearly a full liter less.

One honest caveat: body weight formulas estimate, they don't measure. The field test that beats them is weighing yourself before and after a hard hour: each pound lost is about 15 fl oz of sweat.

How Much Do Exercise and Climate Add?

Exercise adds a flat 0.4 oz per minute, or 24 oz (0.71 L) an hour. Climate then multiplies the whole total, exercise included. Here's a 180 lb adult:

Because climate is a percentage, it adds more for bigger and more active people: hot weather adds 9 oz to that rest day and about 11 oz to the one-hour day.

These add-ons are the calculator's own allowances, not clinical figures. The American College of Sports Medicine's position stand on fluid replacement says sweat rates vary so much between people that it recommends a customized plan, based on weighing yourself before and after exercise. Its goal during exercise is to avoid losing more than 2% of body weight to water deficit, or 3.6 lb at 180 lb.

For comparison, NIOSH (the CDC's workplace safety institute) advises people working in heat to drink 1 cup every 15 to 20 minutes, or 24 to 32 oz an hour, and never more than 48 oz an hour.

Is Half Your Body Weight in Ounces an Official Guideline?

No. It's a rule of thumb. The US reference values, set by the Institute of Medicine (now the National Academies) in 2004, aren't built on body weight. They're adequate intakes based on median total water intake in US survey data, and they vary by sex and life stage. The European Food Safety Authority set lower figures in 2010 for moderate temperatures and activity.

The weight rule matches the US drinks figure for women (2.2 L, 74 oz) at 148 lb and for men (3.0 L, 101 oz) at 202 lb. Below those weights it gives less than the published number, and above them it gives more.

When Doesn't This Estimate Apply?

The calculator assumes a healthy adult with no fluid instructions from a doctor. Don't use its number if:

  • You've been given a fluid limit. MedlinePlus says people with heart failure may be told to change how much they drink. The National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases tells people on hemodialysis to track and limit liquids, including soup, ice cream and gelatin.
  • You take diuretics. "Water pills" increase urine output and sodium loss, and MedlinePlus lists them among the causes of low blood sodium. Ask your prescriber for a target.
  • You're doing a long endurance event. Overdrinking can dilute blood sodium (hyponatremia), which the Academies' report says can be life threatening. It notes cases in prolonged endurance events. The calculator accepts up to 600 minutes of exercise and keeps adding 24 oz an hour without knowing how much you sweat.
  • You're pregnant, breastfeeding, or estimating for a child. The rule has no adjustment for these.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much water should I drink a day based on my weight?

A common rule is half your body weight in ounces: a 150 lb person targets 75 oz (2.2 L) and a 200 lb person 100 oz (3.0 L). In metric that works out to roughly 30 to 35 mL per kg. Add 12 oz for every 30 minutes of exercise, and about 10% more in hot or humid weather.

Does coffee count toward daily water intake?

Yes. The Institute of Medicine's 2004 report found that caffeinated beverages appear to contribute to daily total water intake much as non-caffeinated ones do. Coffee, tea, milk, juice, soup, and the roughly 19% of intake that comes from food all count toward your day. Water is just the cheapest way to get there.

Is 64 oz of water a day enough?

For a smaller body at rest, often yes. But the 8×8 rule ignores weight entirely: 64 oz is well above target for a 110 lb adult and short for a 220 lb one, whose baseline is 110 oz before exercise. Institute of Medicine adequate intakes for total water run 2.7 L (91 oz) for women and 3.7 L (125 oz) for men, including food.

Can you drink too much water?

Yes, though it's rare in healthy people. Drinking far faster than your kidneys can clear it, at most 0.7 to 1.0 L an hour according to the Institute of Medicine, dilutes blood sodium into hyponatremia, which can be life threatening. It has happened in prolonged endurance events. NIOSH tells people working in heat to stay under 48 oz an hour.

How much extra water do you need when exercising?

This calculator adds 12 oz per 30 minutes, so an hour-long workout adds 24 oz (0.7 L). NIOSH's figure for working in heat is 24 to 32 oz an hour. Weigh yourself before and after a hard session: each pound lost is about 15 fl oz of sweat, which tells you your personal sweat rate far better than any formula.

How do I know if I'm drinking enough?

Check your urine color. MedlinePlus lists dark-colored urine as a sign of dehydration in adults, along with feeling very thirsty, a dry mouth, tiredness and dizziness. Day to day, the Institute of Medicine says thirst and normal drinking habits generally keep healthy people hydrated. Hard work in heat is the exception: NIOSH advises drinking before you feel thirsty.

Is half your body weight in ounces of water accurate?

It is a rule of thumb, not an official guideline. The US reference values (Institute of Medicine, 2004) are set by sex and life stage, not body weight: 3.7 L a day of total water for men and 2.7 L for women, with about 3.0 L and 2.2 L of that from drinks. The weight rule matches those drink figures at 202 lb for men and 148 lb for women. It gives less below those weights and more above them.

How much water should you drink when pregnant or breastfeeding?

The US adequate intake for total water is 3.0 L a day in pregnancy, about 2.3 L (10 cups) of it from drinks, and 3.8 L a day while breastfeeding, about 3.1 L (13 cups) from drinks. This calculator has no pregnancy or breastfeeding setting, so use those figures and your own clinician's advice over the weight-based number.

This calculator estimates needs for healthy adults using published rules of thumb. Heart failure, kidney disease, and medications like diuretics come with real fluid limits. If any of those apply, follow your clinician's target instead.
