Understanding Crypto Prices

Cryptocurrency prices are determined by supply and demand on exchanges worldwide. Unlike traditional currencies, crypto markets operate 24/7 — there are no trading hours or market closures. The price you see is a volume-weighted average across hundreds of exchanges, aggregated by data providers like CoinGecko.

Several factors influence crypto prices: market sentiment, regulatory news, institutional adoption, technological upgrades (like Bitcoin halving or Ethereum upgrades), macroeconomic conditions, and trading volume. Stablecoins like USDT maintain their peg through reserves of fiat currency and other assets.

Why Your Exchange Shows a Different Price

The rate here is a market price averaged across many exchanges. What you actually get when you sell includes two things this converter can't see: the spread, which is the gap between the buy and sell prices on that exchange, and the fee. Together they're usually quoted or buried as a percentage. Here's what they do to a $1,000 conversion:

Total spread + feeYou receive on a $1,000 saleLeft after buying and then selling
0.1%$999.00$998.00
0.5%$995.00$990.03
1%$990.00$980.10
2%$980.00$960.40

A 1% cost looks small until you trade in and out a few times. Simple buy and sell buttons on apps often cost more than the order-book trading screen on the same exchange, so compare the amount you'd actually receive, not the headline price. On top of that, moving coins between wallets costs a network fee, which is a fixed amount in the coin itself rather than a percentage.

Stablecoins have their own wrinkle. USDT usually trades within a few tenths of a cent of $1, but a converter shows the market price, so you may see 0.9992 instead of a round 1.00. That's normal. A move of several cents would be news.

Converting Crypto for Taxes

In the US, the IRS treats crypto as property. Selling it, swapping one coin for another, or spending it is a taxable event, and you report the gain or loss in US dollars at the fair market value when it happened. Gains on coins held a year or less are taxed as ordinary income; longer than a year, at long-term capital gains rates. Brokers now report sales on Form 1099-DA, with gross proceeds for transactions from January 1, 2025 and cost basis for assets acquired from January 1, 2026.

The catch for record-keeping is that you need the price at the moment of each transaction, not today's. This converter shows live rates only, so use it to check what something's worth now. For past trades, use your exchange's transaction history, which records the dollar value at the time, and keep it. If the numbers involve real money, a tax professional who knows crypto is worth the fee.
