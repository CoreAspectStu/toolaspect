🍫 Dog Chocolate Toxicity Calculator

Theobromine dose in mg/kg, mapped against veterinary severity thresholds

Dog ate chocolate, quick answer: toxicity comes down to theobromine per kilogram of body weight. Milk chocolate holds 44–58 mg per ounce, dark and semisweet 135–160, unsweetened baking 390–450. Under 20 mg/kg usually means mild stomach upset at worst; 20–40 risks restlessness and vomiting; 40–60 can bring tremors and a racing heart; above 60 mg/kg is potentially lethal. If you're unsure, call ASPCA Animal Poison Control at (888) 426-4435 or Pet Poison Helpline at (855) 764-7661 (consultation fees apply).

How the Chocolate Toxicity Calculator Works

Dogs metabolize theobromine, the stimulant in cocoa, far more slowly than humans do. It builds up, strains the heart, irritates the nervous system, and lingers for days. The math behind every chocolate-toxicity chart is the same: estimate how much theobromine went in, divide by the dog's body weight in kilograms, and read the dose against clinical thresholds.

The formula

Dose (mg/kg) = theobromine eaten (mg) ÷ dog weight (kg), where theobromine eaten = amount × the chocolate type's mg-per-ounce content. Since published theobromine content is a range, the calculator reports a dose range and uses the typical value to pick the severity band.

How to use it

Enter your dog's weight in pounds or kilograms, pick the closest chocolate type, and estimate the amount eaten. Wrapper math helps: a standard milk chocolate bar is about 1.55 oz, a bag of dark chocolate chips 10–12 oz, one baking square half an ounce. When the type is unknown, run it once as milk and once as dark to see both ends of the range.

A worked example

A 50-lb Labrador shares a 4-oz milk chocolate bar. Four ounces at 44–58 mg/oz delivers 176–232 mg of theobromine. At 22.7 kg, that's 7.8–10.2 mg/kg, below the 20 mg/kg threshold: expect, at worst, an evening of GI grumbling, and it would take nearly 8.9 oz of that same chocolate to reach the mild line.

Now the scary version. A 12-lb terrier eats 2 oz of dark chocolate. At 135–160 mg/oz that's 270–320 mg, and at 5.4 kg the dose is 49.6–58.8 mg/kg, squarely in the severe band where tremors and cardiac signs appear. And a 20-lb beagle swallowing 4 oz of baking chocolate takes on 172–198 mg/kg, near the estimated lethal range for theobromine, an emergency room trip, full stop.

One more honesty note: chocolate also contains caffeine, roughly a tenth of the theobromine by weight, which stacks its own stimulant effect on top. The calculator's numbers cover theobromine only, one more reason to let a vet make the final call.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much chocolate can kill a dog?

It depends on the chocolate and the dog. The theobromine LD50 in dogs is estimated around 100-200 mg per kg of body weight. For a 20-lb dog that's roughly 2-5 oz of unsweetened baking chocolate (390-450 mg/oz) but 16-41 oz of milk chocolate (44-58 mg/oz). Serious symptoms can begin far below lethal doses, around 40-60 mg/kg, so don't wait for danger math to start making calls.

My dog ate chocolate and seems fine. What now?

Watch closely and call your vet or a pet poison line anyway. Theobromine is slow to clear in dogs, and symptoms commonly take 6 to 12 hours to show, starting with vomiting, diarrhea, restlessness, and thirst. A dog that seems fine at hour two can be in trouble at hour eight. Have the wrapper and amount handy when you call.

Is white chocolate toxic to dogs?

Practically no. White chocolate carries only trace theobromine, about 0.25 mg per ounce, so a 10-lb dog would need to eat over 350 oz to approach the mild threshold. The real issues with white chocolate are its fat and sugar, which can trigger pancreatitis or GI upset in a serious binge.

How soon after eating chocolate do symptoms start?

Typically 6 to 12 hours, since dogs metabolize theobromine very slowly. Early signs include vomiting, diarrhea, increased thirst, restlessness, and pacing. Higher doses progress to a racing heart, muscle tremors, and seizures. Symptoms can linger for days because of the slow clearance, which is part of why vets take even moderate doses seriously.

Should I make my dog throw up after chocolate?

Not on your own initiative. Inducing vomiting is sometimes the right move, but the timing, method, and whether it's safe depend on the dog and the dose, and doing it wrong causes its own injuries. Call your vet or ASPCA Animal Poison Control at (888) 426-4435 first and follow their instructions; they may tell you to come in for activated charcoal or monitoring instead.
