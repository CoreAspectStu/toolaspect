Free Contractor Calculators for Every Trade

Estimating is where construction jobs are won or lost. Bid too high and the job goes to a competitor; bid too low and you donate your labor to the client. These 35 free contractor calculators exist to make material takeoffs fast, repeatable, and defensible. Each tool uses the same unit conventions your suppliers use — concrete in cubic yards with 80lb bag equivalents, roofing and siding in squares, asphalt by the ton at 145 lb/ft³, brick counts from bedded dimensions with mortar joints, aggregate base by the compacted ton at roughly 1.4 tons per yard — so the number you calculate is the number you order. Whether you are pricing a weekend fence, a full basement pour, or a production run of remodels, the takeoff follows the same discipline: measure the geometry, apply the trade's coverage or density constant, add a realistic waste factor, and price it at this week's material cost.

The collection now spans the whole job, start to finish. Sitework tools handle excavation with swell, base rock, and haul-off before anything is built. Concrete tools cover slabs, footings, sonotube piers, rebar grids, and CMU walls. Framing tools count studs, plates, sheathing, stairs, window rough openings, and full shed material lists. Envelope tools price roofing squares, siding, gutters, soffit and fascia, batt and blow-in insulation. Interior tools take off drywall, paint, flooring, tile, countertops, epoxy, and crown. Landscape tools finish with topsoil, sod, and mulch. A crew can estimate an entire project — dirt to lawn — without leaving this page.

Every calculator runs entirely in your browser: enter dimensions, get quantities instantly, and adjust waste factors, prices, and product sizes to match your market. Nothing is uploaded, saved, or paywalled.

Why Unit Conventions Matter

  • 1 square = 100 sq ft — the universal unit for roofing and siding orders
  • 1 cubic yard = 27 ft³ — concrete, gravel, and mulch are sold by the yard
  • Asphalt ≈ 145 lb/ft³ compacted — about 0.054 tons per sq yd per inch
  • Modular brick ≈ 7 per sq ft with a ⅜ in mortar joint (bedded 8 × 2⅝ in)
  • 3 bundles = 1 square for standard-weight shingles

Tips for Accurate Estimates

  • Measure twice from the plans, then verify one dimension on site — scale errors multiply everything
  • Use realistic waste factors: 5–10% for formed concrete, 10–15% for siding and roofing, 15–20% for diagonal tile
  • Price materials the week you bid — lumber, asphalt, and steel move with commodity markets
  • Keep the takeoff; a defensible number is your best protection in a change-order dispute
  • For anything structural, have an engineer review sizes and spacing — these tools estimate quantities, not structural adequacy

Remodel Cost Estimators vs Material Takeoffs

The two families of tools on this page answer different questions, and knowing which one you need saves time. Material takeoff calculators answer "how much do I order" — they return quantities like cubic yards, squares, and bags, and you apply your own pricing. Installed cost estimators answer "what will the homeowner pay" — they start from national installed ranges and adjust by scope, material grade, and project size, returning a low/typical/high spread you can use in a first conversation with a client before anyone measures anything. A production contractor bidding work typically runs the takeoff tools daily and the cost estimators when a homeowner calls cold. A homeowner planning a project usually starts with the cost estimators to size the budget, then uses the takeoff tools once the scope is set and they want to check a bid or buy materials themselves.

The cost estimators cover the projects homeowners price most: window replacement by window type and glass package, kitchen remodels at facelift, midrange, and upscale tiers, bathrooms from refresh to gut, fencing across nine materials with gates and terrain factors, decks in wood versus composite with a twenty-year cost comparison, HVAC replacement sized from home square footage, water heaters compared across tank, tankless, and heat-pump types with payback, and solar systems sized from an electric bill. Each one shows its assumptions so you can defend or adjust the numbers rather than taking them on faith.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate how much concrete I need?

Multiply length × width × thickness in feet to get cubic feet, then divide by 27 for cubic yards. Add 5–10% for waste on formed pours. The concrete calculator handles slabs, footings, and round columns, converts to 80lb bags for small jobs, and prices the pour when you enter your local ready-mix rate.

How many squares of shingles do I need?

One square covers 100 square feet of roof. Because roofs are pitched, actual roof area is larger than the footprint — the roofing calculator applies the pitch multiplier for you, then returns squares, bundles (three per square for standard-weight shingles), underlayment rolls, and ridge cap length.

What is a swell factor in excavation?

Soil occupies more volume loose than it did in the ground. The swell factor — commonly around 25% for ordinary earth — converts bank cubic yards to loose cubic yards. That loose volume is what determines your truck count and haul-off cost, which the excavation calculator works out from your pit dimensions and truck capacity.

How many studs do I need for a wall?

At 16 inches on center, a wall needs roughly one stud per 16 inches of plate length plus extras for corners, openings, and intersections, along with the plates and sheathing. The wall framing calculator takes wall length, height, and spacing (16 or 24 inch OC) and returns the full lumber list with cost.

How is asphalt calculated by the ton?

Compacted hot-mix weighs about 145 pounds per cubic foot. Multiply pavement area by thickness to get volume, apply that density to get tons, then divide by your truck's capacity for load count. The asphalt calculator does this directly from area and thickness.
